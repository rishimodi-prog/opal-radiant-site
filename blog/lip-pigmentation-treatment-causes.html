Pigmentation

Lip Pigmentation Treatment: What Causes Dark Lips and What It Can Realistically Do

By Dr. Punit Sakhare, MD Dermatologist · 2026-08-29 · 12 min read

Dark lips are among the most searched skin concerns in India and among the least studied. The entire published trial literature on lip pigmentation treatment comes to three prospective studies — one randomised controlled trial in 30 people, one uncontrolled series of 20 and one uncontrolled series of 85 — with only one control group between them, and not a single Indian participant. That is the honest starting point, and it should shape what you expect before you book anything.

This article covers what actually darkens the vermillion of the lip, how the causes are told apart, what the controlled evidence shows a laser or a topical can and cannot achieve, why Fitzpatrick IV–VI skin changes the risk arithmetic, and which lip-lightening claims have no study behind them at all. It is not medical advice. A dark lip has a real differential diagnosis that includes conditions needing medical management, and only a qualified dermatologist examining you in person can work through it.

What Dark Lips Actually Are

Lip pigmentation is melanin sitting within and beneath the vermillion — the visible red-brown border of the lip — and often in the adjacent mucosa as well. A 2012 review by Vachiramon and McMichael in the International Journal of Dermatology set out the diagnostic framework still used today: lip hyperpigmentation is sorted by extent (a single macule versus diffuse darkening), age of onset, and whether there is systemic involvement alongside it.

That framework produces seven categories: physiologic pigmentation; genodermatoses such as Peutz-Jeghers and Laugier-Hunziker syndromes; inflammatory disease; endocrine disorders including Addison's disease; drugs and chemicals; benign and malignant neoplasms; and non-melanotic causes, where the colour comes from something other than melanin. Two of those seven are systemic diseases and one is cancer — which alone explains why a lip is examined before it is treated.

The review's authors state their own limitation plainly: some of the data underneath these associations comes from case reports and small series that may not reflect a true association. The classification is sound; the evidence beneath most individual causes is thin. Colour interpretation is subjective too — how dark a lip looks depends both on how much pigment is present and how deep it sits, and deeper pigment responds far less readily to anything applied to the surface.

The Common Causes of Dark Lips, Ranked by How Often They Turn Up

The best frequency data comes from Hassona and colleagues, who examined 1,275 consecutive dental patients at the University of Jordan Hospital over one year, biopsying focal lesions of uncertain diagnosis. A total of 386 patients (30.2%) had oral pigmentation, distributed as follows:

  • Racial or physiological pigmentation — 39.9%
  • Smoker's melanosis — 32.9%
  • Amalgam tattoo (dental restoration metal) — 18.9%
  • Focal melanotic macules — 5.7%
  • Post-inflammatory pigmentation — 1.6%
  • Medication or systemic disease — 0.52%
  • Heavy metal deposits — 0.26%
  • Oral naevus — 0.26%

The single most useful number: physiological pigmentation and smoking together account for roughly three-quarters of pigmented oral lesions. If your lips have been the same shade since adolescence and your parents' lips match, the likeliest explanation is constitutional pigmentation — a normal variant, not a disease, and not something a treatment course is designed to correct.

Three caveats belong with those figures. It was a Jordanian dental-clinic population, not an Indian dermatology one. Gingivae and buccal mucosa were the commonest sites reported, and the vermillion lip was not reported separately. And it was a prevalence survey with no control group. Use the ranking as a guide; do not treat the percentages as Indian numbers.

Two further causes are routinely blamed in clinic and have no prevalence data at all: allergic contact cheilitis from lipsticks, toothpastes and betel or tobacco products, and chronic lip-licking or biting causing post-inflammatory pigmentation. Both are clinical consensus rather than measured findings — no prevalence study of contact cheilitis causing lip hyperpigmentation in an Indian population was located. Where a contact cause is suspected, patch testing is the investigation, not a laser.

The Lip Lesions That Need a Diagnosis Before Any Cosmetic Treatment

A dark lip is usually benign. Occasionally it is not, and the consequences of getting that wrong are not cosmetic. Uribe and colleagues, across two Australian melanoma centres, examined 10 normal lips, 16 patients with melanotic macules and 5 patients carrying 6 lip melanomas, comparing dermoscopy against reflectance confocal microscopy.

The findings are sobering in both directions. A confocal microscopy algorithm achieved 100% sensitivity and 88% specificity for lip melanoma. By dermoscopy alone, benign melanotic macules were correctly classified in only 13 of 16 cases (81%), and melanomas were flagged as equivocal or malignant in 5 of 6 (83%) — one lip melanoma in six was not flagged. The melanoma sample is small (6 lesions) and the study was retrospective at specialist referral centres, so these are not population accuracy figures. The direction is what matters: dermoscopic assessment of a solitary dark lip patch errs both ways.

The rule that follows is not negotiable. Diffuse, symmetrical, long-standing darkening of both lips is a different situation from a single patch that is new, growing, changing colour, bleeding or asymmetric. The second is assessed medically — dermoscopy, and biopsy where indicated — before appearance is discussed at all. Sudden darkening of lips and oral mucosa together, particularly alongside fatigue or low blood pressure, also warrants medical evaluation, because endocrine causes sit in the differential.

What Lip Pigmentation Treatment Can Realistically Achieve

Lip pigmentation treatment has exactly one controlled trial behind it. Limpjaroenviriyakul and colleagues ran a prospective, randomised, evaluator-blinded trial in 30 Thai patients (15 per arm), comparing five sessions of low-fluence Q-switched Nd:YAG 1064 nm against a single session of Q-switched Nd:YAG 532 nm. All 30 completed. The 532 nm group achieved greater melanin-index reduction and better photographic change, both at p < 0.001 — but the adverse effects (scaling, hypopigmentation, bleb formation, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, labial oedema) occurred only in that group. The authors open their own paper by stating that clinical data from controlled comparative studies is insufficient to support the efficacy of laser treatments for hyperpigmented lips. The comparison was also unbalanced: five sessions against one.

The second study is Ng and colleagues' single-arm evaluation of a 755 nm picosecond laser in 20 Thai patients: five biweekly treatments, follow-up at 1, 3 and 6 months. At 6 months, 52.6% showed moderate clinical improvement and 70% reported moderate-to-marked improvement themselves. The melanin index fell significantly after the fourth treatment (p = .048) and at 1 month (p = .026) — two of several timepoints, not throughout. One patient had transient lip oedema. With no control group, none of that can be separated from what would have happened anyway.

The third is Agha and colleagues' uncontrolled case series of 85 patients treated with an ablative Er,Cr:YSGG 2780 nm laser: 74.1% complete improvement, 84.7% satisfaction, mild adverse effects in 82.4%. Outcomes were self-reported, there was no control arm and no blinding, relapse was called "low" but never quantified, and nothing was broken down by skin type — which matters for an ablative procedure on Fitzpatrick IV–VI lips.

What can be said confidently is this. A course of conservative laser sessions may reduce the measured melanin index of the lip and may produce a visible, moderate evening of tone in around half of patients over about six months. No study has shown clearance, none has followed patients long enough to quantify relapse, and none has enrolled Indian skin.

Two comparisons matter. Within the only randomised trial, the more aggressive wavelength bought greater pigment reduction and every single adverse event — a trade-off, not an upgrade. And the largest improvement figure in this literature (74.1%) comes from its weakest design: effect size and evidence quality run in opposite directions. A supervised, conservative course — such as a properly indicated chemical peel for perioral pigmentation — is judged on the same standard.

Why Fitzpatrick IV–VI Skin Changes the Risk Arithmetic

Most Indian patients are Fitzpatrick IV to VI, and none of the three lip studies enrolled them. The wider pigmentation literature shows deeper skin carries a different risk profile from the same procedure. In a retrospective analysis of 473 superficial chemical peels in Fitzpatrick III–VI skin, Vemula and colleagues recorded a complication rate of 3.8% — crusting 2.3%, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation and erythema 1.9% each — all resolving within 8 months. But Fitzpatrick VI carried an odds ratio of 5.14 (95% CI 1.21–21.8) for any adverse event, and side effects were significantly less common in winter. A low absolute rate with a real gradient by skin type — from a procedure gentler than an ablative lip laser.

The second problem is that post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, the commonest complication of any pigment procedure in deeper skin, is itself poorly treatable. A 2024 PRISMA systematic review by Mar and colleagues pooled 48 studies covering 1,356 individuals with skin of colour. Topical retinoids (22% of studies) gave partial improvement in 85%; laser (17%) gave partial improvement in 66% and was the only modality producing complete resolution in a subgroup, at 26% — while the same review recorded cases of PIH worsened by laser. The authors' conclusion is blunt: a lack of robust efficacy across all treatment modalities.

The logic for the lip follows. If a treatment triggers post-inflammatory pigmentation on Fitzpatrick V skin, you have exchanged a benign constitutional shade for an iatrogenic mark that no modality reliably clears. That is why conservative settings and wider intervals are the standard approach to pigmentation in Indian skin, and why an ablative technique on the lip deserves scepticism until someone publishes skin-type-stratified safety data.

What the Evidence Does Not Support

Each claim below appears routinely in lip-lightening marketing, followed by the evidence that undermines it.

"Results are permanent." No lip study has quantified long-term relapse. Ng and colleagues followed patients to 6 months and stopped; Agha and colleagues called relapse "low level" without a number; the randomised trial reported no long-term follow-up at all. There is no published basis for a permanence claim about the lip. These treatments do not remove pigment-producing melanocytes — they quieten them, and the original driver usually persists.

"A stronger setting clears it faster." In the only randomised lip trial, the more energetic 532 nm wavelength did produce greater melanin-index reduction — and produced every adverse event in the study, including post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation and hypopigmentation.

"Ablative lip peeling is a proven treatment." The 74.1% complete-improvement figure comes from an uncontrolled, unblinded series of 85 patients with self-reported outcomes, mild adverse effects in 82.4%, no quantified relapse and no breakdown by Fitzpatrick type. That is a signal, not trial-grade evidence.

"Skin-lightening or steroid creams are a safe option for lips." A prospective multicentre study across 12 Indian centres by Saraswat and colleagues screened 2,926 patients with facial dermatoses and found 433 (14.8%) using topical corticosteroids — most commonly as a lightening cream (126 patients, 29%). Adverse effects occurred in 392 of 433, or 90.5%. Hydroquinone has been prescription-only Schedule H in India since 1 April 2019. Neither belongs on a lip outside medical supervision.

"The before-and-after photos prove it works." In the picosecond study, 70% of patients reported moderate-to-marked improvement while the objective melanin index reached significance at only two of several timepoints. Reported satisfaction and measured pigment change do not track each other reliably, and without a control arm neither can be attributed to the treatment.

"A lip-lightening facial, scrub or de-tan clears the pigment." No controlled trial with lip pigment as a defined endpoint was located for any facial, exfoliation or de-tan protocol. The claim has no study behind it in either direction — which means nobody selling it knows whether it does anything.

The Part With the Best Support: Remove the Cause, Then Protect

The strongest evidence here is not about devices at all. Roughly a third of pigmented oral lesions in the Hassona series — 32.9% — were smoker's melanosis, a cause with a modifiable driver. Smoking cessation addresses the mechanism rather than the appearance, and no device does that. Where a contact allergen from a lipstick, toothpaste or betel or tobacco product is suspected, patch testing identifies it and removal stops the inflammation generating the pigment. Treating pigment while its source continues is the commonest reason a course underperforms.

Photoprotection is the second pillar, and it matters more in deeper skin for a specific reason. Sklar and colleagues' review of ultraviolet, visible and infrared effects documents three distinct pigmentary responses: immediate pigment darkening, driven by UVA, which redistributes existing melanin rather than making new melanin; persistent pigment darkening; and delayed tanning, genuine new melanogenesis following UVB and UVA. Critically, visible light induces erythema and a tanning response in dark skin but not in fair skin — so a UV-only sunscreen leaves the visible-light driver untouched, which is the argument for tinted, iron-oxide-containing formulations. That review is physiology, not trial data, and no lip-specific photoprotection trial was located; a tinted SPF lip balm is a low-risk measure with a coherent mechanism rather than a proven one. Our guide to sunscreen for Indian skin covers the same logic for the face.

The habits worth reviewing are prosaic: chronic lip-licking and biting, which drive post-inflammatory pigmentation through repeated low-grade injury; long-worn matte lipsticks and stains that may sensitise; and chapping, which darkens the look of a lip through surface roughness alone.

How to Approach a Course Sensibly, and When to See a Dermatologist

An assessment should establish the cause before it discusses treatment: how long the darkening has been present, whether onset was gradual or sudden, family pattern, smoking and betel use, cosmetics and toothpastes, medications and systemic symptoms. It means examining the oral mucosa and gingivae as well as the vermillion, since those were the commonest sites in the Hassona series, and dermoscopy of any solitary or asymmetric patch, with biopsy where the picture is uncertain.

Where a conservative device course is appropriate, the published protocols ran to five sessions: five at biweekly intervals in the picosecond study, five in the low-fluence Nd:YAG arm of the randomised trial. Response was gradual, the picosecond melanin index reaching significance only after the fourth treatment. Timelines vary, and any clinic quoting a guaranteed result on this evidence base is going beyond what has been published. Fees vary by area and session count, and prices move; our pricing page lists current ranges.

See a doctor promptly, rather than booking a cosmetic appointment, if a lip patch is new, solitary, growing, changing colour, ulcerated or bleeding; if lips and mouth have darkened together over weeks to months; if darkening began after a new medication; or if pigmented lip spots run in the family alongside gastrointestinal symptoms, which raises the question of a genodermatosis. For a dermatologist-led assessment first, you can book a consultation at any of our four Mumbai branches.

The overall picture is one this blog would rather state than dress up: three studies, one control group, 135 patients in total, no Indian data. Modest, partial evening of tone over several months is a defensible expectation. Clearance, permanence and a guaranteed shade are not.
