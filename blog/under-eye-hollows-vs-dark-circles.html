Under Eye Hollows Treatment vs Dark Circles: Pigment, Shadow or Volume?

By Dr. Punit Sakhare, MD Dermatologist · 2026-10-05 · 12 min read

Three different problems get sold under one name. One is pigment in the skin around the eye. One is blood vessels showing through thin lid skin. One is a hollow, the groove between lower lid and cheek, which casts a shadow that photographs as a dark line. A fourth, post-inflammatory pigmentation after rubbing or eczema, is common in India. This is why someone searching for under eye hollows treatment and someone searching for a brightening eye cream arrive at advice that contradicts itself. They do not have the same condition.

The distinction decides everything that follows. In 200 Asian patients with periorbital hyperpigmentation examined by three dermatologists, the causes divided into vascular 41.8%, constitutional pigmentary 38.6%, post-inflammatory 12% and shadow effects 11.4%. A cream aimed at melanin does nothing to a shadow cast by a bony groove; a filler placed in a hollow does nothing to dermal pigment. What follows is what has been measured for each type, and at what quality of evidence.

Dark circles are four different problems sharing one name

The classification treatment has to follow comes from Ranu and colleagues, who screened 1,000 consecutive patients at the National Skin Centre in Singapore and examined the 200 with periorbital hyperpigmentation. Cause was assigned by three dermatologists, extent measured by mexameter. The split was vascular 41.8%, constitutional (pigmentary) 38.6%, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation 12% and shadow effects 11.4%. Those figures exceed 100% because mixed types are common, which is itself the lesson. One finding matters more in Mumbai than anything else in the paper: the vascular type predominated in Chinese patients, whereas the constitutional type was most common in Indians and Malays.

A systematic review of 39 studies by Michelle and colleagues in Dermatologic Surgery in 2021 uses four labels: pigmentary, structural, vascular and mixed. Fillers and fat grafting are described as most effective for volume-loss circles; blepharoplasty where excessive skin laxity is the cause; creams and chemical peels for pigment-based circles; lasers as mildly to moderately beneficial for vascular and pigmentary types. The review then adds its own warning, verbatim: "Given the scarcity of high-quality evidence supporting these results, recommendations should be interpreted selectively."

How to tell an under-eye hollow from a stain

Three checks separate a hollow from a stain. The first is light. A shadow depends on the angle between the light source and the groove, so it moves when the light moves; pigment does not. Photograph the same face under overhead light and again under flat frontal light; if the darkness largely disappears in the second image, a structural hollow is doing most of the work. The second is the eyelid stretch test, in which the lower lid skin is gently stretched to see whether the colour persists. The third is a Wood's lamp, which separates epidermal from deeper dermal pigment.

That last test produces the most useful number in Indian dark-circle research. Sheth and colleagues examined 200 consecutive Indian outpatients with Wood's lamp, eyelid stretch test and laboratory investigations. Constitutional periorbital hyperpigmentation accounted for 51.5% and post-inflammatory for 22.5%. Most striking, Wood's lamp showed the pigment was dermal in 60.5% of patients, and dermal pigment sits below the layer any cream can reach.

Malakar and colleagues examined 100 consecutive patients with Wood's lamp and took 2 mm punch biopsies in 17. In 92 of the 100, periorbital melanosis was an extension of the pigmentary demarcation line of the face: a constitutional pigment boundary, not acquired damage from tiredness or screens.

Under eye hollows treatment: what the published evidence supports

An under-eye hollow is an anatomical deficit, and that is why under eye hollows treatment sits in a different category from pigment treatment. The tear trough is a bony and ligamentous depression; the infraorbital hollow is a volume deficit between orbital rim and cheek. No topical study in this literature measured a change in orbital volume, and none plausibly could. The 2021 systematic review states the alternative plainly: fillers and fat grafting for volume loss, blepharoplasty where lid laxity is the driver.

The realistic volumes are small. Viana and colleagues treated tear-trough deformity with hyaluronic acid gel in a 10-patient pilot with no control group, placing filler pre-periosteally. Mean volume per side was 0.61 mL (SD 0.25) right and 0.65 mL (SD 0.26) left, and the effect lasted up to 12 months, with bruising, erythema, swelling and injection-site pain reported. Two-thirds of a millilitre a side is the scale of the intervention; ten uncontrolled patients is the scale of the evidence.

The risk belongs in the same breath. Hufschmidt and colleagues dissected the infraorbital artery in 18 fresh cadavers. It anastomoses with the supratrochlear, dorsal nasal and angular arteries, giving a documented route by which injected material can travel retrogradely towards the eye. They identified two danger zones, injection placed too superficially in the midcheek and injection placed in a periosteal layer during tear-trough correction, and concluded verbatim that "injections to the periosteum layer in tear-trough correction and above the periosteum on the zygomatic arch is not advised," which partly contradicts the older teaching that deep-on-bone is safest. In a 60-patient randomised comparison by Liu and colleagues, blue-grey Tyndall discolouration occurred in three patients given hyaluronic acid alone, significantly more than in the other arms.

Filler correction of a hollow may improve the appearance of the under-eye area, but it is a procedure with a vascular risk profile, not a facial. Book a consultation for an assessment of your own anatomy.

If the cause is pigment: what creams and peels actually measured

The most informative trial for Indian pigmentary dark circles is also the most sobering. Ranjan, Sarkar and colleagues at Maulana Azad Medical College randomised 50 Indian patients, 25 to 4% hydroquinone and 25 to 30% salicylic acid, over 12 weeks. Most patients in both groups showed mild improvement, defined as 10-30%, with no significant difference between treatments. Their conclusion is the sentence to remember: periorbital hyperpigmentation is "less responsive to standard treatments due to its multifactorial etiology and deposition of melanin in both dermis and epidermis." There was no placebo arm, so part of even that 10-30% may be the sun avoidance a trial imposes. Hydroquinone is prescription-only in India.

Peels show the same profile: consistent modest reports, almost no controlled data. Vavouli and colleagues treated 30 patients of skin types II to IV with trichloroacetic acid 3.75% plus lactic acid 15% over four weekly sessions, unblinded and uncontrolled, reporting 93.3% physician-rated fair-to-excellent results lasting 4 to 6 months where sun protection was maintained; side effects included erythema, oedema, frosting and telangiectasias. Thirty patients without a control arm cannot say how much of that was treatment.

For Indian skin the safety data on superficial peels is better quantified than the efficacy data. Vemula and colleagues reviewed 473 superficial peels in Fitzpatrick III to VI skin: overall complication rate 3.8%, but Fitzpatrick VI carried an odds ratio of 5.14 (95% CI 1.21-21.8) for any adverse event. Crusting was commonest at 2.3%, with post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation and erythema tied at 1.9%; all resolved within 8 months, and side effects were significantly less common in winter. That is why peeling thin periorbital skin in deeply pigmented patients is a careful decision, and why facial pigmentation is managed on the same principles.

Lasers, carboxytherapy and needling: a thin and contradictory literature

Energy devices for periorbital pigmentation are offered far more widely than the evidence justifies. Montolio-Marzo and colleagues screened 208 papers and included 14 in a 2024 review of light devices, finding the best results with intense pulsed light and ruby laser combined with depigmenting agents. Their verdict on the rest, verbatim: "the Nd:YAG, alexandrite and diode lasers were the ones giving the worst outcome regarding pigmentation treatment." Q-switched Nd:YAG is the most commonly advertised dark-circle laser in India, and that review placed it in the worst-performing group. A separate 2021 review pooled 10 trials published between 1990 and 2018: immediate response was excellent in 35.9% and good in 45.3%, but by 4 to 6 months it had shifted worse, fair rising to 25% and good falling to 26.27%. Ten trials in 28 years is the entire world laser literature for this condition.

Carboxytherapy, the injection of medical carbon dioxide into periorbital skin, shows how unstable a thin evidence base is. Nilforoushzadeh and colleagues randomised 28 patients and found carboxytherapy significantly more effective than fractional Q-switched Nd:YAG, with fewer side effects. Two years later a 30-patient split-face randomised trial found fractional CO2 laser outperformed carboxytherapy on patient satisfaction. Carboxytherapy beat Nd:YAG; CO2 beat carboxytherapy; both had roughly 30 patients, and no one has run the trial that settles it.

Two numbers set honest expectations. In a 31-woman split-face evaluator-blinded trial, carboxytherapy beat microneedling with topical glutathione during treatment (P = 0.001) and at follow-up (P = 0.006), yet in the winning arm marked satisfaction was only 3.2%. And in a 78-patient imaging study, 25 of 78 participants (32%) withdrew during the 7-week course because of discomfort, with the authors stating that "due to the absence of a control group, the results should be interpreted with caution."

Can energy-based tightening fill a hollow?

Focused ultrasound is frequently proposed for the under-eye area, so it is worth stating what has been measured there. Pak and colleagues treated seven patients with 1.5 mm and 3.0 mm probes, with no control group, and assessed them by orbital CT. The change in distance from the orbital-rim baseline to the most protruding point of the orbital septum was 0.51 plus or minus 0.23 mm right and 0.54 plus or minus 0.17 mm left, both P less than 0.001. The paper is titled a pivotal clinical trial, but pivotal there is not a regulatory designation: it is an uncontrolled series of seven people, and the measured effect is half a millimetre.

The other directly periorbital study points the same way. Suh and colleagues treated 10 Korean women with the 1.5 mm transducer alone in a single uncontrolled session. Mean satisfaction was 3.2 on a five-point scale, and objective improvement was highest in fine crow's-feet wrinkles and lowest in deep infraorbital wrinkles: the lines closest to the hollow responded worst. An independent randomised trial comparing 1550-nm fractional laser with focused ultrasound around the eye does exist, published in 2024 in Fitzpatrick I to III subjects only, but it appeared as a research letter with no indexed abstract, so we will not tell you who won it.

Regulatory scope is often blurred. Under US 510(k) clearance K243035 of 24 February 2025, the Ulthera system's cleared indications are lifting the eyebrow, lifting lax submental and neck tissue, improving lines of the decollete, and improving the appearance of skin laxity on the abdomen and arms. Infraorbital hollowing is not on that list; a 510(k) is a clearance, not an approval, and not an Indian authorisation. Where focused ultrasound has a role around the eye, it is for fine surface lines and mild laxity, on 17 uncontrolled patients.

What the evidence does not support

Several routine claims about dark circles and under-eye hollows are unsupported or actively contradicted by the published literature.

  • "This eye cream is clinically proven for dark circles and puffiness." The trial most often cited for multicorrective eye creams enrolled 40 women aged 35 to 60, of whom 37 completed 12 weeks. It was open-label, with no control and no placebo, and three of its five authors are employed by the manufacturer of the tested cream. Without a comparator arm its results cannot be separated from regression to the mean or seasonal change.
  • "A cream will fix your hollows." No topical adds volume, and no study measured a cream changing orbital contour.
  • "Q-switched Nd:YAG is the treatment for dark circles." The 2024 review of 208 papers placed Nd:YAG, alexandrite and diode lasers in the worst-performing group, and a randomised comparison found carboxytherapy more effective with fewer side effects.
  • "Tear trough filler is completely safe." Cadaveric anatomy identifies the periosteal plane in tear-trough correction as a danger zone for retrograde embolism, and the dissecting authors advise against periosteal injection there. Tyndall discolouration occurred in three patients in the hyaluronic-acid arm of a 60-patient trial.
  • "Carboxytherapy is gentle and comfortable." Thirty-two percent of participants in the largest imaging-based carboxytherapy study withdrew mid-course.
  • "Dark circles mean you are anaemic, insulin resistant or thyroid deficient." These are cross-sectional associations, not causes. An Indian study of 100 patients against 100 matched controls found leptin, fasting insulin and HOMA-IR significantly higher and adiponectin lower, still significant after adjusting for BMI, blood pressure, LDL and HDL. A genuine controlled association, and still not causation. A separate 50-patient series found anaemia in 10%, low vitamin B12 in 12% and no deranged thyroid function.

One further absence is worth naming: nobody can promise a number of sessions or a duration of result, because the durability data does not exist at that resolution.

The dark circles around Indian eyes that are not cosmetic at all

Before any dark circle is treated as cosmetic, it is worth knowing what else presents in the same place. Besra and colleagues catalogued 250 consecutive periorbital presentations at JIPMER, south India. Benign and malignant skin tumours were the commonest category at 25.2%, ahead of pigmentary disorders at 20.4%, eczema at 17.6%, infections at 13.2% and naevoid conditions at 10.4%. A quarter of everything appearing around the eye in that series was a tumour, so a new, asymmetrical or changing patch deserves examination rather than a treatment package.

A second entity is easily mistaken for ordinary pigmentation in young adults. Razmi and colleagues at PGIMER Chandigarh described periorbital acquired dermal macular hyperpigmentation in 19 of 177 patients with that condition. It had a significantly younger onset, 23.26 against 36.16 years, P less than 0.001, and dermoscopy showed speckled blue-grey dots with a characteristic outer-corner crease sign. It sits on the lichen planus pigmentosus spectrum: a different disease with a different course.

Two contributors in the Indian data are free to address and often overlooked. In the 200-patient Sheth series, habits included inadequate sleep in 40%, frequent cosmetic use in 36.5%, frequent eye rubbing in 32.5% and uncorrected refractive error in 12%. Chronic eye rubbing, from allergy or from squinting through an outdated spectacle prescription, drives post-inflammatory pigmentation, which accounted for 22.5% of that cohort. Stopping it before spending on devices is the highest-yield step available.

A realistic plan, and what to ask before you start

The clearest articulation of cause-matched treatment comes from Kounidas and colleagues, who reported 34 patients treated in the UK and India in three groups: fillers, lasers, and both combined. There was no randomisation, no blinding and no control group; all three improved with no significant difference between them, and 82% rated their own improvement excellent, which is what uncontrolled self-rating produces. Take it for its logic, not its effect sizes, and the logic is a decision map: hollow eyes point to fillers; loose, wrinkled lid skin to CO2 laser; tear troughs with hyperpigmentation to fillers plus topical agents; visible veins to fillers plus long-pulsed Nd:YAG.

For a consultation, that becomes a short list of questions.

  • Which of the four causes, or which combination, do I have, and on what examination is that based?
  • Was a Wood's lamp used, and was the pigment epidermal or dermal?
  • Will there be standardised baseline photographs, in fixed lighting and at a fixed angle?
  • What happens if there is no measurable change after the agreed course?
  • What are the risks for my skin type, in writing?

The honest summary is that periorbital hyperpigmentation and infraorbital hollowing are treatable to a modest, cause-dependent degree; that structural hollows respond to volume restoration rather than pigment-directed treatment; and that the largest systematic review in the field ends by asking for the randomised trials that would let anyone be more definite. Improvement in appearance is a reasonable goal. Elimination is not one the literature supports.

This article is for information only and is not medical advice. Dark circles and under-eye hollows have several distinct causes, some medical rather than cosmetic. Please consult a qualified dermatologist for assessment and an individualised plan before starting any treatment.
