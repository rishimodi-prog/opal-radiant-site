Back Acne Treatment: Why the Back and Body Are Treated Differently

By Dr. Punit Sakhare, MD Dermatologist · 2026-09-11 · 12 min read

Back acne treatment is not facial acne treatment applied lower down. The trunk carries larger, deeper pilosebaceous units across a surface area many times that of the face, it scars differently, and almost nobody can reach the middle of their own back twice a day for six months. Those facts change what is practical, affordable and likely to work.

The more uncomfortable point is how thin the evidence is. A structured search of the published literature for this article found no randomised controlled trial comparing facial with truncal treatment response for any acne drug, and no study at all of acne scar procedures on the back or chest. The 2024 American Academy of Dermatology acne guideline, built on 18 evidence-based recommendations and 5 good practice statements, does not separate truncal from facial disease. Nearly everything a clinic tells you about back and chest acne is extrapolated from trials run on faces.

That does not mean nothing works. It means the confidence attached to the advice should be lower than the advice usually sounds. What follows is what the trials measured, and where they stop.

How common is truncal acne, and how soft the numbers really are

Truncal acne — acne on the back, chest and shoulders — is usually described as affecting about half of everyone with facial acne. That figure is weaker than it sounds. It traces to a 2022 review by Tan and colleagues in the Journal of Clinical and Aesthetic Dermatology, which paired a narrative literature review with an online survey of 2,000 US respondents aged 14 to 29 who self-reported active acne. There was no clinical examination and no control group.

The reported figures: truncal acne alone in under 1% to 14% of people depending on the population, and roughly 30% to 60% of those with facial acne also reporting truncal involvement — 49% of respondents aged 14 to 20 and 54% of those aged 21 to 29, with a similar incidence in males and females. Among those calling their acne clear or mild, 46% reported truncal involvement; among those calling it moderate or severe, 60%.

One disclosure belongs with those numbers: a co-author is affiliated with Galderma Laboratories, which makes a topical retinoid marketed for truncal acne, and several others are industry consultants. The authors themselves note that "online surveys have inherent limitations, such as self-reporting and potential confounders." The prevalence range is a working estimate, not a measured epidemiological fact.

What actually makes back and body acne different

Six differences between truncal and facial acne are stated routinely in clinics. Graded against the literature, five of the six are convention rather than trial findings.

  • Larger surface area. Self-evident, and the difference with the biggest practical consequence: a tube that lasts a month on a face lasts days on a back and chest.
  • Thicker skin, larger and deeper pilosebaceous units. Anatomical fact, drawn from anatomy rather than acne trials. No study has shown it changes drug response.
  • Poorer adherence, because the back is hard to reach. Clinically obvious, and this search found no randomised trial of long-handled applicators or any other aid.
  • More hypertrophic and keloid scarring on the trunk. Widely taught and plausible. No acne-specific comparative incidence study was located.
  • Under-diagnosis, because patients undress less in consultation. Asserted in Tan 2022, on self-reported survey data.
  • Facial plus truncal disease means earlier onset and more severe acne. The one item with study data behind it — the same 2,000-person self-reported survey.

The direction of each claim is probably right. The magnitude of none has been quantified in a controlled study of acne — and that gap is where most overselling happens.

Back acne treatment: what the guidelines actually recommend

The 2024 American Academy of Dermatology guideline (Reynolds and colleagues) is the current reference point for back acne treatment, and it makes exactly four strong recommendations for topical and oral therapy: benzoyl peroxide, topical retinoids, topical antibiotics, and oral doxycycline. Oral isotretinoin carries a strong recommendation specifically for acne that is severe, causing psychosocial burden, causing scarring, or failing standard therapy. Truncal acne falls disproportionately into that category because of surface area and scarring propensity — the most defensible evidence-based statement anyone can make about managing it.

A second tier is graded conditional, not strong: topical clascoterone, salicylic acid and azelaic acid; oral minocycline and sarecycline; combined oral contraceptive pills; and spironolactone. Clinics presenting spironolactone or the pill as first-line for adult acne are overstating what the guideline says.

The good practice statements matter most for a back: combine topicals with different mechanisms rather than stacking similar ones, limit systemic antibiotic use, pair systemic antibiotics with topicals, and consider intralesional corticosteroid for larger lesions. On duration, a 2024 JAMA Dermatology qualitative study by Festok and colleagues — 30 stakeholders interviewed, weak as evidence but useful for the standard it records — notes that guidelines recommend limiting long-term oral antibiotics for acne to under three months, and that dermatologists prescribe more oral antibiotics per clinician than any other specialty. In India, where oral antibiotics are widely accessible without prescription, that ceiling is worth knowing. These are prescription decisions described educationally; dosing belongs with a doctor.

The trunk-specific retinoid, and what its trial really showed

Trifarotene is the topical retinoid most often described as developed for truncal acne. Its trunk data comes from Blume-Peytavi and colleagues (2020): 453 enrolled, 342 (75.5%) completed, 52 weeks, multicentre — and open-label, uncontrolled, unblinded, with no placebo arm. Three authors were manufacturer employees.

The results: at week 12, physician global assessment success on the trunk was 38.6% and on the face 26.6%. At week 52, trunk success reached 66.9% and face 65.1%, with 57.9% succeeding at both sites. Treatment-related adverse events occurred in 12.6% of patients, none serious, and local irritation peaked at week 1 on the face but at weeks 2 to 4 on the trunk.

The 66.9% figure circulates widely and should not. With 24.5% dropout in an uncontrolled study, completer-based success rates are inflated by design — the patients for whom it did not work are disproportionately the ones who left. The quality-of-life figure rests on 92 of 171 patients who completed that assessment, out of 453 enrolled. An independent 2021 systematic review by Bell and colleagues, with no manufacturer affiliation, concluded: "Future trials should compare its efficacy and tolerability with that of the older, clinically established retinoids. Despite efficacy, cost may be a prohibitive factor." For a treatment that must cover a back and chest nightly, that unanswered cost question is not academic.

Hormonal drivers in adult women with body acne

Adult women with persistent acne, facial or truncal, are often offered hormonal therapy, and this is the one corner of acne medicine where the evidence has genuinely improved. The SAFA trial (Santer and colleagues, 2024) randomised 410 women to spironolactone or placebo, double-blind, dosed at 50 mg daily to week 6 then 100 mg to week 24. Investigator-assessed success at week 12 was 18.5% on spironolactone versus 5.6% on placebo (adjusted odds ratio 5.18, 95% CI 2.18–12.28). Read both halves: the odds ratio is dramatic, the absolute success rate is under one in five.

SAFA also shows the drug is slow. The primary quality-of-life outcome at week 12 barely cleared zero (adjusted difference 1.27, 95% CI 0.07–2.46) and self-reported improvement at week 12 was not significant (OR 1.16, 95% CI 0.70–1.91). By week 24 it changed decisively (adjusted difference 3.77, 95% CI 2.50–5.03). Headache was more common on spironolactone, 20.4% versus 12.0%. Two caveats for Indian readers: only 7.9% of participants (28 of 356) were from non-white backgrounds, and the trial studied facial acne — it did not measure the trunk.

The dosing evidence closest to an Indian population is Patiyasikunt and colleagues (2020), a double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in 63 Thai women: only the 50 mg arm separated from placebo, and potassium and creatinine showed no significant change. In the double-blind FASCE trial of 133 women (Dréno and colleagues, 2024), spironolactone beat doxycycline at six months (p = 0.007) — an argument for hormonal therapy over prolonged antibiotics. All prescription-only decisions. Acne alone is also not a basis for a PCOS diagnosis: the 2023 international PCOS guideline (Teede and colleagues, 254 recommendations, 39 organisations) sets a specific algorithm and calls its own evidence base "low to moderate quality". That diagnosis belongs with endocrinology or gynaecology, not a skin clinic.

Scarring on the back and chest: where the evidence runs out

Scarring is the reason truncal acne deserves early treatment, and also the point where the evidence disappears. This search located zero studies of any acne scar procedure performed on the back or chest. Subcision, TCA CROSS, fractional laser, radiofrequency microneedling — every published trial of each was conducted on faces. When a clinic quotes a session count or percentage for back scarring, that number was measured elsewhere on the body.

Keloid and hypertrophic scarring, which the trunk produces more readily than the face, has a slightly better evidence base — but not an acne-specific one. Zhuang and colleagues pooled 15 randomised trials in Burns (2021): intralesional triamcinolone beat verapamil short-term for vascularity (MD −0.22, 95% CI −0.42 to −0.02), while over the medium term 5-fluorouracil beat triamcinolone for scar height (SMD 0.95, 0.15–1.75) and botulinum toxin A beat it for pliability (SMD 1.99, 0.98–3.00). None of those 15 trials studied acne keloids; the populations were burn, surgical and mixed scars. Acne keloids on the chest and shoulders, common and often severe in South Asian skin, are treated by direct extrapolation from that data.

What is better established is that severity drives scarring. Etgu and Sekerlisoy Tatar's 2025 cross-sectional study of 269 patients found scars in 71.3%, with male sex and severe acne associated with greater scar risk, and post-lesional hyperpigmentation and erythema predicting more severe scarring. That study also linked prior topical and antibiotic use to higher scar risk — almost certainly confounding by indication, since sicker patients receive more treatment, and cross-sectional data cannot establish causation. If scarring is already present, the acne scar assessment is a separate conversation from the acne itself.

Pigmentation after body acne in Indian skin

For Fitzpatrick IV–VI skin, which describes most patients in Mumbai, the mark left after a body acne lesion resolves is often the complaint rather than the lesion — and the procedures used on texture can cause pigmentation as readily as the acne did.

The most useful number is from Argobi and colleagues' 2026 meta-analysis of 8 randomised trials (249 patients) comparing fractional CO2 laser with radiofrequency microneedling. Fractional CO2 carried a relative risk of 4.44 for post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (95% CI 2.39–8.26, p < 0.00001), with erythema lasting 1.72 days longer, while its efficacy advantage was a mean difference of just 0.31 (95% CI 0.13–0.48, p = 0.0005). A small efficacy gain against a 4.4-fold pigmentation penalty runs against ablative lasers in deeper skin, which is why radiofrequency microneedling is often the more sensible starting point.

Two honest complications. In a small open study of 9 Chinese patients with Fitzpatrick III–IV skin (Chan and colleagues, 2010), a single fractional ablative CO2 session produced hyperpigmentation in 55.5% at one month, still present in 11.1% at six months — nine patients is not a population incidence, but the authors called for a review of the modality's role in Asian skin. Against that, two large network meta-analyses pull the other way: Wu and colleagues (68 RCTs, 4,480 patients) found "no strong evidence suggesting a treatment reduced the risk of erythema nor post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation compared to the other treatments," and Li and colleagues (24 RCTs, 1,546 patients) found the same null. Report the conflict, do not resolve it.

Superficial chemical peels carry the best skin-of-colour safety dataset here: Vemula and colleagues reviewed 473 superficial peels in Fitzpatrick III–VI skin, finding an overall complication rate of 3.8% — crusting 2.3%, hyperpigmentation and erythema 1.9% each — all resolving within 8 months, and side effects significantly less common in winter. Fitzpatrick VI skin carried an odds ratio of 5.14 (95% CI 1.21–21.8, p = 0.0118) for any adverse event. The caveat matters here: every side effect in that study was recorded on the face. Where pigmentation rather than texture is the main concern, it is assessed differently.

What the evidence does not support

These claims are common in body-acne marketing and are not supported by the studies that looked for them.

  • "Half of everyone with facial acne has back acne." The 30–60% range comes from a self-reported online survey of 2,000 people, reviewed by a group including an employee of a company selling a truncal acne product.
  • "Trifarotene is proven superior for truncal acne." The 52-week truncal data is open-label and uncontrolled, with 24.5% attrition and three manufacturer-employed co-authors, and it has never been compared head-to-head with older, cheaper retinoids.
  • "One session will transform your back." In a blinded split-face trial of 15 patients (Hendel and colleagues, 2023), one session of fractional CO2 or radiofrequency microneedling produced a median improvement of one point on a ten-point texture scale; best responders reached three points in only 10% of treated areas, median satisfaction 6 out of 10. And that was on faces.
  • "Microdermabrasion and glycolic peels resurface scars." In a systematic review by Kravvas and Al-Niaimi (2017), 27.3% of microdermabrasion patients got no benefit despite eight sessions and only 9.1% achieved good results; 20% glycolic acid gave no improvement in 25% of patients.
  • "Painless, no downtime." Every controlled trial located reports erythema, swelling, pain or scabbing. The meta-analytic difference between modalities was 1.72 days of erythema — duration, not absence.
  • "Our protocol eliminates pigmentation risk." Two network meta-analyses covering 68 and 24 randomised trials found no treatment significantly reduced hyperpigmentation risk relative to the others.
  • Fixed percentage promises. The published improvement range for subcision across 10 studies is 10% to 100% — wide enough to be nearly uninformative. Any guaranteed percentage is invented.
  • Body-specific device claims. With no trial of any acne scar procedure on the back or chest, a claim about what a device does to truncal scarring has no published basis.

Sequencing, and what to ask before you start

Because trunk-specific evidence is sparse, sequencing matters more than device selection. Control active inflammatory acne before considering scar work — the AAD guideline's strong recommendation for isotretinoin in scarring disease is aimed at preventing scars, and Etgu's 2025 data shows severity predicts scarring. Address hyperpigmentation and erythema before or alongside textural work, since both predicted worse scarring in that dataset. In adult women, treat a hormonal driver medically rather than procedurally. Allow scars to mature before revision, noting that no trial defines the correct interval. And combine modalities rather than repeating one: combination beat monotherapy consistently across four network meta-analyses, the most reproducible finding in this field.

One timing question should not be settled by an article. Two 2017 consensus documents — a JAMA Dermatology systematic review covering 32 publications and 1,485 procedures, and an American Society for Dermatologic Surgery task force — concluded there is insufficient evidence to delay superficial chemical peels, non-ablative devices, laser hair removal or fractional procedures in patients currently or recently on oral isotretinoin, noting the traditional 6-to-12 month wait rests on three small case series from the mid-1980s. Both also record that the package insert still advises 6 months' discontinuation. Evidence and approved product information disagree, which makes this an individualised decision with your prescribing doctor.

Three questions are worth asking at any consultation about back or body acne: what study design does this recommendation come from, how many patients were in it, and was it done on the trunk or on faces? For truncal scarring the answer to the third is always "on faces", and a clinician willing to say so is telling you something useful about everything else they say.
