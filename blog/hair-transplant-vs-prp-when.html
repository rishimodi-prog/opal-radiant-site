Hair PRP

PRP vs Hair Transplant: When PRP Is Enough and When It Isn't

By Dr. Punit Sakhare, MD Dermatologist · 2026-10-10 · 12 min read

Quick Facts

The core difference
PRP acts on follicles that still exist and may improve their density; a transplant relocates existing follicular units and does not increase the total number of follicles on the head.
Where PRP has evidence

PRP and hair transplantation are not two strengths of the same treatment. Platelet-rich plasma is injected into scalp that still contains follicles, and the most it has been shown to do is improve the density of the hair those follicles already produce. A transplant does not create follicles either — it relocates them, moving follicular units from the back and sides of the scalp to the areas that have thinned. So the prp vs hair transplant question is not really about how aggressive you are willing to be. It is about whether there are still living follicles where you want hair. Almost every other part of the decision follows from that one answer.

This post sets out where the boundary actually sits according to the published trials, including the trial that deliberately enrolled advanced baldness and found nothing, and the Indian dataset that tells you how much donor hair a scalp can realistically supply. OPAL Radiant is a dermatology-led aesthetic clinic offering hair PRP; we do not perform transplant surgery, which is precisely why it is worth being blunt about when PRP is the wrong tool. This is general information and not medical advice — hair loss needs assessment by a qualified dermatologist.

PRP vs Hair Transplant: The Difference That Decides Every Other Question

PRP and hair transplantation act at opposite ends of the follicle's life. In androgenetic (pattern) hair loss, follicles do not vanish overnight; they miniaturise, producing progressively finer, shorter, paler hairs over successive cycles until the shaft is effectively invisible. Platelet-rich plasma is a concentrate of the patient's own platelets, prepared by centrifuging a blood draw and injected into the scalp; the trials measure what it does to hairs per square centimetre and shaft diameter in regions that still carry follicles. It has no proposed mechanism for scalp where the follicle is gone.

A transplant works on the other side of that line. Follicular units are harvested from the occipital and parietal donor zone and re-sited into the thinned area. The total number of follicles on the head does not increase; the hair is redistributed. That has two consequences people underestimate. Donor supply is finite, so a transplant spends a budget rather than adding to one. And transplanted hair does not protect the native hair around it, which continues to miniaturise on its own schedule.

Underneath both sits medical therapy, which has the strongest evidence of the three. The 2016 Cochrane review of interventions for female pattern hair loss pooled 47 randomised trials and 5,290 participants and found that participant-rated moderate-to-marked regrowth favoured topical minoxidil over placebo, 157/593 versus 77/555, a risk ratio of 1.93 (95% CI 1.51–2.47). Across 8 trials and 1,242 participants, total hair count rose by 13.18 hairs/cm² more than placebo (95% CI 10.92–15.44), graded low-quality evidence. Nothing in the PRP literature is supported at that level. If you are weighing hair fall and thinning options, that ordering matters.

What PRP Has Been Shown To Do, and the Endpoint It Keeps Failing

PRP for pattern hair loss has a real but narrow published effect, and the most important distinction in the whole literature is between improvement versus a patient's own baseline and improvement versus a saline placebo. A 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis of 9 randomised controlled trials in 238 patients with androgenetic alopecia found that PRP increased hair density at 3 and 6 months compared with placebo (P < .05), but that hair count and hair diameter improved against baseline and not against placebo (P > .05). Injecting anything into a scalp with a needle produces some change; the placebo arms show it.

Two placebo-controlled trials found nothing at all. A 2020 randomised placebo-controlled pilot in 30 men (20 PRP, 10 saline) used five PRP treatments at 4–6 week intervals — a more intensive course than most clinics offer — with TrichoScan measurement and an independent blinded reviewer, and reported no improvements on either measure, concluding that PRP as monotherapy does not improve hair growth in men with androgenetic alopecia. A double-blind, multicentre, saline-controlled trial in women found that hair mass index and hair count did not differ significantly between PRP and placebo; 13.3% of treated women reported substantial subjective improvement versus 0% on placebo. That trial is the clearest published demonstration that how PRP feels and what PRP measures can come apart.

The positive evidence is not nothing. A randomised, evaluator-blinded, placebo-controlled half-head study (23 enrolled, 20 analysed, three treatments 30 days apart) reported a mean gain of 33.6 hairs in the target area and 45.9 hairs/cm² in total density against baseline, with histological changes in epidermal thickness and bulge-cell activity. It is 20 patients. That is the scale of the field.

When PRP Is Enough: Early to Moderate Loss With Follicles Still in Place

PRP is a reasonable choice in early-to-moderate pattern hair loss where trichoscopy still shows follicular openings and miniaturised hairs across the affected area — that is, where there is something for a growth-factor injection to act on. It is best supported as an addition to medical therapy rather than a substitute for it. The most useful Indian trial here is a 2023 open-label randomised trial at JIPMER, Puducherry, in 64 men with Norwood grade III–IV loss, comparing 5% topical minoxidil for six months against three monthly PRP sessions. Global photographic response at week 24 was 56% for minoxidil and 38% for PRP — a difference that did not reach significance (P = 0.124), meaning PRP did not beat a topical even in moderate disease. Adverse events occurred in 53% of the PRP arm versus 37% on minoxidil, and patient satisfaction was better with minoxidil.

Read together with the head-to-head literature as a whole, the consistent finding is that PRP combined with minoxidil outperforms either alone. That is a combination story, not a replacement story. So the honest indication for PRP is: someone with visible thinning but preserved follicular density, who is already on or is willing to start medical therapy, and who understands that treatment for pattern loss is ongoing rather than a one-time course.

Doing nothing is also a decision with a measured cost. Pooled five-year placebo arms from two replicate randomised trials in men found a loss of 239 hairs from the target area, a 26.3% decline in density (P < 0.001), with 75% of placebo patients rated visibly worse on global photographic assessment. Waiting does not hold the position; it moves the starting point of whatever you do next.

When PRP Is Not Enough: The Trial That Tested Advanced Baldness

PRP has been tested specifically in advanced pattern baldness, and the result was negative. A randomised, single-blind, intra-patient controlled pilot trial — a PRP-treated square compared against a saline square on the same scalp, 19 men recruited and 17 completed, two sessions one month apart — enrolled Norwood grade 4–6 disease. At six months, terminal hairs went from 87 to 85 and vellus hairs from 43 to 42. The authors' conclusion was that PRP was not effective in treating androgenetic alopecia of grade 4–6. Seventeen completers is a small trial, and it should not be over-read; but it is the only study that deliberately went looking for an effect in advanced loss, and it did not find one.

That fits the mechanism. Where a region of scalp is smooth, with no follicular openings visible on trichoscopy, there is no miniaturised follicle to push back into a longer growth phase. Growth factors delivered to bare skin have no target. This is the point at which the prp vs hair transplant comparison stops being a comparison at all: the two are no longer alternatives, because only one of them has a plausible route to the result being asked for.

It is worth stating the gap precisely rather than overstating it. No randomised trial in the PRP literature has enrolled Norwood VI–VII or Ludwig grade III patients and reported a positive result. That is not proof PRP fails in those grades — it is an absence of evidence that it works. For someone paying for a course of injections, the practical consequence is the same.

The Donor Arithmetic: What a Transplant Can Physically Supply

A hair transplant is limited by donor supply, and there is a specifically Indian dataset for this. A 2019 cross-sectional study of 580 Indian men with Hamilton-Norwood grade III or above, conducted at a single clinic in New Delhi, measured a mean follicular unit density of 78.2 FU/cm² in the scalp donor area and 49.7 FU/cm² in the beard. Assuming 25% extraction, the authors calculated total extractable follicular units of 2,064 from the most limited donor zone, 3,097 from the standard zone, 3,612 from the extended zone, and 824 from the beard. The donor rectangle was defined with its lower border 27–28 mm from a line perpendicular to the tragus passing through the external occipital protuberance.

Three caveats belong with those numbers. This is a single-clinic measurement study, not an outcome study — it says what can be harvested, not what will grow. The 25% extraction figure is a working convention, not a validated safety threshold. And every one of the 580 participants was male; there is no equivalent Indian normative donor dataset for women, which matters because female pattern loss is typically diffuse and the donor zone is less reliably spared.

The reason this belongs in a PRP article is timing. Donor hair is spent once. A 28-year-old with early loss who transplants now, while the surrounding native hair is still miniaturising, may find the transplanted line stranded by 40 and the donor budget already drawn down. That is the standard argument for stabilising with medical therapy first — with or without PRP as an adjunct — and reserving surgery for loss that has demonstrated some stability.

Who Should Not Be Transplanted At All

Some patients are not transplant candidates regardless of how much hair they have lost, and the clearest example is diffuse unpatterned alopecia (DUPA). A 2026 narrative review by a large NYU-led author group describes DUPA as diffuse follicular miniaturisation that includes the occipital region — the donor area itself — which, in the authors' words, "often precludes hair transplantation." Harvesting from a donor zone that is itself miniaturising relocates hair that will thin anyway.

The same review is candid about the diagnostic problem: "Diagnosis remains clinical because histopathologic criteria and standardized trichometric thresholds have not been established." There is no test. DUPA mimics telogen effluvium and diffuse alopecia areata, and the review offers no prevalence figures because none exist. Its stated position on management is that it is "primarily medical, emphasizing stabilization rather than regrowth."

Two things to hold alongside that. It is a narrative review, not a diagnostic-accuracy study, so it carries consensus weight rather than trial weight. And one co-author is affiliated with a commercial hair-transplant group, which is worth knowing when reading a paper about who should and should not be transplanted. The practical takeaway is that a scalp examination including the occipital donor zone under trichoscopy is part of any serious assessment, not an optional extra.

Women Specifically: Why This Decision Runs Differently

For women, the prp vs hair transplant framing is frequently a false binary, because the intervention with the best evidence is neither. Female pattern hair loss is usually diffuse — widening of the midline part with a retained frontal hairline — rather than the discrete bald zones that surgery is designed to fill, which makes both transplant candidacy and donor-zone security harder to establish. The 2016 Cochrane review found investigator-rated improvement with topical minoxidil across 7 studies and 1,181 participants at a risk ratio of 2.35 (95% CI 1.68–3.28). It also found that 5% is not better than 2% on objective measurement: across 3 trials and 631 participants, total hair count showed a mean difference of −2.12 (95% CI −5.47 to 1.23), and adverse events did not differ across 4 trials and 1,006 participants (RR 1.02, 95% CI 0.91–1.20).

The same review found finasteride 1 mg no more effective than placebo in women — improvement in 30/67 versus 33/70, RR 0.95 (95% CI 0.66–1.37), low-quality evidence. And the double-blind saline-controlled PRP trial in women described earlier did not separate PRP from placebo on hair mass index or hair count.

So a realistic sequence for a woman with diffuse thinning is: establish the diagnosis, treat any contributing medical cause, use topical minoxidil correctly and for long enough to judge it, and consider PRP or hair fillers as adjuncts rather than substitutes. Surgery enters the conversation for stable, patterned loss with a secure donor zone, which is a smaller group of women than the marketing suggests.

What the Evidence Does Not Support

Several claims made routinely about both PRP and transplantation are not supported by the published data, and a few are contradicted by it.

  • That PRP results are permanent. In the half-head randomised trial most often cited in PRP's favour, 4 of 20 patients reported progressive hair loss by 12 months, more evident 16 months after the last treatment, and were re-treated. No PRP trial has follow-up beyond two years.
  • That PRP thickens each hair shaft. Diameter is the endpoint PRP most consistently fails. The 2023 meta-analysis of 9 randomised trials found hair count and diameter improved versus baseline but not versus placebo.
  • That PRP can replace minoxidil. In the 64-man JIPMER trial, PRP produced a 38% response rate against minoxidil's 56%, and no head-to-head comparison has shown PRP superior on an objective endpoint.
  • That PRP works in advanced baldness. Tested in Norwood 4–6 and negative: terminal hairs 87 to 85 over six months.
  • That PRP is painless or free of side effects. The JIPMER trial recorded adverse events in 53% of PRP patients, a higher rate than the topical comparator.
  • That anyone can have a transplant. Diffuse unpatterned alopecia miniaturises the donor zone itself and often precludes surgery, and no histopathologic or trichometric criteria exist to diagnose it.
  • That a transplant is a one-time permanent fix. Untreated pattern loss in placebo arms progressed by 26.3% of density over five years. Native hair around a graft keeps behaving the way untreated hair behaves.

One further gap deserves naming because it applies directly to this readership: no trial in the PRP literature reports Fitzpatrick phototype, let alone stratifies outcomes by it. Every efficacy figure quoted above is being extrapolated to Fitzpatrick IV–VI skin from cohorts whose skin type was never recorded. Nobody can tell you what PRP does specifically in Indian skin, because nobody has measured it.

How the Decision Gets Made in a Consultation

The decision between PRP, medical therapy and a surgical referral is made on examination, not on a price list. A useful assessment covers the pattern of loss and its stage, trichoscopy of both the affected area and the occipital donor zone to look for miniaturisation where there should not be any, a pull test, and a targeted history — recent illness, pregnancy, thyroid symptoms, medication changes, iron status where clinically indicated, and any features suggesting polycystic ovary syndrome in women. Grading scales such as Ludwig and Sinclair are descriptive tools, and their published critique is that meaningful volume is already lost by the time a patient meets the earliest grade.

Where that assessment lands on early-to-moderate loss with follicles intact, PRP is a defensible adjunct alongside medical therapy, on the understanding that pooled trial evidence supports density gains and not shaft thickening, that results are not permanent, and that a maintenance schedule is part of the plan rather than a failure of it. Where it lands on advanced loss with no follicular openings, or on a stable patterned defect in someone with a secure donor zone, the honest answer is a surgical opinion — and OPAL does not perform transplant surgery, so that means a referral rather than a sale. Where the donor zone is itself miniaturising, the answer is medical stabilisation and neither of the above.
