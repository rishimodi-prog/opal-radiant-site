Pigmentation

Pigmentation Around the Mouth: Causes, and What the Evidence Says About Treating It

By Dr. Punit Sakhare, MD Dermatologist · 2026-08-30 · 12 min read

Pigmentation around the mouth — a brown or grey-brown shadow on the upper lip, a darker ring at the corners, dusky skin over the chin — is one of the most frequently raised complaints at a dermatology desk in Mumbai and one of the least studied conditions in dermatology. A search of the indexed literature returns no controlled trial of perioral hyperpigmentation as a distinct entity. Every cream, peel and laser offered for it is borrowed from research on melasma, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation or lip mucosa, and the borrowing is almost never disclosed.

That is the honest starting point, and it changes what a sensible plan for pigmentation around mouth and chin looks like. The valuable work here is diagnostic rather than technological, because at least five distinct processes produce darkening in the same few centimetres of skin and they behave very differently. This article sets out what each cause looks like, what the trial evidence shows for the treatments in common use, which numbers come from Indian skin, and which marketed claims fall apart when you read the studies underneath them.

What pigmentation around the mouth actually is

Pigmentation around the mouth is not a diagnosis. It is a location, and several unrelated conditions occupy it. The most useful organising principle comes from a 2023 Journal of the American Academy of Dermatology review by Wang and colleagues, which classifies disorders of hyperpigmentation by depth of pigment deposition — epidermal, dermal or mixed — rather than by the name a patient arrives with.

Depth matters because it predicts response. A 2011 review by Callender and colleagues gives the colour rule: epidermal pigment appears tan, brown or dark brown, dermal pigment blue-grey. Dermal pigment sits below the reach of topical agents, which is why an eight-week course of a lightening cream can clear one patch and leave the patch beside it unchanged.

One caveat belongs here. Wood's lamp examination is used across Indian practice to sort pigmentation into epidermal, dermal and mixed types, but no adequately powered study validating that classification against histopathology surfaced in this research. Treat epidermal-versus-dermal typing as clinical consensus, not established fact.

The conditions sharing this territory are melasma extending onto the upper lip, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, pigmented contact dermatitis, drug-induced pigmentation, constitutional pigmentation, and rarely a lesion that needs a biopsy rather than a facial.

The evidence base for perioral darkening is close to empty

The evidence base for perioral darkening is close to empty, and saying so plainly is more useful than any protocol. Melasma has a 2020 systematic review covering 113 randomised and controlled trials with 6,897 participants. Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation has a 2024 systematic review covering 48 studies in 1,356 people with skin of colour. Perioral hyperpigmentation as a named condition has zero dedicated trials.

The practical consequence is specific: any clinic quoting a success rate for “perioral pigmentation treatment” is quoting a number generated in a different condition, usually melasma on the cheeks, usually from a study measuring MASI at the end of treatment rather than a year later. That number may be the best available guide. It is not a result in this indication.

The consequence for a reader: the first appointment should produce a cause, not a package. Melasma on the upper lip, a steroid-damaged perioral dermatitis and a contact reaction to toothpaste need unrelated plans, and no device distinguishes them.

Melasma reaching the upper lip

Melasma is the commonest medical cause of symmetrical darkening on the upper lip, because the upper lip is part of the centrofacial pattern. In a cross-sectional multicentre study of 331 Indian adults by KrupaShankar and colleagues, published in Dermatology and Therapy in 2014, the centrofacial pattern accounted for 42% of cases and the malar pattern 39%. Mean age was 37.2 ± 9.3 years and a family history was reported by 31%. Only 35% used any sunscreen and 10% used SPF above 50. It was a hospital-based sample, so those proportions describe people who sought care, not the community.

Melasma is also not a simple deposit of pigment in the epidermis, which is why it returns. A 2016 review by Kwon and colleagues describes lesional skin as showing epidermal hyperpigmentation plus solar elastosis, a disrupted basement membrane, increased dermal vascularity and increased mast cells. Melanin dropping through a damaged basement membrane sits in the dermis, where creams cannot reach it.

The relapse numbers follow from that. In a retrospective cohort of 957 patients followed over five years at PGIMER Chandigarh, reported by Kumaran and colleagues in Dermatologic Therapy in 2021, patients on oral tranexamic acid mostly relapsed within six weeks of stopping, triple combination cream users at around two months, and non-hydroquinone regimens at around four months. Response rates at 16 weeks were 33.6% and 40.9% depending on regimen. It is uncontrolled and retrospective, but it is the largest Indian long-term follow-up available, and those timings are the honest answer to “how long will this last”. More sits in our guide to melasma and pigmentation.

Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation: the commonest perioral cause

Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation is the commonest perioral cause and the one most often mistaken for melasma. It follows the shape of whatever inflamed the skin first — perioral dermatitis, acne along the chin and jawline, eczema from lip-licking, mechanical injury. A 2022 review by Maghfour and colleagues describes increased melanin in the epidermis and dermis as the hallmark, and states plainly that “the exact mechanisms underlying PIH are not completely understood.”

The treatment picture is sobering. Mar and colleagues' 2024 systematic review of 48 studies in 1,356 people with skin of colour — Fitzpatrick IV in 40%, V in 34%, VI in 6% — found 89% of cases were precipitated by an inflammatory condition and 83% were facial. Topical retinoids were the most-used intervention at 22%, with partial improvement in 85%. Laser at 17% gave partial improvement in 66% and was the only modality producing complete resolution in a subgroup, at 26% — but the same review recorded cases of PIH being made worse by laser. Peels and hydroquinone performed less well. The authors concluded there is “a lack of robust efficacy across all treatment modalities.”

Repeated hair removal on the upper lip belongs here. Mechanical injury is a named PIH trigger, and threading and waxing deliver it every few weeks to skin already inflamed. No controlled study quantifies how much perioral pigmentation is attributable to threading, so this is mechanism and clinical consensus, not a measured effect. It is, though, why reducing the frequency of irritation with laser hair reduction can change the pigmentation picture more than a cream does — an observation no trial has tested.

Contact reactions, drugs, smoking and steroid creams

Contact reactions, drugs, smoking and steroid creams account for much of the perioral pigmentation that does not fit melasma or acne marks. A 2012 review by Vachiramon and McMichael in the International Journal of Dermatology sets out the categories — physiological change, genodermatoses, inflammatory disease, endocrine disorders including Addison's, drugs and chemicals, and neoplasms — while conceding that much of the evidence beneath individual causes is case reports and small series “that may not reflect true association.” Pigmented contact dermatitis, also called Riehl's melanosis, from lipsticks, toothpaste ingredients or hair dye running down the face is a recognised perioral cause, though no prevalence study of allergic contact cheilitis in an Indian population surfaced in this research. Drug-induced pigmentation is documented for minocycline and hydroxychloroquine among others.

Smoking has better numbers, though from an adjacent site. A cross-sectional study of 1,275 dental patients published by Hassona and colleagues in 2016 found oral pigmentation in 386 people (30.2%), of which racial or physiological pigmentation accounted for 39.9% and smoker's melanosis for 32.9% — together roughly three-quarters. These are oral mucosal figures from a Jordanian dental population, not perioral skin in Indians, and are indicative only.

The Indian-specific cause deserving most attention is topical corticosteroid use. A prospective study across 12 Indian centres by Saraswat and colleagues screened 2,926 patients with facial dermatoses and found 433 (14.8%) using topical corticosteroids, the commonest reason being use as a fairness cream (126 patients, 29%). Adverse effects occurred in 392 of 433 — 90.5%, and 59.3% had been recommended the steroid by a non-physician. Perioral skin is thin, and steroid-induced perioral dermatitis followed by post-inflammatory pigmentation is a loop that starts with a cream bought to lighten the area.

What the trial evidence supports, in order

What the trial evidence supports, in order, starts with topicals rather than devices. The 2020 systematic review by McKesey, Tovar-Garza and Pandya — 113 randomised and controlled trials, 6,897 participants — concluded that triple combination cream and hydroquinone monotherapy are the most effective and best-studied options in melasma, and that chemical peels and laser or light devices are equal or inferior to topicals while carrying higher adverse-effect risk. That hierarchy points away from the treatments most heavily marketed.

Hydroquinone in India is a prescription drug: since 1 April 2019 it sits in Schedule H, and over-the-counter sale of hydroquinone-containing topicals is prohibited. It is used under medical supervision in intermittent courses, not sourced independently.

Oral tranexamic acid has the best systemic evidence, including a trial run in this city. Minni and Poojary's triple-blind, placebo-controlled trial at K.J. Somaiya Medical College in Mumbai randomised 130 patients and completed 120. At week 12, 65.6% on tranexamic acid plus triple combination cream achieved more than 75% mMASI improvement versus 27.1% on cream plus placebo; recurrence at 24 weeks was 18.03% versus 64.4%. A different trial disagrees on recurrence: Lajevardi and colleagues' assessor-blinded randomised trial of 100 patients found relapse of 30% with tranexamic acid versus 26% without — not a significant difference. The disagreement is unresolved. Safety is not trivial either: a retrospective series of 561 patients in Singapore recorded adverse events in 7.1%, including one deep vein thrombosis in a patient later found to have familial protein S deficiency. Screening for thrombotic risk before prescribing is not optional.

Photoprotection is the one intervention that acts on cause rather than consequence. In a double-blind randomised trial by Castanedo-Cazares and colleagues — 68 randomised, 61 completed — both arms received 4% hydroquinone and differed only in sunscreen. The arm using an iron-oxide-tinted sunscreen covering visible light as well as UV improved 15% more on MASI and 28% more on colorimetry than the UV-only arm. The histological melanin difference was only 4%, and the study was small and single-centre. It remains the cornerstone citation for tinted sunscreen in deeper skin, where visible light provokes a tanning response it does not in fair skin.

Peels, lasers and devices around the mouth: the risk arithmetic in Indian skin

Peels, lasers and devices around the mouth carry a risk profile that changes with Fitzpatrick type. A retrospective study by Vemula and colleagues of 473 superficial chemical peels in Fitzpatrick III–VI skin found an overall complication rate of 3.8%, but Fitzpatrick VI carried an odds ratio of 5.14 (95% CI 1.21–21.8) for any adverse event. Crusting was commonest at 2.3%, with post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation and erythema tied at 1.9%. All resolved within eight months, and side effects were significantly less common in winter, which in Mumbai argues for the drier months.

For efficacy in Indian skin, Sarkar and colleagues randomised 90 Indian patients with melasma across three peel types. MASI reduction at 12 weeks was 62.36% with 35% glycolic acid, 60.98% with salicylic-mandelic and 44.71% with phytic acid; the first two beat phytic (p = 0.00) and did not differ from each other (p = 0.876). The authors judged salicylic-mandelic better tolerated and more suitable for Indian skin. The confound is important: every arm was primed for four weeks with hydroquinone and tretinoin, so these are not peel-alone results. A chemical peel used perioral belongs inside a regimen, at superficial depth, out of the sun season.

More energy is not better. Kar and colleagues randomised 75 Indian patients between low-fluence Q-switched Nd:YAG, high-fluence Q-switched Nd:YAG and a glycolic peel; high-fluence performed significantly worse than both (p < 0.005 and p < 0.05). Laser toning has its own signature complications: a retrospective series of 360 Asian patients by Choi and colleagues recorded mottled hypopigmentation or rebound hyperpigmentation in 14.1% of the monotherapy toning group. Microneedling carried the highest adverse-event rate of any modality in a 2021 network meta-analysis of 59 randomised trials, at 52.3%, against 10.1% for tretinoin.

What the evidence does not support

What the evidence does not support is a longer list than what it does, and it is where most money is spent.

That any treatment has demonstrated efficacy specifically for perioral pigmentation. No controlled trial of perioral hyperpigmentation as a distinct condition exists in the indexed literature; efficacy figures quoted for it are transplanted from melasma or PIH research.

That laser removes pigmentation permanently. A randomised split-face study by Zhou and colleagues with two-year follow-up — 20 enrolled, 17 analysed — found recurrence or exacerbation in 10 of 17 patients (58.82%), with no difference between a 755 nm picosecond laser and a 1064 nm Q-switched Nd:YAG. At six months the picosecond side showed no statistically significant difference from baseline, while the older Q-switched side did (p = 0.022). Newer is not automatically better, and permanence is not on offer.

That stronger settings clear pigment faster. Kar's Indian trial found the opposite.

That intravenous glutathione lightens skin. A 2025 systematic review by Sarkar and colleagues found the only placebo-controlled study of intravenous glutathione reported 6 of 16 responders (37.5%) versus 3 (18.7%), p = 0.054 — not statistically significant. The review concludes intravenous glutathione is contraindicated for lack of efficacy and side effects. Oral and topical forms give moderate, unsustainable lightening at best.

That a lightening cream bought over the counter is a safe shortcut. Saraswat's 12-centre Indian study found 90.5% adverse effects among facial steroid users, with fairness-cream use the leading indication. Around the mouth it is the fastest route to entrenching the problem.

That a “de-tan” treatment removes a tan. No trial with tanning as a defined endpoint exists. Delayed tanning fades as the epidermis turns over, and no study has compared a de-tan treatment against doing nothing over the same weeks, so the result cannot be separated from natural fading.

When darkening around the mouth needs a diagnosis, not a treatment

Darkening around the mouth needs a diagnosis rather than a treatment whenever it is solitary, new, asymmetric or changing. Symmetrical, gradual, bilateral pigmentation that has crept in over years is a pigmentary condition; a single patch that has appeared or altered is a question to answer before anything is done to it.

The reason is documented. A review by Lallas and colleagues of facial pigmented macules notes that the differential on chronically sun-damaged facial skin includes lentigo maligna, pigmented actinic keratosis, solar lentigo and lichen-planus-like keratosis, and that these “may reveal overlapping criteria, making their differential diagnosis clinically difficult”; dermatoscopy improves accuracy but does not resolve the problem. On the lip itself, Uribe and colleagues found that dermoscopy alone correctly classified benign lip macules in only 13 of 16 cases, and flagged 5 of 6 lip melanomas as equivocal or malignant — meaning one was missed. The melanoma sample was six lesions, so precision is limited, but the direction is not.

Two other referral triggers matter. Sudden or rapidly progressive darkening, particularly with mucosal involvement, fatigue or weight change, points to systemic causes a cosmetic clinic should not treat. And a perioral rash treated with a steroid cream for weeks needs the steroid withdrawn under supervision, not another active on top.

An assessment rather than a package is what a consultation should produce: a named cause, the evidence behind whatever is proposed, and an honest relapse figure. This article is general information and not medical advice. Pigmentation around the mouth has causes ranging from cosmetic to systemic and should be assessed in person by a qualified dermatologist before any treatment is started.
