Profhilo and Injectable Skin Boosters: What They Are and Do They Work?

By Dr. Punit Sakhare, MD Dermatologist · 2026-11-18 · 8 min read

'Skin boosters' and Profhilo have become some of the most talked-about injectables, promising glowing, firmer, better-quality skin rather than the plumped lips or sculpted cheeks of traditional fillers. They're a genuinely interesting category — and one where it's easy to be swept up in marketing without understanding what they actually are, what the evidence really shows, and how they compare to the energy-based treatments (like HIFU and microneedling RF) that address some of the same concerns.

This guide explains what injectable skin boosters are and how they differ fundamentally from fillers, how Profhilo in particular works, what the clinical evidence does and doesn't support, how boosters compare to energy devices so you can tell which suits your goal, and the practical realities — sessions, results, longevity and safety. The aim is to let you judge whether a skin booster is right for you on the evidence, rather than on the hype that currently surrounds this category.

Skin Boosters Are Not Fillers

The most important thing to understand is the difference between a skin booster and a dermal filler, because they're often confused and they do opposite jobs. A traditional filler is a firmer gel placed in a specific spot to add volume or structure — plumping lips, building cheek projection, softening a deep fold. It stays where it's put and changes shape.

A skin booster, by contrast, is a thinner, more spreadable product injected into the skin across an area to improve the skin's overall quality — its hydration, smoothness, firmness and glow — rather than to add volume or change contour. It disperses through the skin and works at the tissue level. So you'd choose a filler to change shape and a skin booster to change skin quality. This distinction matters practically: if your concern is dull, crepey, dehydrated or slightly lax skin texture, a booster is the relevant category; if it's loss of volume or a specific contour, that's filler territory. Many people confuse the two and end up disappointed because they chose the wrong tool for their actual concern.

How Profhilo Works

Profhilo is the best-known skin booster, and it's essentially a stabilised hyaluronic acid product. Hyaluronic acid is the molecule the body uses to hold water in the skin, and it's well established as a hydrating agent that improves skin hydration. What makes Profhilo distinctive is that it combines high and low molecular weight hyaluronic acid in a stabilised form, so after injection it spreads through the skin and releases slowly, delivering prolonged hydration and, according to its proposed mechanism, stimulating the skin's own production of collagen and elastin over time — a process often called 'bio-remodelling'.

In practice it's injected at a small number of precise points, from which it spreads, and the effect builds over the weeks following a short course. The intended result is not a change in shape but skin that is more hydrated, firmer, smoother and more radiant — an improvement in quality. It's part of a broader family of injectable boosters that also includes products based on other ingredients (for example polynucleotides/PDRN and multi-ingredient 'mesotherapy' cocktails), all sharing the same basic idea of improving skin quality from within rather than adding volume.

What the Evidence Actually Shows

Here honesty matters, because the marketing runs ahead of the evidence. There is supporting data: according to PubMed, a randomised clinical trial using Profhilo for neck rejuvenation found that adding Profhilo gel to botulinum toxin significantly improved skin hydration and thickness and gave better overall aesthetic improvement than the toxin alone, concluding it's a safe and more effective option for neck ageing in people who aren't surgical candidates. That's genuine, measured improvement in skin-quality parameters.

At the same time, it's fair to say the overall evidence base for skin boosters is still emerging and smaller than for long-established treatments — many studies are small, some are manufacturer-linked, and long-term comparative data is limited. The hydrating mechanism (via hyaluronic acid) is well grounded, and early results on skin quality are promising, but claims of dramatic 'lifting' or 'tightening' equivalent to energy devices or surgery aren't well supported — boosters improve skin quality and hydration, modest firmness, and glow, not major laxity. The sensible reading: skin boosters are a reasonable, evidence-supported option for improving skin quality and hydration, with realistic (not miraculous) expectations, and the newest products in the category have the thinnest evidence, so a degree of caution about the latest 'it' injectable is wise.

Boosters vs HIFU and Microneedling RF

People often ask whether to get a skin booster or an energy treatment like HIFU or MNRF, as if they're interchangeable. They overlap but address different things, so the better question is what your main concern is:

  • Skin boosters (Profhilo etc.): best for skin quality — hydration, glow, smoothness, mild firmness, crepey texture. They add hydration and stimulate some collagen from within, but don't meaningfully lift or tighten significant laxity.
  • HIFU: best for firming and subtle lifting of mild-to-moderate laxity, by stimulating collagen deep in the skin with focused ultrasound. More about structure/tightness than surface quality or hydration.
  • Microneedling RF (MNRF): best for texture, pores, acne scars and skin tightening via collagen remodelling, working through the dermis.

So: dull, dehydrated, crepey skin quality → booster; early laxity and firmness → HIFU; texture, scars, pores → MNRF. Crucially, these are often complementary rather than either/or — a common approach pairs a skin booster (for quality and hydration) with HIFU or MNRF (for firmness and texture), since they work by different mechanisms on different aspects of ageing. A clinician assessing your skin can tell you whether one, or a combination, fits your goals and budget better than chasing whichever treatment is most marketed.

What to Expect: Sessions, Results and Longevity

A typical skin booster course looks like this. Profhilo is usually given as two initial sessions about four weeks apart, with the effect building over the following weeks as hydration and bio-remodelling take hold. Results — more hydrated, smoother, firmer, more radiant skin — generally become apparent over a month or two rather than instantly. The effect then lasts in the range of several months, after which maintenance sessions (often every six months or so) keep it going, because the hyaluronic acid is gradually metabolised and the stimulated effect fades.

The procedure itself is quick, done with fine needles at defined points, with minimal downtime — small bumps or mild bruising at injection points that settle within a day or two. It's comfortable for most people, sometimes with numbing cream. The realistic result to expect is a visible improvement in skin quality — the 'my skin looks healthier and more hydrated' kind of change — not a dramatic lift or a change in facial shape. People who go in wanting glow, hydration and a subtle firming are generally pleased; people expecting a filler-like volumising or a HIFU-like lift are looking at the wrong treatment. As with all injectables, results and longevity vary between individuals.

Who Skin Boosters Suit Best

Skin boosters aren't for everyone or every concern, and knowing whether you're a good fit saves money and disappointment. They tend to suit people who have good facial volume and structure but whose skin quality has declined — skin that looks dull, dehydrated, a little crepey or has lost some bounce, often from the mid-30s onward, without significant sagging or volume loss. If your complaint is 'my skin just looks tired, dry and lacklustre rather than old', that's squarely booster territory. They're also used on areas where skin quality shows and other treatments are tricky, such as the neck, décolletage and backs of the hands.

They're a poorer fit if your main concern is something a booster doesn't address. If you've lost volume and want restored contour, that's filler. If you have real laxity and want lifting, that's HIFU, energy devices or ultimately surgery. If your issue is pigmentation, a booster won't treat it — that's peels, lasers and topicals. And if you have active skin infection, certain medical conditions, a relevant allergy, or you're pregnant or breastfeeding, boosters are generally deferred or avoided. A frequent, honest outcome of a good consultation is discovering that what you actually want is a different treatment altogether, or a combination — which is far better to learn before injecting than after.

It's also worth being realistic about cost and commitment: boosters are an injectable course with maintenance every several months, so they're an ongoing investment rather than a one-off. For someone whose priority is skin quality and who's happy to maintain it, that can be well worth it; for someone wanting a single dramatic change, the budget is usually better spent elsewhere. Matching the treatment honestly to the concern, the expectation and the willingness to maintain is the whole game — and it's exactly what the next section's 'choose well' principles are about.

Safety and Choosing Well

Skin boosters are injectables and therefore medical procedures, not spa treatments — this is the single most important safety point. They must be performed by a trained, qualified injector (a dermatologist or appropriately trained doctor), using genuine, regulated products, under sterile conditions. The risks of injectables in untrained hands — infection, injecting into the wrong plane or a vessel, nodules, poor results — are real and avoidable by choosing properly. The surge of 'skin booster' and 'glow drip' offerings, some of unclear provenance, makes this caution especially relevant: be sure what's actually being injected and by whom.

Be wary, too, of the newest and most heavily marketed injectables with the thinnest evidence, and of anything promising dramatic lifting or whitening from a 'booster' — those claims outrun what this category does. Contraindications (pregnancy, certain conditions, active infection at the site, allergy) should be screened at consultation. Choosing well comes down to the same principles as any injectable: a qualified medical injector, a genuine product, realistic expectations set honestly, and a treatment matched to your actual concern. If your goal is better skin quality and hydration and you're treated by the right person, a skin booster is a reasonable, evidence-supported option. The short version, if you take away one thing: skin boosters are a legitimate, evidence-supported way to improve skin quality and hydration, not a filler and not a face-lift — they do a real but modest job, over a maintained course, and they're only as safe as the product and the injector behind them. Match them honestly to the concern, treat the latest hyped variants with healthy scepticism, and choose the practitioner over the buzzword, and they're a reasonable addition to a thoughtful skin plan.
