PCOS and Your Skin: Acne, Pigmentation and the Treatments That Help

By Dr. Punit Sakhare, MD Dermatologist · 2026-11-30 · 8 min read

Quick Facts

Why PCOS affects skin
PCOS commonly raises androgen (male-hormone) levels and involves insulin resistance, which together drive several skin and hair changes.
Common skin signs
Hormonal acne (often jawline), excess facial/body hair (hirsutism), scalp hair thinning, and dark velvety patches (acanthosis nigricans) on the neck and underarms.
The key principle
PCOS skin issues respond best to treating the hormones/metabolism AND the skin together — dermatology alone, without addressing the cause, underperforms.
Acanthosis = a signal
Dark, velvety neck/underarm pigmentation often reflects insulin resistance — a reason to check metabolic health, not just treat the skin.
Needs a team

For many women, the first sign of PCOS (polycystic ovary syndrome) isn't a period problem — it's the skin: stubborn adult acne along the jawline, unwanted facial hair, thinning scalp hair, or dark velvety patches on the neck. These aren't separate, random issues; they're connected expressions of the hormonal and metabolic changes of PCOS, which is exactly why treating them in isolation — a cream for the acne here, threading for the hair there — so often underwhelms. The skin changes are driven by an underlying cause, and the most effective approach treats that cause alongside the skin.

This guide explains how PCOS affects the skin and hair, walks through each of the common manifestations — acne, hirsutism, hair loss and pigmentation like acanthosis nigricans — and the treatments that genuinely help each, and makes the central case: that PCOS skin is best managed by addressing the hormones and metabolism and the skin together, ideally with a gynaecologist or endocrinologist and a dermatologist working in tandem. If PCOS is behind your skin concerns, understanding the connection is the key to treating them effectively rather than endlessly chasing the symptoms.

Why PCOS Shows Up on Your Skin

PCOS is a common hormonal and metabolic condition in women of reproductive age, and its skin effects trace back to two linked drivers. The first is hyperandrogenism — higher levels of androgens (hormones like testosterone that everyone has, but which are often elevated in PCOS). Androgens act on the skin's oil glands and hair follicles, which is why they produce the classic cluster of acne, excess hair in a male-pattern distribution, and scalp hair thinning. The second driver is insulin resistance, common in PCOS, which both worsens the hormonal picture and directly causes certain skin changes like acanthosis nigricans and skin tags.

According to PubMed, a review of the association between PCOS and its dermatological manifestations describes this as an intricate, interconnecting network involving hormonal, inflammatory and genetic factors, and notes the strong association between PCOS and specific skin conditions — to the point that the authors suggest women presenting to dermatology with these signs should be screened for PCOS, because the skin can be the presenting clue to an undiagnosed condition. That's the key insight: the acne, hair and pigmentation of PCOS aren't unrelated skin problems to be treated one by one, but surface expressions of an underlying hormonal-metabolic state — which is precisely why addressing that state is central to treating them, as the rest of this guide explains.

Acne in PCOS

PCOS acne has a characteristic pattern: it often appears or persists in adulthood (when 'teenage acne' should have settled), tends to concentrate on the lower face and jawline, chin and neck, can flare cyclically with the menstrual cycle, and is frequently deeper and more inflammatory (papules, nodules) rather than just surface blackheads. It's driven by androgens increasing oil production and follicular changes, so it behaves like the hormonal condition it is — which has direct treatment implications.

Because it's hormonally driven, PCOS acne often responds poorly to over-the-counter products and surface treatments alone, and responds well to treatment that addresses the hormones: dermatologists frequently use topical treatments (retinoids and others), and importantly hormonal or anti-androgen therapy (such as certain combined oral contraceptives or anti-androgen medication) and, where insulin resistance is a factor, metabolic treatment — all alongside standard acne care, and in severe cases oral medication. The practical message is that for PCOS acne, a facial or a cream is rarely enough; the effective route is dermatologist-guided treatment that tackles the hormonal driver, which is why getting the PCOS itself assessed and managed matters so much for the skin. Clearing the acne also matters because, left inflamed, it leaves marks and scars — so controlling it early protects the skin long-term. Our guide on hormonal acne in adult women goes deeper.

Hirsutism (Excess Hair) and Hair Loss

Two opposite-seeming hair problems both stem from PCOS androgens. Hirsutism — excess coarse, dark hair in a male-pattern distribution (upper lip, chin, jaw, neck, chest, lower abdomen) — is one of the most distressing PCOS signs, caused by androgens driving fine vellus hairs to become coarse terminal hairs. It responds best to a combination: laser hair removal to reduce the hair (though hormone-driven hair is more stubborn and needs more sessions and maintenance), plus medical management of the underlying androgens (anti-androgens, hormonal treatment) so new hair is less driven. Laser alone, without addressing the hormones, can feel like it 'doesn't last', because the hormonal stimulus keeps pushing new follicles — which is the classic reason PCOS hirsutism frustrates people who only ever treat the hair, not the cause.

Paradoxically, the same androgens can cause scalp hair thinning — female-pattern hair loss with thinning over the crown — which also benefits from addressing the hormonal driver alongside treatments like minoxidil (and, where appropriate, anti-androgens), under guidance. Our guides on laser hair removal and hormonal conditions, female pattern hair loss and PCOS hair growth cover these. The unifying theme repeats: for both excess hair and hair loss in PCOS, treating the hormones and the hair together works far better than either alone.

Pigmentation: Acanthosis Nigricans and More

One of the most under-recognised PCOS skin signs is acanthosis nigricans — dark, thickened, velvety patches of skin, typically on the back and sides of the neck, the underarms, and skin folds. Unlike ordinary pigmentation, acanthosis nigricans is strongly associated with insulin resistance, so its appearance is a visible signal of the metabolic side of PCOS — in effect, the skin flagging that blood-sugar handling needs attention. This matters hugely for treatment: while topical treatments can modestly improve the appearance, the most important 'treatment' is addressing the insulin resistance through weight management, diet, exercise and, where indicated, medical therapy — and doing so can improve the skin as the metabolic picture improves.

So dark neck or underarm patches in the context of PCOS shouldn't just be lightened cosmetically and ignored — they're a reason to check metabolic health. Beyond acanthosis, PCOS-related acne also leaves post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (the brown marks after breakouts), especially on Indian skin, which responds to the usual pigmentation care (sunscreen, topicals, gentle peels) once the acne is controlled. And skin tags, also linked to insulin resistance, commonly accompany acanthosis. The pattern, again, is that PCOS pigmentation is best treated by addressing the underlying metabolic cause alongside the skin — treating the neck patches with creams while ignoring the insulin resistance they signal is treating the symptom and missing the message. Our guide on neck pigmentation and acanthosis nigricans covers this specifically.

The Combined Approach That Actually Works

If there's one thing to take from this guide, it's that PCOS skin is managed best by treating the cause and the skin together, not the skin alone. That means a two-pronged approach: on one side, managing the PCOS itself — with a gynaecologist or endocrinologist — through lifestyle (diet and exercise genuinely help the hormonal and metabolic picture), weight management where relevant, and medical treatment (hormonal or anti-androgen therapy, and metabolic treatment for insulin resistance as indicated). On the other side, dermatological treatment of the skin and hair — acne care, laser for hirsutism, hair-loss treatment, pigmentation care — targeted at the visible problems.

These two sides reinforce each other: addressing the hormones makes the skin treatments work better and last longer (laser hair removal holds better when androgens are managed; acne stays clearer), while the dermatological treatments handle the visible concerns that lifestyle and hormones alone are slow to fix. The common failure is doing only one side — endlessly treating acne or threading hair while the underlying PCOS goes unmanaged, so the problems keep returning. This is why the ideal is a coordinated team: a gynaecologist/endocrinologist for the PCOS and a dermatologist for the skin and hair, communicating so the plan is joined-up. For a woman with PCOS skin concerns, the single most effective step is usually to get the PCOS properly diagnosed and managed as well as treating the skin — not instead of it, and not the skin instead of it.

Diagnosis, Expectations and the Bottom Line

Because the skin can be the presenting sign of PCOS, and because effective treatment depends on addressing the cause, diagnosis comes first. If you have the cluster — adult jawline acne, excess facial/body hair, scalp thinning, dark velvety neck patches, often with irregular periods or difficulty with weight — it's worth being assessed for PCOS (typically involving history, examination, blood tests for hormones and metabolic markers, and sometimes an ultrasound) rather than treating each skin issue in isolation. Diagnosing and managing the PCOS doesn't just help the skin; it matters for fertility, metabolic and long-term health too.

On expectations: PCOS skin concerns are very treatable, but they're managed rather than cured, because PCOS itself is a chronic condition. Acne can be cleared and kept clear, hirsutism substantially reduced, hair loss slowed or improved, and pigmentation lightened — but all of it needs ongoing management and maintenance, and results build over months, not days. The realistic, encouraging goal is good control of all the skin and hair manifestations, maintained over time, as part of managing the underlying PCOS. The worst approach is piecemeal symptom-chasing without addressing the cause; the best is a coordinated, patient, two-pronged plan. If there's a single reassurance to leave with, it's this: PCOS skin and hair changes can feel relentless and demoralising — the acne that won't clear, the hair that keeps returning, the patches that won't fade — but they are among the most treatable parts of PCOS once the approach is right. The frustration almost always comes from treating the surface in isolation while the hormonal and metabolic drivers go unaddressed. Join the two up — manage the PCOS with the right medical team and treat the skin and hair with a dermatologist in parallel — and the picture usually improves steadily and stays improved with maintenance. It's not a quick fix, but it is a genuinely effective one, and understanding the connection between the condition and your skin is the first and most empowering step toward treating it properly rather than chasing each symptom in turn, month after month, while the real driver goes unaddressed.
