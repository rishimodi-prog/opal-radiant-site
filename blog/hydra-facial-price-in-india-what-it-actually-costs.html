Hydra Facial Price in India: What It Actually Costs, and Why the Range Is So Wide

By Dr. Punit Sakhare, MD Dermatologist · 2026-08-16 · 10 min read

Quick Facts

Typical range in India
Roughly ₹1,500 at the salon end to about ₹13,000 at the top clinic end, based on published price lists current in mid-2026. Most standard clinic sessions sit between ₹4,000 and ₹8,000.
Mumbai specifically
Broadly ₹4,000–₹12,000 across published clinic sources. Mumbai is consistently the most expensive Indian metro for this treatment; Pune and Kolkata have the lowest starting prices.
The single biggest driver
The machine. Devices marketed in India as hydrafacial machines are listed from ₹7,000 to ₹5,50,000 — a roughly 79-fold spread, all sold under the same word.
What a course actually costs

Most common hidden cost

Hydra facial pricing in India makes very little sense from the outside. The same treatment name is advertised at ₹1,500 in a neighbourhood salon and ₹13,000 in a dermatology clinic — an eight-fold difference for something described in almost identical language on both price lists.

That gap is not simply a matter of premises and branding. There are real, identifiable differences underneath it, and most of them are things you can check before you hand over any money. This guide explains what actually drives the price, what each tier realistically buys, and the specific questions that separate the two.

We run a clinic, so read this with that in mind. We have deliberately not named any competitor or reproduced anyone else's price list. The price bands below are aggregated from publicly published Indian clinic and salon price pages current in 2025–26; the equipment figures come from live B2B marketplace listings you can check yourself; and the statements about the branded system are quoted from the trademark owner's own website. Pricing in this category moves quickly, so treat every figure as indicative rather than current.

What the three price tiers actually look like

Published Indian pricing falls into three fairly distinct bands, though they overlap in a way that matters.

Salon and beauty parlour tier — roughly ₹1,500 to ₹3,500. Typically a 30-to-45-minute service, often structured explicitly as a step count: a “4-step” at one price and a “6-step” at a higher one. Note that the sources for this band are mostly from 2023 and 2024 and are the oldest data available, so the real floor today may be somewhat higher.

Chain clinic tier — roughly ₹2,500 to ₹4,000 list price. This tier is the hardest to pin down, because published per-session prices are scarce at this end of the market; several chain price pages list tiers without rupee figures and invite an enquiry instead. Where third-party figures exist they suggest list prices in the high ₹3,000s, with introductory offers running considerably lower. We would treat any of these numbers as approximate.

Medical and dermatology clinic tier — roughly ₹2,500 to ₹13,000. Published clinic prices cluster at ₹4,000–₹8,000 for a standard session and ₹8,000–₹13,000 for top-tier sessions with boosters. Consultation fees, where charged separately, run around ₹1,200–₹1,400.

Here is the part worth pausing on: the tiers overlap heavily between ₹2,500 and ₹4,000. At ₹3,000 you genuinely cannot tell from the price alone whether you are buying a medical-grade treatment at the bottom of a clinic's range or a salon treatment at the top of theirs. Price is not a reliable signal in that band, which is exactly why the questions further down matter.

Mumbai is consistently at or near the top, and by the figures above the lowest floors are in Pune and Kolkata. Within the city, published sources note that Bandra and Juhu tend to run above the quieter suburbs, though nobody has quantified that gap in Mumbai specifically. Where it has been quantified elsewhere — in Bangalore — the differential between prime and non-prime neighbourhoods was 20–30%, and was attributed to commercial rent rather than to any difference in the treatment.

One international aggregator reports substantially lower Indian figures, in the ₹1,500–₹3,800 range. That source appears to be sampling the salon end rather than the clinic end. It is a useful reminder of how low the floor goes, but it is not comparable to the clinic figures above.

The real reason for the spread: the machine

This is the single most useful thing to understand, and it is verifiable in about thirty seconds.

Machines marketed in India under the word “hydrafacial” are listed on open B2B marketplaces at the following prices, live as of July 2026:

  • 7-in-1 unit — ₹7,000
  • Table-top unit — ₹9,999
  • Fully automatic 7-in-1 — ₹11,000
  • 9-in-1 H2O2 unit — ₹19,999
  • 10-in-1 unit — ₹35,000
  • 10-in-1 professional unit — ₹2,50,000
  • Multifunction 10-in-1 — ₹5,50,000

That is a spread of roughly 79 times, every one of them sold in India under the same treatment name.

A ₹7,000 machine costs less than the retail price of two sessions at a mid-range clinic. Once you know that number, the ₹1,500 session stops being mysterious. The equipment is amortised almost immediately, the consumables are generic, and the economics work comfortably at that price.

The trademarked HydraFacial® system sits at the far other end. Its manufacturer, Hydrafacial LLC, states publicly on its own website that “to legally promote your services as a Hydrafacial treatment and order official consumables, every provider must hold a valid trademark licence from Hydrafacial LLC,” and that buying directly from them is the only way to be certain a system meets their standards. They also impose a US$10,000 recertification fee on second-hand devices before licensing is available.

We are quoting the trademark owner describing its own licensing regime — not a competitor's characterisation. It follows that a provider using a genuine branded system will be able to tell you so and show you the device. A provider using a generic machine is offering a hydra facial in the descriptive sense, which may still be a perfectly good treatment, but it is a different product at a different cost base.

Consumables, and the one hygiene question that matters

The branded system uses patented disposable tips — one tip per client, then discarded — and named, sealed, colour-coded solution canisters. Only a licensed provider can order those official consumables at all, which means device licensing and serum authenticity are effectively the same question.

We want to be careful here. We have seen no published inspection finding, regulatory action or study about how tips are handled by any category of provider in India, and we are not going to imply anything about anyone else's practice. What we can say is what the manufacturer says: the genuine system is designed around a single-use tip.

So a reasonable question to ask any provider, including us, is simply: “Is a new tip used for each client?” It is a standard hygiene question of the sort you might ask before any procedure, and any provider should be able to answer it directly.

Steps, duration and the booster problem

A full protocol takes 30 to 45 minutes. Six independent sources converge on that figure. A fifteen-minute “hydra facial” is a different and shorter service, whatever it is called on the menu.

The step count is where most of the pricing confusion originates, and it is worth understanding because the manufacturer's own materials give three different answers.

The device performs three core passes: cleanse and peel, extract and hydrate, then fuse and protect. Those are the three sequential passes of the spiral tip. (The manufacturer's own published clinical trial also describes a three-step protocol, though it counts blue LED as its third step rather than the serum pass — another illustration of how loosely the step count is used.)

The “7-step” version you will see advertised is the three core steps plus four additions — lymphatic drainage, a booster serum, LED light, and a split of the core steps into separately-named items. Those additions correspond almost exactly to the tier ladder:

TierDurationWhat's included
Entry / Signature~30 minThe three core steps only
Mid / Deluxe~45 minCore three + one booster + LED
Top / Platinum~60 minCore three + lymphatic drainage + boosters + LED

The step count tracks the price, not the physiology. Steps one, six and seven of the “seven-step” protocol are precisely the items you pay extra for. Across published Indian price lists, the bill roughly doubles from entry tier to top tier — ₹2,499 to ₹6,999 at one clinic, ₹6,000 to ₹12,500 at another, ₹2,999 to ₹5,000 at a third.

Whether the add-ons are worth that doubling is a separate question, and the evidence is not flattering. Blue LED for acne showed no statistically significant benefit on pooled lesion counts across a meta-analysis of fourteen randomised trials covering 698 participants, though a minority of those trials did favour it on global-improvement scores; the authors concluded the evidence is too weak to judge. Red LED does have a positive trial behind it — but that trial required thirty sessions, twice weekly. Five to ten minutes of LED once every four to six weeks amounts to roughly a tenth of that exposure over the same period, so evidence for the intensive course does not transfer to the add-on. For facial lymphatic drainage in cosmetic rejuvenation, there is no indexed clinical evidence at all.

We cover which parts of the protocol are supported and which are upsells in more detail in our step-by-step breakdown of the treatment.

Packages: where the real cost is decided

A single hydra facial is a cosmetic event. A clinical course is 4 to 6 sessions, three to four weeks apart, followed by maintenance. That means the package price, not the single-session price, is the number that actually determines what this treatment costs you.

Published Indian package discounting is remarkably inconsistent:

  • ₹6,000 → ₹4,800 per session at three sessions — 20% off
  • ₹6,000 → ₹4,000 per session at six sessions — 33% off
  • “Buy 3, get 1 free” — 25% off
  • “6 sessions for the price of 4” — 33% off
  • ₹9,499 single → ₹26,999 for three — about 5% off

A consumer who assumes a package means a meaningful saving can be wrong by a factor of six. Ask for the per-session price inside the package in rupees, not as a percentage, and compare that number across clinics rather than comparing headline single-session prices.

Also ask whether the package expires, and whether it is refundable or transferable if you stop partway. Those terms vary widely and are rarely volunteered.

The questions to ask before you book

All of these are answerable on a phone call, and each one detects something specific.

About the device

  1. What is the make and model of the machine, and who manufactures it? An answer like “a 10-in-1 hydra machine” identifies the generic category immediately.
  2. Are you a licensed provider for that system, and can I see the certification?
  3. Can I see the machine before we start?

About consumables

  1. Will a new sealed tip be opened in front of me?
  2. Can I see the serum containers and their labels before they go into the machine?
  3. Which booster are you using for my skin, and why that one? A provider who cannot name it is unlikely to be stocking genuine ones.

About the protocol

  1. How many steps am I getting, and how long is the appointment? Under 30 minutes is a shorter service.
  2. Is LED, booster or lymphatic drainage included at this price, or extra? This is the most common reason a bill exceeds a quote.

About who treats you

  1. Who performs the treatment — a doctor, a trained therapist, or a beautician — and are they certified on this device?
  2. Is a doctor on the premises during treatment?

About money

  1. Is the quoted price inclusive of GST?
  2. Is the consultation fee separate, and is it adjusted against treatment?
  3. What is the per-session price at three or six sessions, in rupees?
  4. Does the package expire, and is it refundable or transferable?

On GST specifically: salon and wellness services were reduced from 18% to 5% with effect from 22 September 2025, but aesthetic and cosmetic procedures are not exempt healthcare and may be classified differently. On a ₹8,000 treatment the difference between 5% and 18% is ₹1,040, so it is worth asking whether the quoted figure includes it.

Our own hydra facial pricing is published in full on our Mumbai cost page and our master price list, with what is included at each tier stated up front rather than revealed at the counter.

The reason for writing this guide the way we have is straightforward. A patient who arrives having asked these fourteen questions is a better patient to treat — they know what they are buying, they have realistic expectations about what the treatment does, and they are not going to be surprised by the bill. Our evidence review of what hydra facial actually delivers is written in the same spirit, including the parts where the evidence does not support the marketing.

If you are comparing us against somewhere cheaper, the honest advice is: don't compare the prices. Compare the answers to questions one, four and seven. If those answers match and the price is lower, take the lower price.
