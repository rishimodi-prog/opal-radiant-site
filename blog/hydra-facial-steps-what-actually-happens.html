Hydra Facial Steps: What Actually Happens, and What You're Paying Extra For

By Dr. Punit Sakhare, MD Dermatologist · 2026-08-17 · 9 min read

Quick Facts

How many steps, really
Three passes of the spiral tip: cleanse and peel, extract and hydrate, fuse and protect. That is what the device physically does. (Confusingly, the manufacturer's own published trial also says three steps but counts blue LED as the third instead of the serum pass.)
Why you've seen 5 and 7
The manufacturer publishes three different step counts on three different pages. The higher counts split the core steps into separately-named items and add optional therapies.
What the extra steps are
Lymphatic drainage, a booster serum and LED light. These map exactly onto the tier ladder — they are the items that distinguish the entry tier from the top tier.
Total duration
About 30 minutes for the core three steps, 45 minutes with a booster and LED, and around 60 minutes for the full menu.
The acid ladder
The peel solution comes in three strengths — 7.5%, 15% and 30% glycolic acid. The strongest is designated physician-only by the manufacturer. This is where the risk sits, not in the suction.
What you feel
Most people describe it as comfortable rather than painful — a cool wet vacuum gliding over the skin. Transient stinging or tingling was reported by about 40% of participants in the one study that measured it. Extraction is suction, not squeezing.

If you have researched this treatment at all, you have probably come across three different answers to a simple question. Some pages describe a three-step protocol. Others describe five. The most commonly advertised version has seven.

Here is the thing that resolves it: all three step counts come from the manufacturer. Their North American site describes seven therapies. Their EMEA site describes five steps. And the peer-reviewed clinical trial their own staff co-authored describes three. Nobody is being dishonest — they are describing different product tiers — but the effect on a consumer trying to compare two clinics is genuine confusion.

This article sets out what the machine physically does, in order, with what solutions, for how long. Then it separates the steps that are doing the work from the steps you are paying extra for.

The three core steps: what the device actually does

Strip away the naming and the tiering, and a hydra facial is three sequential passes of a handpiece fitted with a spiral tip. The tip simultaneously delivers a solution onto the skin and vacuums it away, carrying loosened debris with it. That simultaneous delivery-and-extraction is the mechanism that distinguishes this from ordinary microdermabrasion.

Step 1 — Cleanse and peel. The first pass combines gentle mechanical exfoliation with a chemical exfoliant. Two solutions are typically used in sequence: a lactic-acid-based cleansing solution containing glucosamine and algae extract, at a mild pH of around 4.25–4.50, followed by a glycolic and salicylic acid peel.

That peel comes in three strengths, and this is the most important technical detail in the whole protocol:

SolutionConcentrationpHUsed for
Prep7.5% glycolic + 2% salicylic3.0–3.5Entry strength, first sessions, sensitive skin
Standard15% glycolic + 1.5% salicylic3.0–3.3Standard treatment strength
MD30% glycolic + 1.5% salicylic3.0–3.3Physician-only per the manufacturer

Most consumer articles say “glycolic and salicylic acid” and stop there. The strength is what determines both how much you see and how much you risk — and it is a variable your clinician chooses, not one the machine decides.

Step 2 — Extract and hydrate. The second pass uses focused suction to draw out the contents of clogged pores, paired with a salicylic-acid-based solution containing honey and willow bark extract. This is where blackheads and whiteheads come out. Crucially, it is suction rather than manual squeezing, which is why extraction during a hydra facial is comfortable in a way that traditional extraction is not.

Step 3 — Fuse and protect. The final pass switches to a non-abrasive tip that delivers an antioxidant and hyaluronic acid serum without further exfoliation. Two versions exist: a peptide-containing formulation at pH 6.0–7.0, and a gentler vitamin-A-and-E formulation at pH 4.2–4.7 which the manufacturer designates for especially sensitive skin.

That is the treatment. Everything else is an addition.

How three steps became seven

The seven-step version does three things to the list.

First, it splits step one into two — “microdermabrasion” and “customised peel” become separate line items, though they are the same pass.

Second, it splits step three into two — “target skin concerns” (the booster) and “hydration infusion” (the serum).

Third, it adds two therapies that are not part of the spiral-tip sequence: manual lymphatic drainage at the start, and LED light therapy at the end. (LED is delivered by the same platform on current systems, but it is a separate modality from the vacuum-and-serum passes, and it is priced separately.)

Tellingly, the seven-step page omits the classic step names entirely — because “cleanse and peel” cannot be counted as two items while still being called one thing.

Now compare that against how the treatment is sold:

TierDurationContents
Signature / Classic~30 minThe three core steps. No LED, no booster, no lymphatic drainage.
Deluxe~45 minCore three, plus one booster and LED.
Platinum~60 minCore three, plus lymphatic drainage, boosters and LED.

Steps one, six and seven of the seven-step protocol are exactly the items that separate the cheapest tier from the most expensive one. The step count is a pricing structure, not a physiological sequence. If you are comparing a “7-step hydra facial” at one clinic against a “3-step” at another, you may well be comparing the same core treatment with different add-ons attached.

That has a direct bearing on cost, which we cover in our guide to hydra facial pricing in India — across published Indian price lists, the bill roughly doubles from entry tier to top tier.

Which steps have evidence behind them

Worth grading honestly, because the answer is not uniform.

The exfoliation-plus-suction combination (steps 1 and 2): supported. An independent randomised split-face trial applied a 4% alpha-hydroxy acid to one side of 23 participants' faces, and the same acid combined with negative pressure to the other. The suction side showed significantly reduced blackheads and whiteheads at one, two and four weeks; the acid-alone side showed only a non-significant trend. The two sides differed significantly from each other at week one (Kim et al., 2015). This is the cleanest demonstration that the vacuum does work the solution alone does not.

The serum infusion step (step 3): supported, thinly. The one study isolating the device randomised twenty women to either hydradermabrasion with an antioxidant serum, or the same serum applied by hand. The device group showed significantly increased epidermal thickness, dermal thickness and measured skin antioxidant levels. The hand-applied group showed no change in any measure (Freedman, 2008). One study, twenty people, eighteen years ago — but a properly randomised design.

LED light: weak to null. Blue LED for acne was assessed in a systematic review and meta-analysis of fourteen randomised trials covering 698 participants. Pooled lesion counts showed no statistically significant benefit for inflammatory (p=0.78) or non-inflammatory lesions (p=0.11). To be fair, three of the five trials reporting global improvement did favour blue light; the authors' conclusion was that the evidence is too weak and too biased to draw a verdict either way (Scott et al., 2019).

Red LED for skin rejuvenation does have a positive randomised trial — 136 participants, significant improvement in roughness and measured collagen density (Wunsch & Matuschka, 2014). But that result required thirty sessions, twice weekly. A hydra facial delivers five to ten minutes of LED once every four to six weeks — over the same fifteen weeks, roughly a tenth of the exposure in that trial. Evidence for a full LED course does not transfer to LED as a facial add-on, and clinics routinely cite the former while selling the latter.

Lymphatic drainage: no evidence. Searches for manual lymphatic drainage in facial cosmetic rejuvenation return no indexed clinical results. That is absent evidence rather than weak evidence. Lymphatic drainage has a genuine evidence base in post-surgical lymphoedema — an entirely different clinical problem.

Boosters: unevaluated. No published study compares one booster against another, or a booster against no booster, on this platform. The one positive booster study was run by a manufacturer on its own serum and its own competing device, with three of its five authors employed by that manufacturer and the other two by the contract research organisation it engaged (Huang et al., 2024).

None of this means the add-ons are worthless — a pleasant treatment is worth something, and absence of evidence is not proof of absence. But it does mean that when a clinic justifies a doubled price by pointing to LED and lymphatic drainage, the justification is not coming from the literature.

What it actually feels like, step by step

The experience is fairly consistently described, and it is one of the treatment's genuine strengths.

During steps 1 and 2 you feel a cool, wet sensation and a light tugging as the vacuum passes over your skin. Most people describe it as comfortable rather than painful. That said, the one study that formally measured tolerability recorded transient stinging, tingling or itching in roughly 40% of its twenty participants — mostly mild, with one severe tingling event — and mild-to-moderate redness in most of them at some point (Storgard et al., 2022). Comfortable is accurate; painless overstates it. The clinician stretches the skin gently between two fingers and glides the wand in overlapping passes — forehead, cheeks, chin, around the mouth, then the nose, where a small twisting motion is used around the nostrils.

Extraction, which people often dread, is the part most patients are pleasantly surprised by. Because the pores are drawn out by suction rather than pressed out by fingers, there is no pinching and no sharp pain.

During step 3 there is essentially no sensation beyond the coolness of the serum.

Immediately afterwards, expect a flush. Imaging has measured blood-vessel diameter roughly doubling straight after treatment — that vasodilation is literally what the post-treatment glow is. On gentle settings there is often no visible redness at all. On more aggressive settings, visible erythema lasting roughly two to five days, and occasionally bruising, is documented.

In the following days, some people see a few small blemishes surface — a consequence of extraction bringing congestion to the surface, not a reaction to the treatment. It settles.

Longevity: imaging shows stratum corneum thickness and dermal appearance back at baseline by two weeks, with blood flow still partly elevated and the surface-smoothing effect largely holding. That is why maintenance is recommended every four to six weeks — the effect is genuinely transient, not a schedule invented to sell sessions. What the treatment does and does not achieve is covered in our evidence review of hydra facial benefits.

How the protocol should change for Indian skin

This is where a generic step-by-step becomes actively unhelpful, because the standard protocol was not developed on skin like most of ours.

The risk in this treatment sits in the chemical step, not the suction. A retrospective review of 473 superficial chemical peel treatments in Fitzpatrick types III to VI found an overall complication rate of 3.8% — genuinely reassuring — but Fitzpatrick type VI carried five-fold higher odds of an adverse event (OR 5.14, 95% CI 1.21–21.8 — a wide interval, so the direction is clearer than the magnitude). The most frequent complication overall was crusting at 2.3%, with post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation and erythema tied at 1.9%, and all side effects resolved within eight months (Vemula et al., 2018).

Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation is driven by inflammation, and imaging has shown directly that more aggressive settings produce measurably more of it — dilated vessels, spongiosis, inflammatory cells visible on confocal imaging. In deeper skin, the “more aggressive equals better results” trade is a worse bargain than in lighter skin, because the inflammation you accept is the route by which pigmentation arrives.

What that means in practice:

  • Start at the 7.5% prep strength, not the 15% standard, on a first session, and escalate across a course rather than opening at full strength.
  • Gentle tip, single pass, low suction to begin with. Tolerance is built, not assumed.
  • The gentler serum formulation is the sensible default for reactive skin — the manufacturer's own materials designate it for that purpose.
  • Salicylic acid is the safer active in pigmented skin — it is lipophilic and stays epidermally limited, and it is well established in Indian dermatology practice.
  • Strict photoprotection afterwards is not optional. The same review found complications were significantly less common in winter, which in Mumbai's UV levels translates directly into how carefully you shield your face for the following week.
  • Active melasma is not an indication for this treatment. A randomised study of melasma treatment in Fitzpatrick III–IV skin recorded post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation in 28% of patients, resolving in all but one by twelve weeks (Hong et al., 2012). That study used a fractional laser and a 15% TCA peel — both considerably deeper than a hydra facial — so it is an indication of how reactive melasma-prone skin is, not a direct read-across. Melasma has its own treatment pathway.

Screening before a first session should also cover recent isotretinoin use, active inflammatory acne, eczema, an active rosacea flare, recent lasers or injectables, and sunburn. We cover contraindications in full in our article on who should avoid this treatment.

The short version

Three steps do the work: exfoliate, extract, infuse. Everything beyond that is optional, priced separately, and supported by markedly less evidence than the core protocol.

If you are choosing between tiers, the honest guidance is that the entry tier contains the entire evidence-backed treatment. The upgrades buy you a longer, more pleasant appointment and add-ons whose clinical case is weak. That may still be worth it to you — an hour of being looked after is not nothing — but it should be a choice you make knowingly rather than one made for you by a step count on a menu.

And if you have Fitzpatrick IV to VI skin, the variable worth asking about is not the number of steps. It is the acid strength.
