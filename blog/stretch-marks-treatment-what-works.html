Stretch Marks Treatment: What Works, What Doesn't, and the Honest Ceiling

By Dr. Punit Sakhare, MD Dermatologist · 2026-09-06 · 12 min read

Quick Facts

The short answer
No treatment removes stretch marks. Published trials report partial improvement in texture and appearance only, and Cochrane's plain-language summary states stretch marks “do not disappear entirely.”
How common they are

Laser versus microneedling

The number that matters in Indian skin

What reliably improves

Stretch marks treatment is one of the few areas in aesthetic dermatology where the honest summary can be written in a single line: nothing has been shown to prevent them, nothing removes them, and the treatments that do help appear to help by roughly the same modest amount as each other. A 2026 PRISMA-guided systematic review by Wu and Wang, searching PubMed, Web of Science and Scopus for studies published between 2010 and 2025, put the estimated incidence of striae distensae at 56% and described them as arising from mechanical tension, hormonal imbalance and inflammation-induced disruption of the dermal matrix. More than half of people get them, and they are not caused by a failure of skincare.

That is an uncomfortable starting point for a clinic to publish, so it is worth being precise about where the evidence is strong and where it is thin. The strongest finding in this field is a negative one, from Cochrane. The second strongest is that the major device options perform the same as one another. The third, and the most important one for anyone treated in Mumbai, is that the choice between those devices should be driven almost entirely by pigmentary risk in deeper skin rather than by claimed efficacy. This article works through each, with the sample sizes, confidence intervals and design flaws stated in the body rather than hidden in a footnote.

What Stretch Marks Are, and Why Rubra Versus Alba Decides Everything

Stretch marks, or striae distensae, exist in two stages, and the stage matters more than the choice of machine. Striae rubra are the early lesions: red to violaceous, sometimes slightly raised, still carrying an inflammatory infiltrate and an active vascular component. Striae alba are the mature end-state: pale, atrophic, slightly wrinkled, with reduced and reorganised collagen and elastin. Histologically, a striae alba is a scar.

That distinction sets the ceiling on what treatment can achieve. In striae rubra there is a vascular and inflammatory target, and remodelling treatments have partly intact tissue to work with. In striae alba the problem is doubled: a treatment has to induce new collagen inside established scar tissue and, separately, restore pigment to skin that has lost it. The second problem has no reliable solution.

Every comparative trial of striae alba discussed below reports partial improvement. Not one reports resolution. The Cochrane review's own plain-language summary states that stretch marks “do not disappear entirely.” Wu and Wang's review lists alpha hydroxy acids, 1540 nm Er:Glass, 532 nm Nd:YAG, picosecond lasers, intense pulsed light and combination protocols among the options with reported efficacy, and concludes that management is “evolving towards individualized combination strategies”. That review has a declared focus on evaluating 1565 nm non-ablative fractional laser, which shapes its emphasis.

The practical consequence is that timing is the one variable a patient controls.

Prevention: The Strongest Negative Finding in This Field

Prevention of stretch marks in pregnancy is the best-studied question here, and the answer is a clear no. Brennan, Young and Devane's Cochrane systematic review pooled 6 randomised and quasi-randomised trials covering 800 women, with three of the six judged at low risk of bias. Conflicts of interest were declared as “none known”.

The results were uniformly non-significant. For the development of stretch marks, topical actives versus placebo or no treatment gave an average risk ratio of 0.74 (95% CI 0.53 to 1.03) across 5 trials and 474 women, with substantial heterogeneity (I² = 65%). Excluding the higher-risk-of-bias trials moved it further towards no effect: RR 0.81 (95% CI 0.60 to 1.10). For severity, the pooled standardised mean difference was −0.31 (95% CI −1.06 to 0.44) across 2 trials and 255 women. Every interval crosses the line of no effect.

The preparations tested were Alphastria, Trofolastin, Verum, olive oil and cocoa butter, all containing vitamin E. The two household remedies that dominate Indian antenatal advice were specifically tested and specifically failed. The authors' conclusion is worth quoting verbatim: “We found no high-quality evidence to support the use of any of the topical preparations in the prevention of stretch marks during pregnancy.”

One later trial adds nuance rather than contradiction. Hajhashemi and colleagues randomised 160 nulliparous women in a double-blind design to aloe vera gel, sweet almond oil, base cream or no treatment. Aloe vera and sweet almond oil significantly reduced itching and erythema and limited the spread of existing striae (p < 0.05). But on the outcomes patients care about, the diameter and number of stretch marks, all three creams performed the same (p > 0.05). Emollients relieve the itch of stretching skin; they do not stop the marks forming.

Topical Tretinoin for Early, Red Stretch Marks

Topical tretinoin is the most commonly recommended prescription option for striae rubra, and the evidence behind it is thinner than its reputation. The most quantified study is Rangel and colleagues' open-label, uncontrolled, multicentre study in 20 women, who applied tretinoin 0.1% cream daily for three months to pregnancy-related abdominal striae, with one preselected target lesion rated on a six-point scale.

The finding was a significant global improvement at week 12, with the target lesion decreasing in length by 20% (p = 0.01). That is the honest headline number for topical retinoid in stretch marks: a fifth off the length of one chosen lesion, measured without a control group, without blinding, in twenty people. Irritation was common, with erythema and scaling in 11 of the 20 patients (55%), easing after the first month.

There is a safety point here that is also a prescribing point. Topical tretinoin is contraindicated in pregnancy and is not recommended during breastfeeding, and the population most motivated to treat striae rubra is postpartum women, many of them lactating. Retinoid treatment for stretch marks should not be started without screening for pregnancy and lactation first.

Alpha hydroxy acids appear in Wu and Wang's list of options with reported efficacy, and in-clinic chemical peel protocols are sometimes used on early striae for the same superficial remodelling rationale. The controlled data for peels in striae specifically is weaker than for retinoid, and in deeper skin the pigmentary caution below applies to peels as much as to devices.

Stretch Marks Treatment for Striae Alba: Laser, Microneedling and Radiofrequency

For mature white stretch marks, three device categories dominate practice: ablative fractional CO2 laser, microneedling and microneedle radiofrequency, and non-ablative fractional laser. Two independent meta-analyses and a randomised split-body trial agree that they work about equally well.

Mustafa and colleagues' 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis pooled 6 RCTs and 166 patients, with Cochrane risk-of-bias assessment. Both modalities benefited “over 70% of patients”. Comparing fractional CO2 against microneedling, clinical improvement gave RR 0.97 (95% CI 0.74 to 1.28), p = 0.85; satisfaction gave RR 0.91 (95% CI 0.52 to 1.58); and cross-sectional area of the largest striae gave a mean difference of 0.22 (p = 0.24). Three outcomes, three null results.

Aktoz and Yilmaz's 2024 meta-analysis asked the same question of fractional microneedle radiofrequency versus fractional CO2 laser and found the same thing: professional assessment of improvement gave OR 1.27 (95% CI 0.49 to 3.31), and patient self-assessment gave OR 0.72 (95% CI 0.13 to 3.90). Neither was statistically significant.

The best-designed single trial agrees. Naspolini and colleagues ran a randomised split-abdomen study in 20 women with Fitzpatrick skin types III or IV, each patient her own control, comparing a 1340 nm Nd:YAP non-ablative fractional laser against microneedling over five monthly sessions, with biopsies. Patients reported improvement with both and no statistically significant difference between them. What separated the two was tolerability: pain on a visual analogue scale was 5.23 for microneedling versus 2.39 for the laser (p < 0.001), while adverse events lasted slightly longer after the laser (4.03 versus 3.0 days, p = 0.02).

If efficacy is a tie, the decision has to be made on something else. In Indian skin, that something else is pigment.

The Pigmentation Problem in Deeper Indian Skin

Striae treatment is one of the few areas of body contouring with useful skin-of-colour data, and the signal is consistent: ablative fractional CO2 laser carries materially more post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation risk than microneedling or microneedle radiofrequency. That should drive device selection in a Mumbai clinic, where most patients are Fitzpatrick IV to VI.

Mustafa's meta-analysis found post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation significantly more frequent with CO2 laser than with microneedling: RR 8.37 (95% CI 1.42 to 49.44), p = 0.02. That confidence interval is very wide, so the honest reading is “several-fold higher”, not “eight times higher”. The authors concluded that microneedling “emerges as the safer option due to its lower risk of PIH”. Aktoz reached the same place from the other direction: PIH was significantly less frequent with fractional microneedle radiofrequency, OR 0.24 (95% CI 0.08 to 0.70).

Two primary studies in deeper skin put absolute numbers on it. Madegowda and colleagues, at a tertiary care centre in Karnataka, treated 24 Indian patients with bilateral striae using fractional CO2 laser on both sides across three monthly sessions, following them for six months. Hyperpigmentation was the most common side effect, occurring in 19 of 24 patients (79%), on both sides. It resolved spontaneously by six months, which matters, but 79% is not a rare event.

Listiawan and colleagues studied 22 patients, 11 per group, exclusively in Fitzpatrick skin types IV and V, comparing tretinoin 0.1% cream against combined fractional microneedle radiofrequency plus fractional CO2, with biopsies. Their verbatim finding was that “the side effect experienced by all combination group subjects was postinflammatory hyperpigmentation”, in other words 100% PIH in the energy-device arm. Two patients in the tretinoin group experienced irritation. The study was small and not randomised, but it is the only one conducted solely in skin types comparable to most Indian patients. If you are considering ablative laser for stretch marks in deeper skin, the pigmentation risk deserves more consultation time than the efficacy discussion.

Platelet-Rich Plasma: What the Systematic Review Actually Found

Platelet-rich plasma is widely offered as an add-on to microneedling or laser, and the quality assessment of that practice is blunt. Sawetz and colleagues ran a PRISMA systematic review with two independent reviewers, finding 12 studies of PRP for striae distensae, of which 6 met inclusion criteria.

Of those six, two had no control group at all, only two used intra-individual comparison, and none of the six used validated outcome scores or patient-reported outcome measures. All six reported clinical and subjective improvement, which is exactly what uncontrolled studies of a desired treatment reliably report. The authors' conclusion, verbatim: “The current literature evidence supporting the use of platelet-rich plasma for striae distensae is poor.”

The histological findings across those studies are more interesting than the clinical ones: increased epidermal thickness, rete ridge formation, collagen and elastin formation, and reduced inflammatory cell infiltrate. The mechanism is plausible. What is missing is a trial designed well enough to show whether it translates into a visible difference.

The Indian split-body study gives a sense of the size of any added effect. In Madegowda's 24 patients, fractional CO2 alone produced 25% moderate, 56% good and 16.6% excellent responses; adding PRP to the left side produced 20.8% moderate, 45.8% good and 33.3% excellent. That is a shift towards the top grade, but the study was not randomised, not blinded, had no untreated control and used subjective grading, so it cannot settle the question. PRP is a reasonable adjunct to discuss, not an evidence-backed upgrade.

What the Evidence Does Not Support

Several claims are routine in stretch mark marketing and are contradicted by the studies that looked.

  • “This cream or oil prevents stretch marks in pregnancy.” Cochrane's 6 trials in 800 women found RR 0.74 (95% CI 0.53 to 1.03) for development and SMD −0.31 (95% CI −1.06 to 0.44) for severity, neither significant. Olive oil and cocoa butter were tested directly and did not work.
  • “Laser removes stretch marks.” No published study reports elimination of striae. Outcomes here are quartile grades, aesthetic improvement scores, scar scale points and percentage changes in length or area.
  • “Our laser is the most effective option.” Two meta-analyses and a randomised split-body trial all found no significant efficacy difference between fractional CO2, microneedling and microneedle radiofrequency (RR 0.97; OR 1.27; no significant between-group difference in Naspolini's biopsy-controlled trial).
  • “It is safe for all skin types.” PIH was several-fold more frequent with CO2 laser than microneedling (RR 8.37, 95% CI 1.42 to 49.44), affected 79% of patients in the Indian series, and affected 100% of the energy-device group in the only Fitzpatrick IV–V study available.
  • “PRP dramatically improves results.” Sawetz's systematic review found six studies, two with no control group, and none using a validated outcome measure, concluding the evidence is “poor”.
  • “X sessions will clear them.” No trial establishes an optimal session count. Published protocols run three to five sessions at four- to six-week intervals because that is what the investigators chose, not because it was compared against anything else.

Before-and-after photographs deserve the same scepticism. Response grades in the Indian series ranged from moderate to excellent under one protocol, so no single image is a typical outcome.

What Improvement Actually Looks Like

If treatment does not remove stretch marks, it is fair to ask what patients are paying for. The published answer is more specific than the marketing version.

Something measurable happens in the dermis. In Naspolini's split-abdomen trial, biopsies after the third and fifth sessions showed collagen and elastic fibres increased significantly (p < 0.01), with no significant difference between the laser and microneedling sides. That is histological confirmation, independent of how anyone rated the photographs.

Quality of life improves substantially, even while the marks remain visible. In the same trial, Dermatology Life Quality Index scores fell from 8.4 at baseline to 3.17 after three sessions and 2.64 after five (p < 0.001). That is a large shift on a validated instrument, in a cohort whose stretch marks were still there at the end.

The response distribution is wide. In Mustafa's pooled RCTs, “over 70% of patients” benefited from either modality, which is another way of saying close to 30% did not.

Put together: expect a softening of texture, some narrowing and fading, an improvement in how the area looks in ordinary light, and continued visibility of the marks on close inspection.

How to Decide, and What to Ask Before Starting

Because efficacy between the main options is statistically indistinguishable, the decision in Indian skin comes down to three practical variables rather than to which device a clinic owns.

Stage. Red, recent marks respond better than white, atrophic ones, so if marks are still red that is the moment to act. Topical retinoid has the only quantified single-agent result in that stage, and pregnancy and breastfeeding must be excluded first.

Pigmentary risk. In Fitzpatrick IV to VI skin, microneedling and microneedle radiofrequency carry a significantly lower post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation risk than ablative fractional CO2, at equivalent efficacy, supported by two meta-analyses and two primary studies in deeper skin. A test area, conservative settings, strict photoprotection and a plan for managing pigmentation if it occurs all belong in the consent conversation.

Tolerance. Microneedling hurt more than twice as much as non-ablative fractional laser on a visual analogue scale (5.23 versus 2.39, p < 0.001), while adverse events after the laser lasted about a day longer. Both should be described accurately rather than as “no downtime”.

Reasonable questions to ask at a consultation: which stage are my marks in, what is the PIH risk for my skin type with the device proposed, how many sessions before we reassess, and what will we do if pigmentation develops. A clinician who answers with ranges and caveats is describing the literature accurately.
