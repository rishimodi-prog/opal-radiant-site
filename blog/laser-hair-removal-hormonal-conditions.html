Laser Hair Removal

Laser Hair Removal, Hormonal Imbalance and What to Check First

By Dr. Punit Sakhare, MD Dermatologist · 2026-10-22 · 12 min read

Quick Facts

Reduction in PCOS after six sessions
31% ± 38% mean hair count reduction (P = 0.001) in 60 women treated with alexandrite — McGill 2007. The ±38 standard deviation matters as much as the mean.
Hair-free interval, six vs ten sessions
1.9 weeks after six treatments, 4.3 weeks after ten (P = 0.001). A mean of twelve treatments got 31% of patients past a six-week interval.
PCOS vs idiopathic hirsutism
Greater than 75% reduction in 70% of idiopathic hirsutism patients versus 54% of PCOS patients (n=100, Nd:YAG); results persisted at three months only in the idiopathic group.
Paradoxical hypertrichosis risk with PCOS
33.3% in women with PCOS versus 14.1% without (P < 0.001) in a prospective facial alexandrite study with 16.2% overall prevalence.
Long-term evidence base
Five randomised trials and 223 patients in total have followed laser hair removal for at least one complete hair cycle at the treated site.
Studies of laser hair removal in thyroid disease
None. No published study has examined outcomes specifically in thyroid disease, so the association is mechanistic reasoning only.

People searching laser hair removal hormonal imbalance are almost never at the start of a course. They are usually six or eight sessions in, on the chin or the upper lip, watching hair reappear within two weeks of a session that was supposed to have thinned it. The instinct is to blame the machine, the operator or the clinic. The published data points somewhere else: when an untreated endocrine driver is present, laser hair removal measurably under-delivers, and it does so in a way that is consistent across three separate studies on three continents.

This matters more in India than in the populations most laser trials recruited from. Hormonally driven hair is predominantly facial, facial hair is the site with the poorest published long-term results, and Fitzpatrick IV–VI skin narrows the safe energy window at exactly the point where more energy is being asked for. What follows is what the trials measured, where the evidence runs out, and what is worth investigating before you commit to a course of laser hair removal.

Laser and hormones act on two different parts of the same problem

A hair-removal laser and a hormonal driver are working on different targets, which is why one does not cancel the other. The laser heats melanin inside the hair shaft and follicular epithelium and damages the follicles it reaches during that session. It does nothing to the androgen signalling that converts fine vellus hair into coarse pigmented terminal hair in the first place. Where that signal continues, follicles the laser never treated — because they were not producing terminal hair on the day of treatment — keep being recruited. The area looks like it has regrown when part of what you are seeing is new terminal hair that did not exist at session one.

That framing is mechanistic reasoning, not a trial result, and it should be labelled as such. What is a trial result is how thin the underlying evidence base is even in people without a hormonal condition. The Cochrane review of laser and photoepilation by Haedersdal and Gøtzsche pooled 11 randomised trials and 444 patients, reported that none of which were of high methodological quality, and concluded that long-term hair removal was not documented with any treatment. Roughly 50% reduction with alexandrite and diode up to six months was the ceiling it could confirm. That is the baseline against which hormonal cases are judged.

Laser hair removal, hormonal imbalance and what three studies actually found

Three independent studies have compared laser hair removal outcomes in women with a hormonal driver against women without one, and all three point the same way: roughly half the response, and faster relapse.

The most quoted is McGill and colleagues at Glasgow Royal Infirmary, who followed 60 women with polycystic ovary syndrome through a course of 3 ms alexandrite. After six treatments, mean hair count reduction was 31% ± 38% (P = 0.001) — against the 50–90% typically reported in unselected groups. The standard deviation of 38 percentage points is as informative as the mean: PCOS response is enormously variable, and an average tells an individual patient very little. Hair-free interval after six treatments was 1.9 weeks, rising to 4.3 weeks after ten (P = 0.001). After a mean of twelve treatments, 31% of patients had a hair-free interval longer than six weeks, compared with 2.6% after six treatments (P = 0.003). Note the design honestly: this was prospective but uncontrolled, single-centre, and there was no comparison group without PCOS.

Nabi and colleagues at Government Medical College, Srinagar, supplied that missing comparison. They treated 100 women — 50 with idiopathic hirsutism, 50 with PCOS — with long-pulsed 1064 nm Nd:YAG over six sessions four weeks apart. An excellent response, meaning greater than 75% reduction, occurred in 70% of the idiopathic group versus 54% of the PCOS group. More telling than the gap in response was the gap in durability: at three-month follow-up, results persisted in the idiopathic group but not in the PCOS group, confirmed both clinically and on trichoscopy. The study was prospective and comparative but not randomised, and the authors themselves flag the short three-month follow-up.

Lachure and colleagues at IMS & SUM Hospital, Bhubaneswar, split by biochemistry rather than diagnosis, comparing women with normal versus deranged DHEAS and DHT on Fitzpatrick IV–V skin treated with diode laser. At the second follow-up, response was rated good in 56.3% of the normal-hormone group versus 11.1% of the deranged-hormone group. The caveat is substantial: 50 women were enrolled and only 34 were analysed, which is heavy attrition for a study this size. Their conclusion was that diode laser works irrespective of hormone level but is more efficacious where hormones are normal — a distinction worth keeping.

None of the three is randomised or controlled in the strict sense, so treat the exact percentages as indicative. The direction — replicated in Scotland, Kashmir and Odisha, across alexandrite, Nd:YAG and diode — is what carries weight.

PCOS roughly doubles the reported rate of paradoxical hypertrichosis

Paradoxical hypertrichosis — increased hair growth in or around a treated area after laser or light epilation — is the risk most consistently missing from consent conversations, and hormonal patients sit at the high end of it. Qeyam and colleagues ran a prospective study of facial alexandrite treatment at King Abdullah University Hospital and found overall prevalence of 16.2%. Within that cohort, 33.3% of women with PCOS developed it versus 14.1% without (P < 0.001); irregular menstrual cycles predicted it at 33.3% versus 12.6% (P = 0.001), and a family history of hirsutism at 27.8% versus 13.8%. Session count mattered too — 30.9% among those who had 6–10 sessions versus 6.8% with fewer. Regular sunblock use was protective: 12.1% versus 36.1% (P = 0.001).

Those figures sit far above the classic estimates, and the disagreement is instructive rather than disqualifying. Inoue and colleagues reviewed 7,381 patients retrospectively and found paradoxical hypertrichosis in just 25 (0.34%) — but 24 of those 25 had been treated with alexandrite (P < 0.01), despite alexandrite accounting for only 70% of the caseload. Daily sun protection independently reduced risk there too (OR 0.41, P < 0.05). A meta-analysis by Snast and colleagues covering 9,733 patients across 22 studies put pooled prevalence at 3% (95% CI 1–6) — with I² = 97%, extreme heterogeneity that means the pooled figure is not a reliable population rate. It found the effect concentrated on face and neck, at only 0.08% of non-facial sites, and found treatment modality made no difference — which directly contradicts Inoue.

The honest published range is 0.34% to 16.2%, roughly a forty-fold spread, and prospective studies with active surveillance consistently find higher rates than retrospective chart reviews. In Indian data, Pai and colleagues recorded paradoxical hypertrichosis in 5 of 40 women (12.5%). If you have PCOS, irregular cycles or a family history of hirsutism, this belongs in your consent discussion in numbers, not as a footnote.

What to do first: have the hormonal driver investigated, not just the hair treated

The single most useful finding for anyone whose laser course is stalling comes from a 2024 systematic review in JAMA Dermatology by Tan and colleagues, covering 6 studies and 423 patients with PCOS — four randomised trials and two cohort studies. Heterogeneity was severe enough that the authors could not pool the data into a meta-analysis, which is itself a statement about the field. What they could report is that combination management outperformed laser alone: diode laser plus metformin, and diode laser plus a combined oral contraceptive pill, were each superior to diode laser by itself, and IPL plus metformin was superior to IPL alone. Alexandrite showed significant improvement in hirsutism severity and in psychological outcomes, particularly at higher fluence, and outperformed IPL.

The practical sequence is not complicated. If facial or body hair has changed pattern, thickness or speed of regrowth, particularly alongside irregular periods, acne, scalp thinning or weight change, the endocrine assessment should happen before or alongside the laser course, not two years into it. A laser clinic treats hair. It does not treat polycystic ovary syndrome, thyroid disease or any other endocrine disorder. The investigation and any medical management belong with a gynaecologist, endocrinologist or physician, and the choice of metformin or hormonal contraception is a prescribing decision for that doctor.

Tan and colleagues state their own limitations plainly, and so should anyone quoting them: low certainty of evidence, heterogeneous outcome measurement, and few studies evaluating effectiveness and safety in people with skin of colour. Six studies and 423 patients is the entire high-quality evidence base for treating PCOS-related hirsutism with light.

Device choice gets harder, not easier, when hormones are involved

Device selection in hormonal hirsutism runs into a genuine conflict in the evidence, and clinics presenting it as settled are overstating what is known. Tan's review found alexandrite performed best in PCOS, particularly at higher fluence. Inoue's series found alexandrite responsible for 24 of 25 paradoxical hypertrichosis cases, and Qeyam's 16.2% figure came from an all-alexandrite facial cohort in which PCOS patients fared worst. The wavelength with the best efficacy signal in PCOS carries the strongest association with the complication PCOS patients are most prone to. No trial has resolved that.

Fitzpatrick IV–VI skin adds a second constraint. At 755 nm, epidermal melanin competes strongly for the light intended for the follicle, which is why longer wavelengths — 800–810 nm diode and 1064 nm Nd:YAG — are the usual choices in deeper skin tones. The cost of that safety is efficiency. Pai and colleagues measured outcomes in 40 Indian women across 108 sites using blinded algorithmic hair counting rather than investigator impression, and recorded epidermolysis in 5 patients (12.5%) alongside the same 12.5% rate of paradoxical hypertrichosis. Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation is the downstream consequence of that epidermal injury rather than a separate risk, which makes it largely a settings-and-cooling question.

No adequately powered randomised trial has ever compared all three wavelengths in Fitzpatrick V–VI patients with long-term follow-up. The wavelength-by-skin-type rule is physically sound and clinically universal, but it is consensus built on physics, not on a trial that randomised women like most of this clinic's patients.

Facial hair is the hardest site, and hormonal facial hair is the hardest case of all

Hormonally driven hirsutism concentrates on the chin, upper lip, jawline and neck — which is, by some distance, the worst territory in the published data. Krasniqi and colleagues restricted their systematic review to randomised trials that followed patients for at least one complete hair growth cycle at the treated site. Out of the entire world literature, only five trials and 223 patients qualified. Long-term reduction across those trials ran 30–73.6% for Nd:YAG, 35–84.3% for alexandrite, 32.5–69.2% for diode and 27–52.7% for IPL, and the pattern by site was consistent: greatest on legs, which have roughly a one-year hair cycle, and lowest on the face, which has roughly a six-month cycle.

Indian data measured objectively lands lower still. In Pai's algorithmically assessed cohort, median improvement after a mean of 5.4 sessions was 60% on side locks and 53.9% in the axilla, but 24.1% on the upper lip and 14.9% on the chin. Those two facial numbers are the most honest figures available for exactly the areas most hormonal patients want treated, and they are dramatically below what clinic marketing implies. Snast's meta-analysis adds the final piece: paradoxical hypertrichosis is overwhelmingly a face-and-neck phenomenon, at 0.08% elsewhere on the body.

Shortest hair cycle, lowest measured reduction, highest paradoxical hypertrichosis risk and an ongoing androgen signal recruiting new follicles: hormonal facial hair is the least favourable combination in the field. That is not an argument against treating it, but for expecting a longer course, a smaller percentage and a maintenance plan from the outset.

What the evidence does not support

Several claims attached to this topic are not supported by anything published.

  • That laser treats or improves PCOS. It does not. It damages the follicles it reaches; the endocrine disorder is untouched.
  • That thyroid disease has been studied here at all. No study has examined laser hair removal outcomes specifically in thyroid disease — not one. Grouping thyroid dysfunction with PCOS as a driver of apparent laser failure is reasonable mechanistic reasoning and standard clinical practice, but it has zero direct trial evidence behind it and should never be presented as a finding.
  • That the oral contraceptive pill protects against paradoxical hypertrichosis. Qeyam's study looked for that association specifically and found none (P = 0.19). Irregular cycles predicted it; being on the pill did not prevent it.
  • That results are permanent. The FDA-cleared term for these devices is permanent hair reduction, which the clearance documents define as a long-term, stable reduction in the number of hairs regrowing when measured at 6, 9 and 12 months after a course. That is a reduction in count, benchmarked at twelve months — not elimination, and not forever. Cochrane found long-term hair removal was not documented with any treatment.
  • That there is a correct maintenance interval. No study has ever tested maintenance intervals after a course, so the common six-to-twelve-month top-up recommendation has no trial support. What is supported is that hormonally driven patients relapse faster — Nabi's PCOS group lost its result by three months while the idiopathic group held it.
  • That a fixed session count applies. No randomised trial has compared treatment intervals head to head either. The four-to-eight-week convention is derived from follicular cycle biology, not from a trial that tested it.

Sessions, spacing and maintenance when hormones are in play

Planning a course with a hormonal driver means planning for a different arithmetic, and McGill's numbers are the clearest guide. Six sessions bought a 1.9-week hair-free interval in that PCOS cohort; ten bought 4.3 weeks; and it took a mean of twelve treatments before even 31% of patients reached a hair-free interval beyond six weeks. Roughly twice the number of sessions for an outcome most people would still describe as maintenance rather than clearance. That is the counselling fact, and it is better delivered before payment than after session eight.

One more finding from that study deserves care. Despite mean hair reduction of only 31%, 95% of patients reported satisfaction. Reduced density, finer hair and a longer gap between shaves are real outcomes people value. They are not clearance, and the distinction should survive the consultation.

The commercial implication is worth naming: a large upfront package priced on an assumed session count fits badly when an untreated hormonal driver makes that count unpredictable. Ask what happens if the course needs extending, and get the answer before you pay. Across Mumbai, clinic pricing structures vary widely, and package terms vary more than headline prices do.

Questions worth asking before you start a course

If you suspect a hormonal component — irregular periods, hair that has changed character in adulthood, hair returning within two weeks of a session — these are the questions that change the plan rather than the mood of the consultation.

  • Has anyone actually looked for an endocrine cause? Tan's review found combined management outperformed laser alone in every comparison it examined. If nobody has assessed you, the laser is being asked to do a job it was never going to finish.
  • Which wavelength, and on what reasoning for my skin type? A clinic should be able to explain why it has chosen 755, 810 or 1064 nm for your Fitzpatrick type, and should acknowledge the alexandrite trade-off rather than skip it.
  • Is paradoxical hypertrichosis in the written consent, with a number? At 33.3% in PCOS patients in prospective study, its omission is a consent problem, not a marketing preference.
  • What is the plan if reduction stalls at session six? A defined review point beats an open-ended course.
  • Who is holding the handpiece, and what is their training? This is a medical procedure with documented ocular and cutaneous complications, and it should be performed under qualified medical supervision.
