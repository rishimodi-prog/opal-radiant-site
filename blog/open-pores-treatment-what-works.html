Open Pores Treatment: What Actually Shrinks Them and What Cannot

By Dr. Punit Sakhare, MD Dermatologist · 2026-08-26 · 12 min read

Search for an open pores treatment and you will be told, confidently and in a dozen places, that pores can be shrunk, closed, steamed open, iced shut, vacuumed clean or permanently tightened. Almost none of that survives contact with the published literature. A pore is the visible opening of a hair follicle and its sebaceous gland. It is a hole in an anatomical structure, not a valve. Nothing in the trial record shows that hole being made structurally smaller and kept that way.

What the evidence does support is narrower and more useful: several devices produce a measurable, temporary reduction in how large pores appear, largely by cutting sebum output and thickening the dermis around each opening. The honest summary is that the entire controlled literature on pore treatments amounts to roughly 100 subjects, none of them Fitzpatrick V-VI, and none followed beyond six months. This article grades what exists, names what does not, and sets out what a realistic plan looks like for Indian skin.

What an Open Pore Actually Is

An open pore is the ostium of a pilosebaceous unit — the surface opening shared by a hair follicle and the sebaceous gland that drains into it. That opening has no muscle fibres and no sphincter. It cannot contract, and it does not respond to temperature the way marketing copy implies. Its visible diameter is set by the width of the follicular infundibulum, the volume of sebum and keratin sitting inside it, and the tone and thickness of the dermis holding it open.

This anatomy dictates what any treatment can and cannot do. Dong, Lanoue and Goldenberg, reviewing enlarged facial pore treatments in Cutis in 2016, framed the entire therapeutic logic as addressing "associative factors, such as increased sebum production and cutaneous aging." In other words, every credible pore treatment works on the contributors to how a pore looks, not on the pore as a structure. A treatment that reduces sebum, empties the infundibulum or thickens the surrounding dermis will make the opening less conspicuous. None of them removes it, and none of them narrows the follicle permanently.

That distinction matters more than it sounds. It explains why results fade, why maintenance is required, and why a clinic promising to "close" your pores is describing something that has never been demonstrated in a published study.

What Sets Pore Size: Ethnicity and Age Do More Than Oil

The largest dataset on what determines pore size is a 2015 multiethnic study by Flament and colleagues in Clinical, Cosmetic and Investigational Dermatology, which imaged 2,585 women aged 18 to 80 across Brazil, China, France, India and Japan using polarised-light dermatoscopy with automated image analysis. Pore sizes ranged from 0.03 to 0.22 mm-squared across the groups — a roughly 7-fold spread — and ethnicity was the dominant determinant. Chinese women had markedly lower pore density and pore size; Brazilian, Indian and French women had higher.

The finding that matters most for readers in Mumbai is the age curve. Pore size and density plateaued after roughly age 40 in the Chinese, Japanese and French cohorts, but Indian and Brazilian women showed progressive enlargement with age. If your pores look more prominent at 38 than they did at 28, that trajectory is documented, not imagined.

Two caveats belong in the same breath. Every primary author of that study is an employee of L'Oréal Research and Innovation, which is a cosmetics manufacturer with a commercial interest in pore products — the descriptive imaging data is strong, but the framing is not independent. And dark-skinned African women were excluded from the analysis because the automated image analysis was unreliable on deeply pigmented skin. So the single best dataset on pore size contains no Fitzpatrick VI data at all.

The same authors did something unusual for an industry study: they explicitly rejected sebaceous gland function as the primary determinant of visible pore morphology, writing that pore appearance to the human eye "less result[s] from functional criteria of associated appendages such as sebaceous glands." They raise photoageing as an alternative driver. This is the opposite of the standard oily-skin story.

Nose, Cheeks and Chin Are Not the Same Problem

Facial pores do not behave uniformly, which is why a single product applied everywhere tends to disappoint in some zones and irritate in others. Maia Campos, Melo and Mercurio characterised oily skin using advanced imaging in Frontiers in Physiology in 2019 in a small observational study of 19 subjects with no control group. Their regional findings are worth knowing even though the sample is tiny.

The malar region — the cheeks over the cheekbone — had a thinner epidermis and more large pores, with excess sebum linked to the pores themselves rather than to comedones. The nose carried the highest sebum content inside the infundibulum but had fewer active sebaceous glands, which is a reminder that what you see on a nose strip is accumulated content, not gland count. The chin showed a positive correlation between sebum content, surface roughness, and both pore and comedone counts.

Read that as a map rather than a protocol. With n = 19 and no control arm, these are descriptive observations, not treatment guidance. They do explain a familiar picture: nose pores that refill within days of any extraction, and a chin where pores and breakouts travel together.

Open Pores Treatment: What Has Actual Trial Data

Only a handful of interventions for open pores have been tested with any kind of control. The 1064 nm Nd:YAG laser has the two cleanest studies, both from the same Korean group and both using a split-face design in which one half of the face was left untreated as an internal control.

Roh, Chung and Chung, writing in the Journal of Dermatological Treatment in 2009, treated 40 subjects in four subgroups of 10, each receiving a different set of 1064 nm Nd:YAG parameters over five sessions three weeks apart, with objective image analysis and Sebumeter measurement. Pore size and sebum level fell in all four laser methods compared with the untreated half, p < 0.05. The follow-up study by Roh and colleagues in Laser Therapy in 2011 compared quasi long-pulsed (300 microsecond) against Q-switched (5 nanosecond) 1064 nm modes in 20 subjects, 10 per mode, again with an untreated control half. Both modes significantly reduced pore size and sebum against the control side, with no significant difference between them.

Those are proper internal controls and objective instruments, which puts them ahead of most aesthetic literature. The weaknesses are equally plain: 10 subjects per arm, no blinding reported, and no long-term follow-up in either paper. The 1064 nm Nd:YAG is the same wavelength platform used in what clinics market as a carbon laser facial, but neither published pore study used a carbon suspension. They are evidence for the wavelength and the settings, not for the carbon step, and that difference should not be blurred.

Radiofrequency and Botulinum Toxin: Strong Numbers, Fragile Designs

Two other approaches to open pores carry published data, and both illustrate why the design matters more than the headline percentage.

Techapichetvanich and colleagues reported nonablative monopolar radiofrequency for facial pores and sebum in Thai patients in Lasers in Surgery and Medicine in 2023. Nineteen patients enrolled and 17 completed two sessions four weeks apart, assessed with Antera 3D imaging, Sebumeter, Cutometer and blinded dermatologist photo review. Pore volume fell 24% one month after the first treatment (p < 0.016), 34% one month after the final treatment (p < 0.001) and 38% at six months (p < 0.001). Sebum excretion fell 39% at three months and 36% at six months. No dyspigmentation, textural change or scarring was reported.

Those numbers look excellent and they should be discounted. The study was single-arm with no control group, n = 17, at a single centre. Without a control arm, regression to the mean and seasonal variation in sebum output — which is substantial in a humid climate — cannot be separated from treatment effect. A 38% reduction is what was measured in that cohort; it is not an established effect size, and no one should quote it as an expected result. The radiofrequency principle overlaps with what microneedling radiofrequency does to the dermis, though the pore studies used a nonablative monopolar device rather than a needling one.

Sayed and colleagues tested intradermal botulinum toxin for enlarged pores and seborrhoea in the Journal of Dermatological Treatment in 2020, using a split-face design in 20 patients: toxin in one cheek, saline placebo in the other. At one month both sides improved significantly (p = 0.001) — including the saline side — with significantly more improvement on the toxin side on the clinical score. But here is the detail that gets left out of summaries: dermoscopic pore size and OCT-measured dermal thickness both changed significantly from baseline, and the difference between the toxin and saline halves on those two objective measures was not statistically significant. The authors state the effect lasts an average of four months. Injection-related pigmentation risk in Fitzpatrick IV-VI was not assessed in that cohort.

What the Evidence Does Not Support

Five claims about open pores are made routinely in Indian skincare marketing and none of them is supported by a study located in this evidence base.

Pores cannot be permanently closed or made structurally smaller. No published study demonstrates permanent pore-size reduction. Every intervention with trial data — Nd:YAG, monopolar radiofrequency, intradermal toxin — works by cutting sebum output, emptying the infundibulum, or thickening the dermis around the opening. All of those are appearance changes and all of them are reversible when treatment stops.

Steam does not open pores and cold water does not close them. No study supports this, and the anatomy argues against it: a follicular ostium has no sphincter and no muscle, so its diameter is not thermally actuated. Warmth softens the sebum and keratin plug inside the opening, which makes extraction easier and makes the pore look temporarily different. That is a change to the contents, not the aperture. This is folklore, not clinical consensus.

Controlling oil is not the whole answer. The Flament 2015 dataset of 2,585 women explicitly rejects sebaceous gland function as the primary determinant of visible pore morphology, and notes that men report enlarged pores less often than women despite producing more sebum. Anyone who tells you that oil control alone resolves pore appearance is contradicting the largest dataset in the field.

Ethnicity and age set a floor no treatment moves. A 7-fold spread in pore size across ethnic groups, with progressive enlargement with age specifically documented in Indian women, is a biological baseline. Treatment operates above that floor; it does not lower it.

Fractional picosecond lasers are not established for pores. Zhou, Hamblin and Wen reviewed fractional picosecond histology and applications in Lasers in Medical Science in 2023 and list enlarged facial pores among the reported effects — then close the review by stating that "further research is still needed to confirm the benefits of fractional picosecond lasers." A review whose own conclusion is that the benefits are unconfirmed is not a basis for a treatment recommendation.

The Overtreatment Trap in Indian Skin

The specific danger with open pores in Fitzpatrick IV-VI skin is that pores are a texture complaint, texture complaints invite resurfacing, and resurfacing in deeply pigmented skin carries a real pigmentation cost. The trade is frequently a bad one.

Manuskiatti and colleagues treated 13 Fitzpatrick IV patients with ablative fractional carbon dioxide resurfacing for atrophic acne scars in the Journal of the American Academy of Dermatology in 2010. Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation occurred in 92% of subjects and after 51% of sessions, resolving over roughly five weeks. That study is small and uncontrolled, and the PIH was mild and transient — but a 92% incidence in Fitzpatrick IV is the number to hold in mind before agreeing to aggressive resurfacing for a cosmetic texture concern.

Superficial peels sit at the gentler end and carry better numbers, with an important qualifier. According to PubMed, Vemula and colleagues retrospectively reviewed 473 superficial chemical peel treatments in Fitzpatrick III-VI skin in the Journal of the American Academy of Dermatology in 2018 and found an overall complication rate of 3.8%. The commonest complication was crusting (2.3%), with post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation and erythema tied at 1.9%. All side effects resolved within eight months. Crucially, in the adjusted model Fitzpatrick VI carried an odds ratio of 5.14 for any adverse event (95% CI 1.21-21.8, p = 0.0118), and side effects were significantly less common in winter. This is a single-centre retrospective design, so treat it as a well-quantified signal rather than a trial result — but it is the best available guide to peel risk in deeper skin, and it supports scheduling procedural work for the cooler months.

Note also that no located study tested a chemical peel with pore size as the measured endpoint. Peels have measured data for pigment and for acne; for pores specifically, the peel case rests on clinical reasoning about desquamation and infundibular contents, not on trial output. That is worth saying plainly rather than implying otherwise.

How the Options Compare, and What a Sensible Plan Looks Like

Ranked by strength of evidence rather than by marketing volume, open pore options fall into a short and unflattering order. The 1064 nm Nd:YAG has two split-face studies with untreated control halves (n = 40 and n = 20) showing reductions in both pore size and sebum at p < 0.05. Monopolar radiofrequency has larger measured percentages but no control group at all (n = 17). Intradermal botulinum toxin has a placebo-controlled split-face design (n = 20) in which the objective measures did not separate from saline, and an author-stated duration of about four months. Fractional picosecond has a review that declines to confirm its own benefits. Chemical peels and hydradermabrasion have no located trial with pore size as an endpoint.

What follows from that is a sequencing argument, not a device argument. Photoprotection and a consistent, non-irritating routine come first, because photoageing is the alternative driver Flament's group raised and because inflammation in Fitzpatrick IV-VI skin buys pigment. Gentle regular clearing of the infundibular contents — which is what a hydra facial style hydradermabrasion does mechanically — changes how pores look on the day and needs repeating; it is a maintenance measure, not a structural one. Device work with published control data belongs after that, at conservative settings, spaced generously, and reassessed against objective photographs rather than memory.

Realistic expectations, stated without softening: an open pores treatment may improve the appearance of enlarged pores for a period measured in months, on evidence drawn from about 100 controlled subjects, none of whom had Fitzpatrick V-VI skin. Nobody has shown a durable structural change. If a clinic offers permanence, ask which study they are quoting. There isn't one.

The Bottom Line

Open pores are a real cosmetic concern with a genuinely thin evidence base behind their treatment. Pore size is set mostly by ethnicity, age and photoageing, with sebum a contributor rather than the driver; in Indian women specifically, pore enlargement continues with age rather than plateauing. Treatments that have been measured against a control — 1064 nm Nd:YAG in two small split-face studies, intradermal toxin in one placebo-controlled split-face study — produce modest, temporary improvements in appearance. Monopolar radiofrequency produced the largest measured reductions but had no control arm, so those percentages cannot be treated as an expected result. No study has followed any pore treatment beyond six months, and none has reported outcomes in Fitzpatrick V-VI skin.

The practical conclusion is to treat pore work as ongoing appearance management with realistic ceilings, to prefer conservative settings over aggressive resurfacing given post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation rates as high as 92% in Fitzpatrick IV after ablative fractional CO2, and to be suspicious of any promise of permanence. If you want an assessment of what is actually driving your pore appearance — sebum, texture, photoageing or all three — that is a consultation question, and it should be answered with a dermatoscope rather than a brochure.
