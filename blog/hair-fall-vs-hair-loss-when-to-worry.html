Hair Loss

Hair Fall Reasons: When Shedding Stops Being Normal

By Dr. Punit Sakhare, MD Dermatologist · 2026-09-05 · 12 min read

Quick Facts

Normal daily shedding

Hair fall reasons sort into two clinically different categories, and almost every useful decision depends on which one you are in. In the first, hairs that were always going to be released leave earlier and in greater numbers than usual; the follicle underneath is intact and the shaft is normal calibre. In the second, the follicles shrink with each cycle, producing finer and shorter hairs until they produce nothing visible. The first is shedding. The second is loss. They look identical in the shower drain and behave completely differently over five years.

This post sets out what the published evidence establishes about the boundary between the two, including the uncomfortable finding that the “50 to 100 hairs a day” figure everyone quotes was never measured in a population, and that the instruments used to count hair agree with each other in fewer than half of patients. It also states where the evidence runs out. None of this is medical advice — a diagnosis needs a qualified dermatologist examining your scalp.

Shedding and hair loss are two different problems

Shedding and pattern hair loss are separate processes with separate mechanics. Shedding, telogen effluvium in clinical language, is a disturbance in the timing of the hair cycle. Every scalp follicle runs through a growth phase lasting years, a brief transition, and a resting phase of roughly three months at the end of which the hair is released. A physiological shock pushes an abnormally large share of follicles into that phase at once, and two to three months later they let go together. The follicle survives, and the hair that replaces it is full thickness.

Pattern hair loss works differently. In genetically susceptible follicles under androgen influence, each successive cycle produces a slightly shorter, finer hair, a process called miniaturisation. Nothing dramatic arrives in the drain. What changes is the ratio of thick terminal hairs to fine vellus hairs across the top of the scalp, which is why the parting widens long before shedding becomes noticeable.

Rebora’s 2019 review in Clinical, Cosmetic and Investigational Dermatology, a narrative review rather than a trial, makes the point that matters most. Telogen effluvium is heterogeneous, it frequently coexists with pattern loss in the same patient, and how profuse shedding must be before telogen effluvium can be diagnosed at all remains, in that review’s own framing, an unresolved problem. A woman in her thirties can be shedding heavily from a post-viral effluvium while miniaturising along her midline, and treating only the first will not touch the second.

Where the “50 to 100 hairs a day” figure actually comes from

The claim that losing 50 to 100 hairs a day is normal is a textbook convention, not a measurement. It is arithmetic on the hair cycle: roughly 100,000 scalp follicles, about a tenth resting at any moment, divided by a resting phase of around 100 days, gives approximately 100 hairs a day. No population was counted to produce it.

The one dataset that did count is Wasko and colleagues’ 2008 paper in Archives of Dermatology, which standardised a 60-second hair count in 60 healthy men aged 20 to 60 with no alopecia. Men aged 20 to 40 shed between 0 and 78 hairs in that minute, mean 10.2. Men aged 41 to 60 shed between 0 and 43, mean 10.3. Day-to-day variation within an individual was low, and counts were stable at six-month repeat.

Three things follow. The average is far below 100. The individual range is enormous: one healthy man with no hair loss shed 78 hairs in a single minute of combing. And the study enrolled 60 men at one centre, so no normative shedding dataset exists for women anywhere in the literature, and none for Fitzpatrick IV–VI populations. What carries information is the change from your own baseline, how many months it has run, and whether the hairs coming out are full calibre or short and fine.

The most common hair fall reasons, sorted by which category they belong to

The most common hair fall reasons divide along the shedding-versus-loss line, and sorting them that way is more useful than an undifferentiated list of causes.

Reasons that produce shedding: high fever or significant infection, major surgery, rapid weight loss, crash dieting, severe psychological stress, childbirth, starting or stopping hormonal contraception, and a long list of prescription drugs. The characteristic feature is the lag: the trigger sits two to three months before the shedding starts, which is why patients dismiss the real cause as too long ago to matter. That figure is clinical consensus traceable to Kligman’s 1961 descriptive work, not a cohort study; no prospective study of the natural history of acute telogen effluvium has ever been published, so the quoted “resolves in three to six months” timeline is consensus too.

Reasons that produce pattern loss: inherited susceptibility acting through androgens, and endocrine conditions that raise androgen exposure. Polycystic ovary syndrome matters most in a women’s clinic. In a cross-sectional study of 100 Indian women with PCOS in Srinagar, Keen and colleagues recorded female pattern hair loss in 31%, alongside hirsutism in 78% and acne in 48%. That study had no non-PCOS control group, and the direction of the statistic matters: a third of women with PCOS in that cohort had pattern loss, not a third of women with hair loss having PCOS.

One category belongs in neither box. In scarring alopecias the follicle is destroyed and does not return. Any patch of scalp that is smooth, shiny, scaly, itchy or painful, or where follicular openings have disappeared, needs a dermatologist promptly rather than a supplement. Our hair fall and thinning page describes that assessment.

The signs that it has become pattern loss rather than shedding

Pattern loss announces itself through architecture rather than volume. The most quotable line in the female pattern hair loss literature comes from a 2016 consensus paper by Harries and colleagues in the Journal of the European Academy of Dermatology and Venereology: by the time patients show the full manifestations of Ludwig grade I, they have already lost a significant volume of hair. That paper was an expert consensus of nine clinicians, with no patients enrolled, no control group and, by the authors’ own statement, no validation study. They proposed a Female Pattern Hair Loss Severity Index combining shedding, midline density and trichoscopy, scored out of 20, precisely because the Ludwig and Sinclair scales are too coarse to catch early disease. It is not a validated instrument.

What the reader should watch for is a central parting measurably wider than in her own photographs from a year or two ago, scalp visible through the midline under overhead light, and short fine hairs mixed among normal ones at the crown. The reason it matters is time. Kaufman and colleagues pooled the placebo arms of two replicate randomised trials with four one-year double-blind extensions and followed untreated men with pattern hair loss for five years. Those men lost 239 hairs from the target area, a 26.3% decline in density (P < 0.001), and 75% were rated visibly worse on blinded global photographic assessment. The first author worked for Merck Research Laboratories, which is worth knowing, but this is a placebo arm rather than a product claim. Shedding usually settles by itself. Pattern loss does not.

What the evidence does not support

Several of the things said most confidently about hair fall are not supported by the studies that went looking.

Iron is the largest one. Olsen and colleagues at Duke compared 381 women with female pattern hair loss or chronic telogen effluvium against 76 control women with no hair loss. At a ferritin threshold of 15 µg/L in premenopausal women, iron deficiency was present in 12.4% of the pattern hair loss group, 12.1% of the effluvium group, and 29.8% of the controls. At a 40 µg/L threshold: 58.8%, 63.8% and 72.3%. The controls were more iron-deficient than the patients at both cut-offs, and the authors found no statistically significant increase in iron deficiency in either patient group. Other cohorts found the opposite association, so this is contested, but no randomised trial has shown that giving iron to a non-anaemic woman regrows hair, and the familiar “ferritin above 70” target originates as a personal recommendation in a 2002 narrative review whose own author called it yet to be definitively established.

The broader vitamin panel fares worse. Durusu Turkoglu and colleagues measured haemoglobin, ferritin, vitamin B12, thyroid function, vitamin D, zinc, copper, selenium and both serum and urine biotin in 90 women with chronic telogen effluvium against 90 female controls. There was no difference in haemoglobin, ferritin, B12, thyroid function tests, vitamin D, copper or biotin. Zinc was lower in patients; selenium was significantly higher. Their conclusion was that nutritional deficiencies are not as common as thought in patients diagnosed with telogen effluvium. Higher selenium is a warning about over-supplementation, not a deficiency to correct, since selenium excess is itself a recognised cause of hair loss.

Two drug claims also fail. Across four trials totalling 1,006 women, 5% topical minoxidil produced no more regrowth than 2% (RR 1.12, 95% CI 0.72–1.73) and no difference in total hair count (mean difference −2.12, 95% CI −5.47 to 1.23), while causing more irritation and more unwanted facial hair growth. Finasteride 1 mg, which works in men, was no better than placebo in women across three trials: improvement in 30 of 67 versus 33 of 70, RR 0.95 (95% CI 0.66–1.37).

And no clinic can promise an outcome. No published trial supports a guaranteed result from any treatment for pattern hair loss, or a success percentage for an injectable hair treatment in a comparable population.

How much a hair assessment can actually measure

Counting hair is less precise than the equipment implies. Guarrera and colleagues compared two instrumental methods, the modified wash test and TrichoScan, against clinical diagnosis in 41 patients presenting with hair loss. Agreement between clinical diagnosis and the wash test was κ = 0.32, conventionally read as “fair”; against TrichoScan it was worse at κ = 0.22. Most tellingly, the two instrumental methods agreed with each other in only 17 of 41 patients, or 41%.

That is a 41-patient single-centre study and it does not settle the field, but it is the strongest available evidence on the question and its direction is unambiguous: hair-counting instruments disagree with each other and with the clinician about as often as they agree. Anyone presenting a machine-generated hair count as a precise diagnosis is overstating what the tool does.

What a hair consultation does reliably is narrower and more useful than a single number. Trichoscopy, dermoscopy of the scalp, is standard practice for separating scarring from non-scarring alopecia and for spotting the shaft-diameter variation that signals miniaturisation, although the literature supporting it is largely review-level rather than formal accuracy work. A pull test, standardised photographs from fixed angles and lighting, a drug and illness history covering the previous six months, and blood tests targeted at what that history suggests will get further than any instrument reading alone. Repeating the same photographs six months later is, unglamorously, the most informative thing most patients can do.

What treatment the evidence supports once it is pattern loss

Topical minoxidil is the only intervention for female pattern hair loss with Cochrane-grade evidence behind it. The 2016 Cochrane review by van Zuuren and colleagues pooled 47 randomised trials covering 5,290 participants, and recorded that only 5 were at low risk of bias, with 26 unclear and 16 at high risk — the honest context for the numbers below.

Against placebo, participant-rated moderate-to-marked regrowth occurred in 157 of 593 on minoxidil versus 77 of 555 on placebo, RR 1.93 (95% CI 1.51–2.47), graded moderate-quality evidence. Investigator-rated assessment across seven studies (n = 1,181) gave RR 2.35 (95% CI 1.68–3.28). Total hair count rose by 13.18 hairs per cm² compared with placebo (95% CI 10.92–15.44) across 8 studies and 1,242 participants, though that estimate was graded low quality. Adverse events on 2% minoxidil twice daily did not differ significantly from placebo: 40 of 407 versus 28 of 320, RR 1.24 (95% CI 0.82–1.87).

Thirteen extra hairs per square centimetre is a real, reproducible effect, and a modest one — not the restoration of a lost hairline. The same review’s authors called for randomised trials of spironolactone, dutasteride, cyproterone acetate and lasers, a polite way of recording that all of those are used in women without randomised support.

Platelet-rich plasma and the growth-factor concentrate preparations marketed widely in India sit in a different evidence tier and are examined in a separate post on this blog. What can be said here is that neither carries regulatory approval for pattern hair loss anywhere, that registration of a preparation kit as a medical device is not approval of a treatment claim, and that published comparisons position these injectables as additions to medical treatment rather than replacements for it. Hair fillers sit in the same adjunct category.

What the Indian data does and does not tell us

There is no population-based prevalence study of pattern hair loss in India, for either sex. Any percentage quoted for Indian women has therefore been extrapolated from somewhere else.

The nearest good data is Chinese. Wang and colleagues interviewed 17,886 people across six Chinese cities, with 15,257 completing, and found androgenetic alopecia in 6.0% of women overall — 1.3% at ages 18 to 29, 2.3% at 30 to 39, 5.4% at 40 to 49, 7.5% at 50 to 59, 10.3% at 60 to 69 and 11.8% above 70. Ludwig grade I was the commonest presentation and 19.2% of affected women reported a family history; men in the same survey were at 21.3%. Applying figures from six Chinese cities to Mumbai is an extrapolation, but it is a better anchor than a number with no denominator behind it.

Indian evidence that does exist is clinic-based: a 35-woman hospital series, the 100-woman PCOS cohort above, and a 50-woman cross-sectional study in New Delhi reporting metabolic syndrome in 24% and insulin resistance in 34% of premenopausal women with female pattern hair loss. That last study had no control group, the limitation that stops it supporting any claim about cause or screening.

One further gap bears directly on readers here: no trial in the platelet-rich plasma or growth-factor literature reports Fitzpatrick phototype, let alone stratifies outcomes by it. For a clinic whose patients are predominantly Fitzpatrick IV to VI, the evidence base for injectable hair treatments has never been tested in the population it is offered to.

When to stop watching and get it assessed

No single hair count separates normal shedding from a problem, so the decision rests on pattern, duration and associated signs. Reasonable triggers for a dermatology consultation, none diagnostic alone:

  • shedding that has continued beyond roughly six months instead of tapering, since acute effluvium is generally self-limiting and persistent shedding calls for a different workup;
  • a parting visibly wider than in your own photographs from a year or two ago, or scalp showing through the midline under direct overhead light;
  • short, fine hairs mixed among normal-calibre ones at the crown or temples — the visible signature of miniaturisation;
  • any patch that is smooth, scaly, itchy or painful, or where follicular openings have disappeared, since scarring alopecia does not reverse;
  • shedding alongside missed periods, new coarse facial hair, adult acne, unexplained weight change or persistent fatigue, which together point at an endocrine cause rather than at the hair itself.

The asymmetry should drive the decision. A self-limiting effluvium costs nothing to have looked at, while pattern loss left alone progresses: 26.3% of target-area density over five years in the placebo data quoted above, three-quarters of patients visibly worse.

Nothing here is a diagnosis or a treatment recommendation, and no outcome can be promised for any treatment described. Hair fall reasons overlap, coexist and look alike from the outside, so telling them apart requires a qualified dermatologist examining your scalp and taking your history.
