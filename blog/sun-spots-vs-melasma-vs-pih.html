Sun Spots, Melasma or PIH? The Types of Pigmentation on Face That Get Confused

By Dr. Punit Sakhare, MD Dermatologist · 2026-10-02 · 11 min read

Three conditions account for most of what arrives at a Mumbai skin clinic described only as "pigmentation": melasma, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH) and solar lentigines, which most people call sun spots. On a phone camera they photograph almost identically. They are not the same condition, they do not respond to the same treatments, and at least one of them reliably gets worse when treated as though it were another. Working out which of the types of pigmentation on face you actually have is not an academic exercise. It decides whether the next six months improve your skin or set it back.

The distinction rests on four things a clinician can assess without any device: symmetry, border shape, what happened to the skin beforehand, and colour. A 2023 CME review by Wang and colleagues in the Journal of the American Academy of Dermatology organises the whole family of hyperpigmentation disorders by depth of pigment deposition, epidermal, dermal or mixed, and that framework is the one used here. It is a review rather than a trial, so it carries clinical authority rather than experimental proof.

The three types of pigmentation on face that fill Indian clinics

Melasma, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation and solar lentigines are the three types of pigmentation on face that dominate Indian dermatology practice, and each carries a distinct clinical signature. Melasma appears as symmetrical brown to grey-brown patches, usually across the cheeks, upper lip, forehead or jawline. In the largest Indian dataset available, a cross-sectional multicentre study of 331 adults with Wood's-lamp-confirmed melasma published by KrupaShankar and colleagues in 2014, mean age was 37.2 plus or minus 9.3 years, women outnumbered men by roughly four to one, and 31% reported a family history. The centrofacial pattern accounted for 42% of cases and the malar pattern 39%. That study was hospital-based and uncontrolled, so it describes who attends clinics rather than community prevalence, and two of its authors carry industry-style affiliations that the paper does not clearly disclose.

Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation is the mark the skin leaves behind after it has been inflamed or injured, most often by acne, eczema, a burn, a cosmetic procedure or persistent scratching. A 2024 PRISMA systematic review by Mar and colleagues pooled 48 studies covering 1,356 people with skin of colour. Mean age was 29 years, 78% were female, Fitzpatrick IV accounted for 40% of participants and Fitzpatrick V for 34%, and 89% of cases were precipitated by an inflammatory condition, with 83% occurring on the face.

Solar lentigines, or sun spots, are discrete, well-demarcated light-to-dark brown macules on cumulatively sun-exposed skin, and they become more common with age. They sit at the epidermal end of Wang and colleagues' depth spectrum. The same review flags two conditions common in Indian skin and frequently mislabelled as melasma: lichen planus pigmentosus and ashy dermatosis, whose status as genuinely distinct entities is still debated.

How to tell sun spots, melasma and PIH apart

Symmetry, border, history and colour separate melasma, PIH and sun spots more reliably than any single test. Melasma is symmetrical and follows an anatomical pattern with no preceding event. PIH follows the footprint of whatever inflamed the skin, so it is asymmetric and outlined like the lesion that resolved. A solar lentigo is a scattered, well-demarcated spot on sun-exposed skin with neither symmetry nor an inflammatory history.

The colour rule comes from a 2011 review by Callender and colleagues in the American Journal of Clinical Dermatology, and it is the most practically useful single observation in the whole differential: tan, brown or dark brown lesions indicate epidermal pigment, while blue-grey indicates dermal hypermelanosis. Depth predicts response, because topical agents reach epidermal pigment and largely do not reach dermal pigment. It is a review, not a trial, so treat it as well-founded clinical observation rather than measured effect.

Two caveats. These patterns overlap in real faces, and carrying melasma across the cheekbones and PIH from acne along the jaw at once is routine. And no single feature is diagnostic; they are read together with a drug and hormone history, because several drug-induced and contact pigmentations mimic all three.

Melasma is a whole-unit problem, which is why it comes back

Melasma is not a simple deposit of pigment in the upper layer of skin, and that is the single most important reason it behaves differently from sun spots and PIH. A 2016 narrative review by Kwon and colleagues in the International Journal of Molecular Sciences, synthesising histologic series, describes melasma-affected skin as showing epidermal hyperpigmentation together with solar elastosis in the extracellular matrix, a disrupted basement membrane, increased dermal blood vessels and increased numbers of dermal mast cells. The histology is reproducible across several independent series, which is what gives the finding its weight. It is still a review with no control group, so the pathology is well supported while any treatment inference drawn from it is not.

Three consequences follow, and they are worth stating plainly to anyone starting melasma treatment. The melanocytes are suppressed rather than removed, so the cell population that makes the pigment survives every current treatment. A damaged basement membrane lets melanin and melanocytes drop into the dermis, where topical agents cannot reach them. And the drivers, sun including visible light, hormones and heat, continue after treatment ends. Melasma is managed rather than resolved, which is why we describe it on our pigmentation and melasma page as a long-term condition rather than a course of sessions.

Sun spots have none of this architecture behind them, and PIH has a trigger that can be switched off. Two of the three can fade and stay faded; the third is a relapsing condition needing maintenance.

PIH runs on a different clock

Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation is the one of the three types of facial pigmentation where the published treatment evidence is weakest, and the most useful thing a clinic can do is say so. The 2024 systematic review by Mar and colleagues, covering 48 studies and 1,356 people with skin of colour, found topical retinoids were the most-used intervention at 22% of cases with partial improvement in 85%, and laser therapy second at 17% with partial improvement in 66%. Laser was the only modality that produced complete resolution in a subgroup, at 26%, but the same review recorded cases of PIH being made worse by laser. Chemical peels, used in 9% of cases, and hydroquinone, used in 7%, produced less effective outcomes. The authors' own conclusion is worth quoting in substance: their results show a lack of robust efficacy across all treatment modalities.

Time course matters as much as choice of treatment. Depth predicts how long PIH takes, with epidermal pigment measured in months and dermal, blue-grey pigment potentially taking years or not fully resolving. There is a real evidence gap here: no controlled study establishes a resolution timeline for spontaneous PIH in Fitzpatrick V to VI skin. The only timings available are incidental, from laser studies, where post-procedure PIH in Fitzpatrick IV resolved over roughly five weeks in one small trial and fell from 55.5% of patients at one month to 11.1% at six months in another.

The first step for PIH is not a pigment treatment at all. It is controlling the inflammation producing it, because active acne generates fresh marks faster than any depigmenting regimen clears the old ones.

Sun spots, and the lesion not to mistake for one

A solar lentigo is benign, but not every well-demarcated brown macule on sun-damaged facial skin is a solar lentigo, and this is the one place in the pigmentation differential where the stakes are not cosmetic. A 2014 review by Lallas and colleagues in Clinics in Dermatology sets out the differential for a pigmented macule on chronically sun-damaged facial skin: lentigo maligna, pigmented actinic keratosis, solar lentigo and lichen-planus-like keratosis. The review's central finding is that these lesions may reveal overlapping criteria, making their differential diagnosis clinically difficult, and that dermatoscopy improves accuracy over the unaided eye without eliminating the problem.

The rule that follows is simple. A single new dark patch, or one changing in size, shape or colour, belongs in front of a doctor, not in a facial or peel booking. Melasma and PIH are almost by definition multiple and patterned; a lesion that is alone and evolving is a different question.

Beyond that safety line, sun spots are the most straightforward of the three: epidermal, with no relapsing hormonal driver, and photoprotection genuinely limits new ones accumulating. That does not make them trivially treatable in deeper skin, for reasons the next section covers.

Why the wrong label costs you months

Treating melasma with the settings that suit a sun spot is the most expensive error in facial pigmentation, and the Indian data on it is unusually clean. A 2012 randomised three-arm study by Kar and colleagues in the Indian Journal of Dermatology, Venereology and Leprology treated 75 Indian patients with melasma using low-fluence Q-switched Nd:YAG, high-fluence Q-switched Nd:YAG or a glycolic acid peel. Low-fluence laser outperformed the glycolic peel, which in turn outperformed high-fluence laser. Turning the energy up made melasma worse. With 21, 19 and 20 patients completing per arm and no untreated control, the trial shows direction rather than magnitude, but that direction matches the rest of the melasma literature.

The hierarchy question was settled more broadly by McKesey and colleagues in 2020 in the American Journal of Clinical Dermatology, a systematic review restricted to randomised and controlled trials covering 113 studies and 6,897 participants. It concluded that triple combination cream and hydroquinone monotherapy are the most effective and best-studied melasma treatments, and that peels and laser or light devices are equal or inferior to topicals while carrying higher adverse-effect risk. Hydroquinone has been Schedule H, prescription-only, in India since 1 April 2019, so that is a supervised medical route, not a counter purchase.

Long-term laser data for melasma is scarce and unflattering. A 2024 randomised split-face study by Zhou and colleagues in Lasers in Surgery and Medicine compared 755 nm picosecond alexandrite against 1064 nm Q-switched Nd:YAG with two-year follow-up. Only 17 of 20 enrolled patients were analysed, so it is small, but methodologically it is the strongest long-term laser evidence available. At six months the Q-switched side sat significantly below baseline (p = 0.022) while the picosecond side showed no significant difference from baseline at all. At two years, 10 of 17 patients, 58.82%, had recurrence or worsening, with no difference between the lasers.

Depth of skin tone changes the risk calculation as well. A 2018 review of 473 superficial chemical peels in Fitzpatrick III to VI skin by Vemula and colleagues found an overall complication rate of 3.8%, but Fitzpatrick VI carried an odds ratio of 5.14 (95% CI 1.21 to 21.8) for any adverse event. Crusting was commonest at 2.3%, with PIH and erythema tied at 1.9%. All complications resolved within eight months, and side effects were significantly less common in winter, a useful scheduling point in Mumbai. None of this makes peels or lasers unusable in Indian skin. It means the diagnosis comes first and the settings follow it.

A tan is not melasma, PIH or a sun spot

A tan is a fourth thing that gets folded into the pigmentation conversation and it does not belong there. Tanning is a physiological response: diffuse rather than patterned, confined to skin the sun reached, and reversing as tanned keratinocytes shed through normal epidermal turnover. Melasma and PIH occupy defined patches with an anatomy or a history behind them and do not resolve by staying indoors. Constitutional skin tone does not change at all and is not a condition to be treated.

This distinction has a commercial edge to it, because "de-tan" treatments are widely sold and no controlled trial with tanning as a defined endpoint appears in the indexed literature. The term does not exist as a study endpoint. Since delayed tanning fades with epidermal turnover anyway, a treatment arm without a no-treatment comparator cannot separate its own effect from natural fading. Where superficial resurfacing and peel procedures have measured evidence, the endpoint was melasma severity or melanin index, not a tan.

What the evidence does not support

Several confident claims about facial pigmentation do not survive contact with the literature.

  • That a Wood's lamp tells you exactly how deep your pigment sits. Wood's lamp classification of melasma as epidermal, dermal or mixed is standard practice in Indian clinics, but no adequately powered study validating that classification against histopathology surfaced in this research. KrupaShankar's 2014 study used the lamp to confirm cases, not classify depth. Treat it as clinical consensus, not established fact.
  • That laser permanently clears melasma. Zhou's 2024 two-year split-face data found 58.82% recurrence or exacerbation, and the largest Indian long-term follow-up, a retrospective cohort of 957 patients, found relapse within weeks to months of stopping any modality.
  • That picosecond lasers are the breakthrough. In the only randomised comparison with two-year follow-up, the picosecond side showed no statistically significant improvement from baseline at six months while the older Q-switched Nd:YAG side did. Newer is not automatically better.
  • That stronger settings clear pigment faster. In 75 Indian patients, high-fluence Q-switched Nd:YAG performed significantly worse than low-fluence and worse than a glycolic peel.
  • That one protocol suits all three conditions. The best PIH systematic review concluded there is a lack of robust efficacy across all modalities for PIH, while the best melasma review found topicals outperform devices. A protocol built around one condition is not evidence-based for another.
  • That you can reliably self-diagnose from photographs. Dermatoscopy improves accuracy over the unaided eye for facial pigmented macules and still does not eliminate diagnostic overlap between benign and malignant lesions.

None of this means treatment is futile. It means the realistic promise is a reduction in the appearance of pigmentation with ongoing maintenance, and that a guaranteed outcome or a fixed number of sessions to clearance is not something the evidence contains.

What a proper pigmentation assessment should involve

A useful first consultation for facial pigmentation spends most of its time on history and examination rather than on a device. It should establish when the marks appeared and what preceded them, whether the pattern is symmetrical, what medicines and hormonal treatments are in use, what has already been tried and for how long, and whether any single lesion is new, solitary or changing. Baseline photographs under consistent lighting matter more than patients expect.

Expect the plan to start with photoprotection and topicals, because that is where the controlled evidence sits, and expect device work to be an adjunct at conservative settings if melasma is part of the picture. Expect a maintenance discussion too, since roughly half of melasma patients relapse within six months even on a maintenance regimen.
