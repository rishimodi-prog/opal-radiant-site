Hormonal Acne Treatment for Adult Women in India: What's Actually Driving It

By Dr. Punit Sakhare, MD Dermatologist · 2026-09-16 · 12 min read

Acne that begins or persists after the age of 25, clusters along the jawline and lower cheeks, flares before a period and ignores the routine that worked at 19 is what most women mean by hormonal acne. Hormonal acne treatment is one of the few corners of dermatology where the evidence has genuinely improved in the last three years rather than simply accumulated. Until 2017, a hybrid systematic review covering 10 randomised trials and 21 case series concluded that every trial of oral spironolactone for acne was at high risk of bias and that the quality of evidence was low or very low for all outcomes. Since then a 410-woman double-blind placebo-controlled trial, a 133-woman double-blind trial against doxycycline, and a meta-analysis of five placebo-controlled trials have replaced that with something much firmer.

What has not moved is the descriptive story around it. The lower-face distribution, the premenstrual flare, the deep tender nodules, the resistance to topicals: these are clinical convention, and this review of the published literature located no prospective study validating them as predictors of measurable hyperandrogenism or of response to anti-androgen therapy. They are useful pattern recognition, not diagnostic criteria. What follows is what the trials measured, how large the effects were, how long they took, and which common claims have no supporting data at all.

What hormonal acne describes, and which of its signs are convention

Hormonal acne is not a separate disease with its own diagnostic test. It is a description of adult acne in which androgen activity at the sebaceous gland is thought to be the dominant driver. The rationale behind the FASCE trial, a French multicentre double-blind study of 133 adult women published in 2024, states it plainly: acne in adult females is triggered mainly by hormones, and spironolactone targets the androgen receptor of sebaceous glands. The strongest indirect evidence that androgens matter here is that blocking that receptor produces measurable improvement under randomised placebo-controlled conditions.

The clinical picture is better described by the trial populations than by the folklore. In the SAFA trial, the largest randomised study in this field, the 410 women recruited had a mean age of 29.2 years (SD 7.2) and facial acne persisting for at least six months. Baseline severity was mostly modest: 46% mild, 40% moderate, 13% severe on investigator assessment. Crucially, 82.9% were already using a topical treatment. That is the defining feature of adult female acne in clinic. It is usually not severe, it is chronic, and the woman has already tried the obvious things. The jawline pattern is a reason to discuss hormonal therapy, not a result.

Why adult female acne is managed differently from teenage acne

Adult female acne is managed differently because of where the patient starts. When more than four in five women entering a trial are already on topical therapy, the question is not whether to start a retinoid; it is what to add once topicals have had a fair run. Historically the answer was an oral antibiotic — and SAFA recruited precisely that population.

The 2024 American Academy of Dermatology acne guideline, built from a systematic review with GRADE assessment and containing 18 evidence-based recommendations and 5 good practice statements, sets the framework. Its strong recommendations are benzoyl peroxide, topical retinoids, topical antibiotics and oral doxycycline, plus oral isotretinoin for acne that is severe, causing psychosocial burden or scarring, or failing standard therapy. Combined oral contraceptive pills and spironolactone are conditional recommendations. Conditional does not mean the drug is weak; it means the certainty of evidence or the balance of benefits and harms did not reach the threshold for a strong recommendation.

The guideline's good practice statements also matter: combine topicals with different mechanisms of action, limit systemic antibiotic use, pair any systemic antibiotic with a topical, and consider intralesional corticosteroid for larger lesions. In India, where oral antibiotics are far easier to obtain and to keep taking than in the systems these guidelines were written for, the instruction to limit them is the one most often ignored.

When adult acne is worth investigating for PCOS

Acne is one recognised clinical feature of hyperandrogenism, but acne alone is not a basis for a polycystic ovary syndrome diagnosis. The 2023 International Evidence-based Guideline for the Assessment and Management of PCOS, developed across 39 professional and consumer organisations in 71 countries and containing 254 recommendations and practice points (77 evidence-based, 54 consensus, 123 practice points), sets a specific diagnostic algorithm. Investigation is reasonable when acne coexists with other features such as menstrual irregularity, hirsutism or androgenic alopecia. It is not reasonable to infer the diagnosis from the location of the spots.

The 2023 guideline is unusually candid about its own foundations, stating that the evidence in PCOS assessment and management remains of low to moderate quality and that significantly greater research is needed. Its headline change for adults is that anti-Müllerian hormone is now accepted as an alternative to ultrasound for the polycystic ovarian morphology criterion — in adults only, explicitly not in adolescents. Several named authors declare pharmaceutical and diagnostics industry interests.

The division of labour is straightforward. A dermatology clinic can recognise a possible hormonal driver, treat the skin, and refer. Diagnosing PCOS belongs with endocrinology or gynaecology following that algorithm, not with a hormone panel ordered at a skin clinic. If scalp thinning is part of your picture, that evidence is covered in our guide to PCOS and hair loss.

Hormonal acne treatment: what the largest randomised trial actually found

The best evidence here is the SAFA trial (Santer and colleagues, Health Technology Assessment, 2024): a pragmatic, double-blind, placebo-controlled superiority randomised trial in 410 women aged 18 and over with facial acne persisting at least six months, 201 on spironolactone and 209 on placebo, with 342 in the primary analysis. Dosing was 50 mg daily to week 6, then 100 mg daily to week 24; more than 95% of women in both arms tolerated treatment and increased the dose.

Read the results in both directions. On the primary outcome, the Acne-QoL symptom subscale at week 12, the adjusted difference was 1.27 (95% CI 0.07 to 2.46) — statistically positive, but only just clear of zero. By week 24 it was 3.77 (95% CI 2.50 to 5.03), a far more convincing separation. Investigator-assessed treatment success at week 12 was 18.5% (31 of 201) on spironolactone against 5.6% (9 of 209) on placebo, an adjusted odds ratio of 5.18 (95% CI 2.18 to 12.28). The odds ratio sounds transformative; the absolute figure is fewer than one in five women reaching investigator-defined success at three months.

The pattern repeats on patient-reported outcomes. Self-reported improvement at week 12 was 72.2% versus 67.9%, an adjusted odds ratio of 1.16 (95% CI 0.70 to 1.91) — not significant. At week 24 it was 81.9% versus 63.3% (OR 2.72, 95% CI 1.50 to 4.93), and satisfaction improved in 70.6% versus 43.1%. Adverse reactions were similar between groups with no serious adverse reactions reported, though headache was more common on spironolactone at 20.4% versus 12.0%.

Two limits should be stated before anyone quotes SAFA at an Indian patient. The drug is slow: on every endpoint the week-24 effect dwarfs the week-12 effect. And only 7.9% of participants (28 of 356 reporting ethnicity) were from non-white backgrounds, so this UK primary-care trial provides almost no direct data on Fitzpatrick IV to VI skin.

Spironolactone versus long-term antibiotics, and why that comparison matters in India

Beating placebo is one thing; beating the drug currently in use is a harder and more useful test. The FASCE trial (Dréno and colleagues, Acta Dermato-Venereologica, 2024) ran that comparison in 133 adult women with moderate acne across French centres, in a multicentre, randomised, double-blind, parallel-group design. One arm received doxycycline plus benzoyl peroxide for three months, then placebo plus benzoyl peroxide for three months; the other received spironolactone plus benzoyl peroxide for six months.

Spironolactone produced significantly better treatment success at six months (p = 0.007), and was 1.37 times more likely to succeed at month 4 and 2.87 times more likely at month 6 than the doxycycline sequence. Lesion counts and both scoring systems improved more with spironolactone, quality of life was better at months 4 and 6, and the authors describe it as very well tolerated — the first study, they say, to show spironolactone more successful than doxycycline in adult women with moderate acne.

Note what FASCE does not show. Both arms received benzoyl peroxide throughout, so this is spironolactone in combination, not alone. The trial had 133 participants in French centres, and the antibiotic arm switched to placebo at three months — which mirrors guideline advice but is not how long-term antibiotics are actually used in much of India. Even so, a double-blind trial against an active comparator is worth more than a shelf of open-label series.

Dose, side effects and the evidence closest to Indian women

The dosing data most transferable to South Asian women comes from a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of 63 Thai women aged 25 to 45 with moderate adult female acne, run over 12 weeks in Bangkok. All used benzoyl peroxide 2.5% and were randomised to spironolactone 25 mg, 50 mg, or placebo. Total acne counts fell significantly from baseline in all three groups, placebo included — a reminder of how much apparent improvement in acne studies is not attributable to the drug. Only the 50 mg arm achieved a significantly higher success rate than placebo; the 25 mg arm did not separate from it. Serum potassium and creatinine showed no significant change. Adverse events were mild and temporary: menstrual irregularity, breast tenderness, dizziness.

A 2025 meta-analysis restricted to placebo-controlled randomised trials, covering 5 RCTs and 563 patients (251 on spironolactone) with trial sequential analysis, found objective acne improvement at an odds ratio of 6.59 (95% CI 3.50 to 12.43, p<0.00001) with I² = 0% — complete agreement between trials. Subjective, patient-rated assessment came out at OR 5.22 (95% CI 0.62 to 44.24, p = 0.13) with I² = 85%: not significant and wildly heterogeneous. Objective improvement is robust; whether women reliably notice it is not established. On the two side effects most asked about, menstrual irregularity was OR 1.09 (p = 0.88) and breast enlargement OR 1.37 (p = 0.26) — neither significantly more common than placebo.

The 2017 hybrid systematic review pooled serum potassium results and concluded they supported the recommendation that routine monitoring is not required in this patient population, while noting that menstrual side effects were significantly more common at 200 mg per day. Spironolactone is a prescription-only medicine used off-label for acne in most jurisdictions; dose, monitoring and contraception are decisions for a prescribing doctor who knows your history.

The combined oral contraceptive pill: strong against placebo, untested against the alternatives

Combined oral contraceptives have the largest evidence base in this field by an order of magnitude. The Cochrane review pooled 31 trials and 12,579 participants: 6 placebo comparisons, 17 comparisons of one pill against another, and just 1 comparison against an antibiotic. All nine analysable placebo-controlled trials showed reduced lesion counts, severity grades and self-assessed acne. Levonorgestrel-containing pills gave a mean difference of −9.98 total lesions (95% CI −16.51 to −3.45), norgestimate −9.32 (95% CI −14.19 to −4.45), dienogest −15.30 (95% CI −19.98 to −10.62), and drospirenone an odds ratio of 3.02 (95% CI 1.99 to 4.59) for reaching clear or almost clear.

Two null findings deserve more attention than they get. First, few important and consistent differences were found between contraceptive types in their effectiveness for acne — the marketing of one pill as the anti-acne pill is not supported by 17 head-to-head trials. Second, how combined oral contraceptives compare with alternative acne treatments is unknown, because only one trial addressed the question. Like spironolactone, they sit in the conditional tier of the 2024 AAD guideline, and prescribing them involves cardiovascular and personal-history considerations that belong in a consultation.

Marks, scarring and what happens in deeper skin after the lesion settles

For most Indian women the visible consequence of adult acne is not the active spot but what it leaves behind. A cross-sectional study of 269 patients (175 women, 94 men) at a dermatology outpatient clinic in Türkiye found acne scars in 71.3% of them. The finding most relevant to skin of colour is that post-lesional hyperpigmentation and erythema predicted more severe scarring — marks tracked with worse structural outcomes. The same study linked prior topical and antibiotic use to increased scar risk, but that is cross-sectional data and almost certainly confounding by indication.

This is why sequencing matters. The strongest recommendation in the 2024 AAD guideline is aimed at preventing scars, not treating them: oral isotretinoin is strongly recommended for acne that is severe, causing psychosocial burden or scarring, or failing standard therapy. Controlling inflammation comes before texture work. Once acne is quiet, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation is generally addressed before, or alongside, any procedural work on acne scars.

Procedures carry different arithmetic in deeper skin. A retrospective study of 473 superficial chemical peels in Fitzpatrick III to VI skin found an overall complication rate of 3.8%, crusting commonest at 2.3%, with hyperpigmentation and erythema tied at 1.9%. Fitzpatrick VI skin carried an odds ratio of 5.14 (95% CI 1.21 to 21.8) for any adverse event. All complications resolved within eight months, and side effects were significantly less common in winter — in Mumbai terms, the drier, cooler months are a more forgiving window than peak monsoon humidity.

What the evidence does not support

Each of these is contradicted or unsupported by the studies that looked.

  • "Acne on the jawline means you have PCOS." No prospective validation of jawline distribution as a predictor of hyperandrogenism was located, and PCOS diagnosis follows a specific guideline algorithm.
  • "Spironolactone clears hormonal acne." Investigator-assessed treatment success at 12 weeks was 18.5% on the drug versus 5.6% on placebo — a real effect and a modest absolute result.
  • "You will see results within a few weeks." Self-reported improvement at week 12 in SAFA was not statistically significant (OR 1.16, 95% CI 0.70 to 1.91). The convincing effects appeared at week 24.
  • "A low 25 mg dose is enough." In a double-blind placebo-controlled trial in 63 Asian women, only the 50 mg arm separated from placebo. The 25 mg arm did not.
  • "Spironolactone wrecks your cycle and causes breast enlargement." A meta-analysis of five placebo-controlled trials found menstrual irregularity at OR 1.09 (p = 0.88) and breast enlargement at OR 1.37 (p = 0.26) — neither significant against placebo, though menstrual effects were more frequent at 200 mg daily.
  • "This particular pill is the best one for acne." Across 31 trials and 12,579 participants, few important and consistent differences were found between pill types. Whether the pill beats antibiotics or spironolactone is simply unknown: only one trial in the review made such a comparison.
  • "Hormonal therapy is first-line for adult female acne." Both spironolactone and combined oral contraceptives are conditional, not strong, recommendations in the 2024 AAD guideline.
  • "Your old creams and antibiotics caused your scars." That association comes from one cross-sectional study and is almost certainly confounding by indication.
  • "No procedure carries pigmentation risk if the settings are right." In 473 superficial peels in Fitzpatrick III to VI skin, hyperpigmentation still occurred in 1.9%, and Fitzpatrick VI carried five times the odds of any adverse event. Risk can be reduced; it is not removed.

Three questions make any consultation about adult acne more useful: what study design does this recommendation come from, how many women were in it, and how many of them had skin like mine. Here the honest answers are double-blind placebo-controlled trials, a few hundred, and very few — still the best evidence base in acne medicine, and worth knowing its edges.
