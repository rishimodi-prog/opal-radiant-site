Thread Lift vs HIFU vs Fillers: How to Choose, by the Evidence

By Dr. Punit Sakhare, MD Dermatologist · 2026-10-19 · 12 min read

Three procedures dominate the conversation about a softening jawline or a tired midface: thread lifts, HIFU (high-intensity focused ultrasound, sold clinically as microfocused ultrasound with visualisation) and injectable fillers. They are usually presented as rungs on a ladder with surgery at the top. They are not a ladder. They act on different tissue, at different depths, for different problems, and the published evidence behind each one is of very different quality.

The question people actually search is thread lift vs HIFU, and it has an unsatisfying answer: no published trial has ever compared the two directly. A PubMed search conducted in August 2026 returned no head-to-head trials of HIFU versus thread lift. Anyone who tells you one beats the other is offering an opinion, not a finding, and it is worth knowing that before you spend money on either.

What follows is what each of the three has been measured to do, in how many people, and where the numbers stop supporting the sales pitch. This article is not medical advice: a qualified dermatologist should examine your face and name the problem before any of these is recommended.

Thread lift, HIFU and fillers do three different jobs

Thread lifts, HIFU and fillers are not three brands of the same treatment. Each targets a different tissue layer, which is the most useful thing to understand before comparing them.

HIFU delivers focused ultrasound to a fixed depth, creating small columns of thermal coagulation while leaving the epidermis intact. The foundational work was done in cadavers: White and colleagues, in Archives of Facial Plastic Surgery in 2007, delivered 202 exposure lines across six unfixed human cadaveric specimens and produced thermal injury zones selectively in the superficial musculoaponeurotic system at depths up to 7.8 mm. That paper is the origin of the "it targets the same layer a surgeon lifts" line, and three of its authors were affiliated with the device developer. Clinical transducers work at 4.5 mm, 3.0 mm and 1.5 mm.

Thread lifts are mechanical. A barbed absorbable suture -- polydioxanone, poly-L-lactic acid or polycaprolactone -- is passed under the skin to reposition tissue, with a secondary claim that the material provokes collagen deposition along its track as it dissolves.

Fillers add volume and do not tighten anything. A hyaluronic acid gel placed in a hollow replaces missing volume there, which is a different intervention from heating collagen or repositioning tissue.

Thread lift vs HIFU: the comparison has never been run

There is no published head-to-head trial of thread lift versus HIFU; a dedicated PubMed search in August 2026 found none. Every clinic comparison of thread lift vs HIFU, including this one, is therefore assembled from separate single-arm studies with different patients, outcome scales and follow-up lengths -- one of the least reliable things you can do with clinical data.

What does exist is one randomised head-to-head trial involving HIFU and a different energy device. Alhaddad and colleagues, in Dermatologic Surgery in 2019, randomised 20 subjects with mild-to-moderate laxity to receive microfocused ultrasound on one side of the face and monopolar radiofrequency on the other, in the same session, with evaluator-blinded grading over six months. Both sides improved from baseline. There was no statistically significant difference between the two on investigator laxity scores, patient satisfaction or adverse events. The authors noted in the same paper that up to that point no comparative clinical trials had been performed at all.

The honest reading: this field has one small randomised comparison between two energy devices, and none between an energy device and threads. If a plan is sold to you on a comparison, ask which trial it comes from.

What HIFU has actually been measured to do

The best-quality effect size for HIFU is 1.7 mm. Alam and colleagues, in the Journal of the American Academy of Dermatology in 2010, treated 36 patients (35 evaluated, median age 44) in a rater-blinded prospective cohort at an academic centre. Three masked expert raters judged 30 of 35 patients (86%) to show a clinically significant brow lift at day 90, P = .00001, and the mean brow elevation measured 1.7 mm. The same authors stated plainly that they could not quantitatively measure lower-face tightening because there are no fixed anatomic landmarks there. There was no untreated control arm.

Durability is where the picture darkens. Lu and colleagues, in Dermatologic Surgery in 2017, ran the largest quantitative Asian dataset: 25 Taiwanese patients, 800 treatment lines, 3D imaging at 0, 90 and 180 days. Brow lift measured 0.47 mm at 90 days (P = .0165); at 180 days brow height sat 0.12 mm below baseline. Werschler and Werschler, in the Journal of Clinical and Aesthetic Dermatology in 2016, followed 20 open-label patients for a year: subject and physician satisfaction ran at 95%, but blinded assessors rated only 6 of 14 evaluable subjects (43%) as improved. That gap between what people believe happened and what a blinded observer sees is the most important number in the field.

The largest meta-analysis, by Amiri and colleagues in Aesthetic Surgery Journal in 2025, pooled 42 studies. It found 89% of patients showed some degree of investigator-assessed improvement (95% CI 81-94%, n = 411) and satisfaction of any level at 84%. But satisfaction fell to 62% (95% CI 37-82%, n = 172) as soon as studies offered a "neutral" answer option, and the authors wrote that misclassifying neutral responses as positive "may result in an overestimation of the treatment's efficacy." Pooled mean pain was 4.85 out of 10. Our HIFU face lift page sets out how we assess whether that range is worth it for a given face.

What thread lifts have actually been measured to do

Thread lifts have the only controlled long-term study in this whole comparison, and it is not favourable. Abraham, DeFatta and Williams, in Archives of Facial Plastic Surgery in 2009, followed 33 thread-lift patients and 10 non-thread-lift controls (lipotransfer, chemical peel, rhytidectomy) for a mean of 21 months, with blinded assessment by four independent board-certified facial plastic surgeons. Every group improved at one month. Measurable results persisted to the end of the study in every group except the thread-lift-only group. The controls scored better than thread-lift-alone patients (P < .01), and thread lift combined with other procedures scored better than thread lift alone (P < .01). The authors concluded that the thread lift "provides only limited short-term improvement that may be largely attributed to postprocedural edema and inflammation" and that they "cannot justify further use of this procedure for facial rejuvenation."

One caveat should be stated: that study examined the older non-absorbable barbed-thread era, and modern absorbable threads are a different material class it did not test. What it establishes is that a controlled, blinded, 21-month design produced a result nothing like the marketing, and no equivalent controlled study of modern threads has replaced it.

On safety, Ojha and colleagues published a PRISMA systematic review in Dermatologic Surgery in 2025 covering 19 studies, 1,406 patients and 876 reported complications with absorbable sutures. Ecchymosis occurred in 19.49%, swelling in 16.79% and tenderness in 9.96%; haematoma (0.64%), paraesthesia (1.14%) and infection (2.49%) were rarer. Swelling and bruising were more associated with polycaprolactone sutures, barbed threads and midface lifts. That review is often quoted as showing "complication rates under 5%" -- a line that refers only to the serious-complication subset. It reports safety only, not efficacy or durability.

What fillers have actually been measured to do

Fillers are the only one of the three that can restore lost volume, which is the point where a hollow is a volume deficit rather than loose skin. A 2021 systematic review in Dermatologic Surgery concluded that soft tissue fillers and autologous fat grafting are the most effective options for dark circles caused by volume loss.

The numbers are modest and specific. Viana and colleagues, in Arquivos Brasileiros de Oftalmologia in 2011, treated 10 patients with hyaluronic acid gel for tear trough deformity and reported a mean volume of 0.61 mL (SD 0.25) on the right side and 0.65 mL (SD 0.26) on the left, with the effect lasting up to 12 months. That was a pilot with no control group. Complications were bruising, erythema, local swelling and injection-site pain.

Filler under the eye also carries the most serious anatomical risk in this article. Hufschmidt and colleagues, in the Journal of Plastic, Reconstructive and Aesthetic Surgery in 2019, dissected 18 fresh cadavers and mapped the infraorbital artery and its anastomoses with the supratrochlear, dorsal nasal and angular arteries. They identified two danger zones: injections placed too superficially in the midcheek, and injections placed in the periosteal layer during infraorbital hollow or tear-trough correction, because of retrograde embolism risk. Their verbatim recommendation was that "injections to the periosteum layer in tear-trough correction and above the periosteum on the zygomatic arch is not advised." The documented route runs to arterial occlusion, blindness and stroke. A 2024 randomised comparison of 60 patients in the Journal of Cosmetic Dermatology also recorded blue-grey Tyndall discolouration in 3 patients in the hyaluronic-acid-alone arm.

Choose by the problem, not by the treatment name

The most reliable way to choose between thread lift, HIFU and fillers is to name the problem first. The three failure modes seen in clinic -- laxity, volume loss and skin-surface change -- respond to different things.

  • Mild-to-moderate laxity, no volume loss. This is the population every HIFU study enrolled. Friedman and colleagues, in the Journal of Cosmetic Dermatology in 2020, studied 43 patients with focused ultrasound and restricted their conclusion to "selected patients with minor skin sagging and no volume discrepancy," describing the modality as having a good safety profile but limited efficacy. Radiofrequency microneedling is a reasonable alternative in the same group; our MNRF page covers what that involves.
  • A hollow or a deflated midface. This is a volume problem. Heating collagen does not add volume, and a thread cannot conjure tissue that is not there. Filler, placed by someone who knows the vascular anatomy above, is the intervention with a mechanism that matches the deficit.
  • Texture, pigment or dullness rather than sagging. None of these three is the right tool. A chemical peel addresses a different layer entirely.
  • Significant, heavy laxity or jowling. There is no evidence base for HIFU here. Every trial cited above enrolled mild-to-moderate laxity.

Combining approaches is legitimate. A 2020 randomised split-face study of 20 patients in the Journal of Drugs in Dermatology combined microfocused ultrasound with diluted calcium hydroxylapatite and reported marionette line scores improving from 2.47 to 1.80 (P ≤ 0.00003) with 15-month follow-up and histology. Because it tested a combination, it cannot tell you how much came from the ultrasound.

What the evidence does not support

These claims are common in aesthetic advertising for thread lifts, HIFU and fillers, and the published record does not support them.

  • "HIFU is a non-surgical facelift." The US FDA 510(k) clearance for the Ulthera system (K243035, cleared 24 February 2025) covers lifting the eyebrow, lifting lax submental and neck tissue, decolletage lines, and the appearance of laxity on the abdomen and arms. Jawline sculpting, cheek lift and nasolabial fold correction are not on that list. A US clearance is not an Indian approval, and clearance is not approval.
  • "Results last eighteen months to two years." No two-year blinded outcome data exists. The one-year blinded data comes from 14 evaluable people and showed 43% improved.
  • "Thread lifts give a two-to-four year lift." The only controlled study with 21-month blinded follow-up found the thread-lift-only group was the single group with no persisting measurable improvement. The one paper claiming durability of 4.13 years is retrospective, non-randomised and published in a journal of poor methodological standing, and reported 61% pain and 53% swelling within its own cohort.
  • "Thread lift complications are under 5%." The same systematic review that produced that sentence reports ecchymosis at 19.49% and swelling at 16.79%.
  • "HIFU beats radiofrequency" (or the reverse). In the only randomised, split-face, evaluator-blinded head-to-head trial, n = 20, there was no statistically significant difference.
  • "No downtime, completely safe, painless." A 2018 retrospective multicentre case series in Lasers in Surgery and Medicine documented five patients with blistering, erosion and ulceration, subcutaneous oedema with resulting atrophy and cutaneous necrosis after single sessions, concluding that serious adverse events "are nevertheless possible and may be underreported." Pooled mean pain is 4.85 out of 10.
  • "Collagen stimulation is clinically proven." The human histological evidence for HIFU consists of 11 biopsies in one uncontrolled Korean series from 2011 and 10 uncontrolled periorbital patients in 2019, and a widely cited 2020 study titled for "collagen regeneration" took no biopsies at all. As of August 2026 there is no sham-controlled randomised trial of microfocused ultrasound for facial laxity indexed in PubMed.

Indian skin: what has been studied, and what has not

Most patients reading this are Fitzpatrick type IV to VI, and the evidence in that skin is thinner than any clinic website suggests. For HIFU there is exactly one dedicated study: Harris and Sundaram, in JAMA Facial Plastic Surgery in 2015, treated 52 adults of Fitzpatrick phototypes III to VI in an open-label, non-randomised design. Three adverse events occurred -- two raised areas of mild oedema or welting and one case of moderately severe prolonged erythema with mild scabbing -- and all resolved by 90 days without sequelae.

Read that carefully. It is a safety study, not an efficacy study. It shows microfocused ultrasound did not cause post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation or burns in 52 people with deeper skin. It does not show the treatment works as well in brown skin, and no published efficacy trial of HIFU has been conducted in an Indian population. The Asian efficacy data comes from Korean and Taiwanese cohorts of 22, 25 and 10 patients, generally Fitzpatrick III to IV rather than South Asian.

The wider point about deeper skin holds across procedures. In a review of 473 superficial chemical peels in Fitzpatrick III to VI patients published in the Journal of the American Academy of Dermatology in 2018, the overall complication rate was 3.8%, but Fitzpatrick VI carried an odds ratio of 5.14 (95% CI 1.21-21.8) for any adverse event; all events resolved within eight months and were significantly less common in winter. Deeper skin is not a contraindication here. It is a reason to insist that whoever holds the device has treated skin like yours before.

Questions worth asking before you book any of the three

A consultation should answer the following without hesitation.

  • Which problem are we treating? A plan that does not name the problem is a plan built around the machine in the room.
  • What is the realistic size of the change? The best-measured non-surgical numbers are millimetres: 1.7 mm of brow elevation at 90 days, 0.47 mm in the largest Asian dataset. If someone describes a jawline transformation, ask which measurement that comes from.
  • What happens at six months and at one year? There is no published trial comparing one session with two, and none measuring whether an annual top-up maintains the effect. "One or two sessions and a top-up every 12 to 18 months" is clinical convention, not trial evidence.
  • What are the documented adverse events, including the rare ones? For HIFU: welts, paraesthesia, transient nerve paresis and, rarely, blistering, ulceration, fat atrophy and necrosis. For threads, bruising in roughly one in five. For under-eye filler, Tyndall discolouration and a route to vascular occlusion.
  • Who is performing it, and is the device registered? Since October 2022 all medical devices in India are notified as drugs and require CDSCO registration. Operator skill is measurable too: a study of 52 dermatologists found higher accuracy of transducer-skin coupling with real-time visualisation at every treated site, with the largest difference at the upper neck.

None of these three treatments can be promised to produce a specific result, and no honest reading of the literature supports a guarantee. What the evidence supports is a modest, measurable change in the right patient, chosen for the right problem, with expectations set in advance. For that assessment, book a consultation with a qualified dermatologist rather than choosing a treatment from an advertisement. This article is educational and is not medical advice.
