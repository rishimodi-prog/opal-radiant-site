Female Pattern Hair Loss: Staging and Treatment, and What the Evidence Actually Shows

By Dr. Punit Sakhare, MD Dermatologist · 2026-09-27 · 12 min read

Female pattern hair loss is the progressive miniaturisation of scalp follicles over the crown and central parting, and it is the commonest cause of long-term thinning in women. It is not a bad shedding phase, and the distinction matters: the treatments with evidence behind them differ, and the staging scales used in clinics describe severity rather than predicting who responds.

This piece sets out what the published evidence establishes about staging and treatment, and where it runs out. Topical minoxidil is the only intervention with Cochrane-grade support in women, pooled across 8 trials at an average gain of 13.18 hairs per square centimetre over placebo. Finasteride 1 mg was tested in women and did not beat placebo. No population-based prevalence study of the condition exists anywhere in India. And the staging scale most often proposed as an improvement on Ludwig has never been validated in patients. Every figure below carries its sample size.

Pattern hair loss in women: what it is, and what separates it from shedding

Pattern hair loss in women is a gradual, patterned process: follicles in androgen-sensitive areas of the crown and mid-scalp produce progressively finer, shorter hairs over years, so the central parting widens while the frontal hairline is usually preserved. Telogen effluvium is a different event: a diffuse shed following an illness, a delivery, surgery, a crash diet or a new drug, which reverses when the trigger clears. Andrea Rebora's 2019 review in Clinical, Cosmetic and Investigational Dermatology makes the point most clinics skip: effluvium frequently sits on top of pattern loss in the same scalp, often enough that treating only the shed is a common error.

The number most women arrive with is the wrong number. No primary study established 50 to 100 hairs a day as normal; it is hair-cycle arithmetic that hardened into a rule of thumb. The only published normative dataset is Wasko and colleagues in Archives of Dermatology in 2008, who standardised a 60-second comb in 60 healthy men with no alopecia: men aged 20 to 40 shed 0 to 78 hairs, mean 10.2; men aged 41 to 60 shed 0 to 43, mean 10.3. That study is small, single-centre and entirely male, and no equivalent dataset exists for women. A daily hair count cannot tell you whether you have pattern thinning or a reversible shed; the pattern, the parting width and the trichoscopy do.

Staging female pattern hair loss: Ludwig, Sinclair and the FPHL-SI

Staging female pattern hair loss in practice means one of two descriptive scales. Ludwig, published in 1977, sorts central thinning into three grades; Sinclair uses five points and is finer at the mild end. Both grade what is visible at the parting, and neither measures follicle diameter, predicts treatment response, or carries a validated threshold for starting therapy.

The strongest published critique came from Harries and nine co-authors in the Journal of the European Academy of Dermatology and Venereology in 2016, proposing a Female Pattern Hair Loss Severity Index. Their objection: "by the time patients exhibit the full manifestations of 'Ludwig I', they have already lost a significant volume of hair." The FPHL-SI combines shedding, midline density and trichoscopy into a score out of 20, where 0 to 4 makes the diagnosis unlikely and 5 to 9 indicates early-stage disease.

Here is the part that rarely travels with the scale. That paper was an expert consensus exercise involving 9 clinicians across Europe, North America and Australia: no patients enrolled, no control group, no validation study. The authors state that the index "may have to be further optimized after systematic validation in clinical practice." Ten years on, that validation has not been published. It is a way of recording a structured baseline, not a validated instrument.

How common it is in Indian women, and why nobody actually knows

There is no population-based prevalence study of pattern hair loss in Indian women. PubMed returns no community-based Indian epidemiology for this condition, so any percentage quoted for Indian women is either extrapolated from another country or invented. What Indian data exists is clinic-based. Sehgal and colleagues published a hospital series of 35 women in Skinmed in 2013: Ludwig grade I in 16, grade II in 10, grade III in 1, with 6 showing the Olsen "Christmas tree" pattern. Thirty-five patients with no denominator cannot support a prevalence figure.

The nearest usable comparator is Chinese. Wang and colleagues interviewed 17,886 people across six cities for the British Journal of Dermatology in 2010, 15,257 completing. Among women, overall androgenetic alopecia prevalence was 6.0%, rising from 1.3% at ages 18 to 29 to 5.4% at 40 to 49 and 11.8% at 70 and above. A strong dataset for China; applying it to Mumbai is extrapolation.

One genuinely Indian figure is worth carrying. Keen and colleagues examined 100 women with polycystic ovary syndrome in Srinagar for the Indian Dermatology Online Journal in 2017 and found pattern hair loss in 31%, alongside hirsutism in 78% and acne in 48%. There was no non-PCOS control group, so the reading is that roughly a third of that cohort had pattern loss, not that a third of Indian women with hair loss have PCOS. The link between androgen excess and scalp thinning is real; the prevalence arithmetic runs only one way.

What an assessment should include, and the limits of counting hairs

A competent assessment for pattern thinning is a history, a scalp examination with trichoscopy, and targeted bloods only where the history points at them. Trichoscopy, dermoscopy of the scalp, shows hair-shaft diameter variability before the parting visibly widens. What it cannot do is settle a diagnosis alone, and there is a measured number for that. Guarrera, Fiorucci and Rebora compared TrichoScan against the modified wash test in 41 patients with hair loss for Experimental Dermatology in 2013. Agreement between clinical diagnosis and the wash test was kappa 0.32, rated "fair"; clinical diagnosis versus TrichoScan was kappa 0.22, and the two instrumental methods agreed with each other in only 17 of 41 patients, or 41%. Use them to track one patient against her own baseline on the same device, not to adjudicate what she has.

Reflex vitamin panels have less support than most women are told. Durusu Turkoglu and colleagues compared 90 women with chronic telogen effluvium against 90 controls in the Journal of Cosmetic Dermatology in 2024, measuring haemoglobin, ferritin, vitamin B12, thyroid function, vitamin D, copper and biotin. There was no difference on any of them; only zinc was lower in patients, and selenium higher. Their conclusion: "nutritional deficiencies are not as common as thought in patients diagnosed with TE." Menstrual irregularity, hirsutism, acne and weight change justify a hormonal workup; a widening parting alone does not justify a panel of twelve.

Minoxidil: the only treatment with Cochrane-grade evidence in women

Topical minoxidil is the anchor of medical treatment for pattern thinning in women, on the strength of a 2016 Cochrane review by van Zuuren, Fedorowicz and Schoones covering 47 randomised trials and 5,290 participants. The review is honest about its inputs: only 5 of those trials were at low risk of bias, 26 unclear and 16 at high risk. Participant-rated moderate-to-marked regrowth occurred in 157 of 593 women on minoxidil against 77 of 555 on placebo, a risk ratio of 1.93 (95% CI 1.51 to 2.47), graded moderate-quality. Investigator-rated improvement across 7 studies and 1,181 women gave RR 2.35 (95% CI 1.68 to 3.28). Total hair count rose 13.18 hairs per square centimetre over placebo (95% CI 10.92 to 15.44) across 8 studies and 1,242 women, on evidence graded low-quality. Adverse events with 2% twice daily occurred in 40 of 407 women versus 28 of 320 on placebo, RR 1.24 (95% CI 0.82 to 1.87), not a significant difference.

The 5% question is settled better than the marketing suggests. Four head-to-head trials totalling 1,006 women found no difference in regrowth, and total hair count across 3 trials and 631 women gave a mean difference of −2.12 hairs per square centimetre (95% CI −5.47 to 1.23). Adverse events were equivalent, RR 1.02. The one place 5% won was Lucky and colleagues' 48-week trial of 381 women in the Journal of the American Academy of Dermatology in 2004, where it beat 2% on patients' own assessment of benefit but not on hair count, and caused more pruritus, irritation and hypertrichosis. These trials ran 24 to 48 weeks, response varies between individuals, and the gains recorded were gains during continuous use only.

One Indian nuance, flagged for what it is: Chitalia and colleagues assayed follicular minoxidil sulfotransferase activity in 120 pattern-hair-loss patients at a Mumbai teaching hospital in 2018 and found low activity in 40.8% overall and 26.6% of women — a plausible explanation for non-response, from an uncontrolled study whose co-authors are affiliated with a company selling the test.

Anti-androgens, oral minoxidil and finasteride: what is used without trial support

Beyond topical minoxidil, most of what is prescribed for pattern thinning in women runs ahead of the evidence. Finasteride 1 mg is the clearest case: three randomised trials pooled in the 2016 Cochrane review found improvement in 30 of 67 women on finasteride against 33 of 70 on placebo, RR 0.95 (95% CI 0.66 to 1.37), investigator assessment RR 0.77 (95% CI 0.31 to 1.90). Cochrane's verdict: no more effective than placebo, on low-quality evidence. It is still prescribed to women off-label.

Spironolactone has never been tested against placebo in a randomised trial for this condition; Cochrane called for such trials in 2016 and none has been published. What exists is retrospective and points both ways. Jha and colleagues compared spironolactone 100 mg daily against bicalutamide 50 mg daily in 120 Indian women in Patna for the Australasian Journal of Dermatology in 2024: mean reduction in Sinclair severity score at 24 weeks was 19.51% versus 28.20%, favouring bicalutamide, though global photographic assessment did not reach significance (P = 0.139). Gil-Redondo and colleagues then followed 187 women for 24 months in Madrid and found the opposite ordering: at least one grade of Sinclair improvement in 54.4% on spironolactone versus 37.3% on bicalutamide (P = 0.030).

Low-dose oral minoxidil is now widely used. The largest dataset is a retrospective multicentre safety study of 1,404 patients, 943 of them women, published in 2021: hypertrichosis 15.1%, fluid retention 1.3%, tachycardia 0.9%, discontinuation for adverse events 1.7%, no life-threatening events. Reassuring on tolerability and silent on efficacy, since it had no control group. These are prescription decisions needing a dermatologist and monitoring.

PRP and GFC in women: what the trials measured

Platelet-rich plasma and growth factor concentrate are the injectables most often offered for thinning hair in Indian clinics, and the evidence in women is thinner than in men, which is itself weak. It starts with a negative trial: Puig, Reese and Peters ran a double-blind, multicentre, saline-controlled study of PRP in women for Dermatologic Surgery in 2016 and found hair mass index and hair count did not differ significantly between the PRP and placebo groups, with substantial subjective improvement in 13.3% of treated women against 0% on placebo. Their conclusion names the gap: a therapeutic advantage "as perceived by patients but not according to hair count or HMI." A 2020 randomised trial of 30 women by Dubin and colleagues did report large gains, +105.9 hairs per square centimetre against −52.4 in the saline arm at week 24 — but a placebo arm losing 52 hairs per square centimetre in six months is an outlier, and a critique of its phototrichogram analysis followed in the same journal.

Head-to-head against minoxidil, PRP has not won. A Mayo Clinic randomised crossover trial of 20 women in 2020 found minoxidil foam improved terminal hair density (P = .004) and cumulative hair thickness (P = .004), two endpoints PRP did not move, while quality-of-life scores improved after PRP and not after minoxidil; the authors concluded PRP is effective "although not as effective as minoxidil." A 2023 meta-analysis of 10 randomised trials found the density effect stronger in male-only than mixed-sex trials (P = 0.02), with uncertainty flagged "especially in the case of female." The honest framing for PRP in thinning hair is an add-on to medical treatment, not a replacement.

Growth factor concentrate is marketed as the newer, better version. The entire GFC-specific literature indexed in PubMed as of August 2026 is three records: a retrospective chart review of 42 patients, a five-patient case series indexed as case reports, and a commentary — 47 patients, none randomised, blinded or placebo-controlled. And no trial in the PRP or GFC literature reports Fitzpatrick phototype, so nothing has been shown specifically in Fitzpatrick IV to VI skin.

What the evidence does not support

The claims most often made about treating pattern hair loss in women, each with the study that undermines it.

  • "5% minoxidil grows more hair than 2% in women." Four trials, 1,006 women, no difference in regrowth; total hair count mean difference −2.12 (95% CI −5.47 to 1.23). The 5% advantage in Lucky 2004 was on patients' own opinion, with more irritation and hypertrichosis.
  • "Finasteride works for women too." Three randomised trials: 30 of 67 improved versus 33 of 70 on placebo, RR 0.95 (95% CI 0.66 to 1.37) — no more effective than placebo.
  • "Low ferritin is why your hair is falling, and iron will fix it." The largest controlled study, Olsen and colleagues in 2010, compared 381 women with pattern hair loss or chronic telogen effluvium against 76 controls. At ferritin at or below 15 µg/L in premenopausal women, deficiency was present in 12.4% and 12.1% of patients against 29.8% of controls — the controls were more iron-deficient. No randomised trial of iron supplementation for hair loss exists, and the widely quoted target of "ferritin above 70" is a recommendation in a 2002 narrative review whose author wrote that the right level "is yet to be definitively established."
  • "Biotin strengthens hair." A 2017 systematic review found 18 reported cases of biotin benefiting hair or nails, every one in a patient with an underlying deficiency or pathology, and no evidence for supplementation in people who are not deficient. Serum and urine biotin did not differ between 90 women with chronic effluvium and 90 controls.
  • "GFC is clinically proven, and better than PRP." Forty-seven patients across two uncontrolled studies and a commentary is not an evidence base; the only meta-analysis touching GFC reports I² above 90% and calls its own findings hypothesis-generating.
  • "Laser combs regrow hair." Cochrane pooled 2 sham-controlled studies in 141 women: participant-rated improvement gave RR 1.54 (95% CI 0.96 to 2.49) and RR 1.18 (95% CI 0.74 to 1.89), neither significant.
  • "X% of Indian women have this condition." No population-based Indian prevalence study exists; any figure is imported from Chinese data or from a 35-woman clinic series.

Comorbidity signals, and what a realistic plan looks like

One Indian study is worth knowing and easy to over-read. Verma and colleagues assessed cardiovascular risk factors in 50 premenopausal women with pattern hair loss at a Delhi hospital for the Indian Journal of Dermatology in 2024: metabolic syndrome in 24%, insulin resistance 34%, raised LDL 62%, diabetes 16% and elevated hs-CRP 42%, with metabolic syndrome, BMI and waist circumference all correlating with Ludwig grade, while carotid intima-media thickness stayed normal. With 50 patients and no control group, that is an association within a series, not evidence that pattern hair loss causes or predicts cardiovascular disease, and not a basis for a screening protocol.

A realistic plan follows. Confirm the pattern by examination and trichoscopy rather than by hair counting, and record a baseline photograph and severity grade so change can be measured against something. Start with topical minoxidil, the only option with pooled randomised support in women, and treat it as ongoing rather than a course with an end date. Investigate hormonal and metabolic causes where the history points at them. Take oral drugs and anti-androgens as prescription decisions made with a dermatologist, knowing the evidence behind them is retrospective and contradictory. Regard injectables as an adjunct that may improve density in early to moderate disease. None of the figures here is a promise of an outcome.

A dermatologist consultation is where a structured assessment happens. This article is educational and is not medical advice. Pattern hair loss has several mimics, and what suits you depends on an in-person assessment by a qualified dermatologist.
