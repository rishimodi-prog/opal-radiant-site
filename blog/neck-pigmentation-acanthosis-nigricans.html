Dark Neck Treatment: When It Is Cosmetic and When It Is a Health Signal

By Dr. Punit Sakhare, MD Dermatologist · 2026-10-08 · 12 min read

A dark neck is one of the most common pigmentation complaints brought to Indian dermatology clinics, and it is one of the few skin concerns where the correct first step may not be a treatment at all. Darkening across the back and sides of the neck is frequently acanthosis nigricans, which a 2020 review in the Journal of Cosmetic Dermatology defines as symmetric, velvety, hyperpigmented plaques in body folds rather than pigment sitting flat on the surface. In an Indian cross-sectional series of 87 untreated patients aged 18 to 60, every single patient had neck lesions and 55 of them (63.2%) had measurable insulin resistance.

That makes dark neck treatment a two-part question rather than one. Some neck darkening is genuinely cosmetic: friction from collars and chains, post-inflammatory pigment left behind by a rash or by shaving and threading, or simple sun exposure on an area most people never apply sunscreen to. Some of it is a visible metabolic sign, and the appropriate response is a blood test before a peel. This article separates the two, quotes the numbers behind each claim, and states plainly where the published evidence is thin, because on this topic it is genuinely thin. This is not medical advice; a qualified dermatologist should examine your neck in person before anything is started.

What darkening on the neck actually is

Neck darkening is not a single condition, and the four common patterns behave differently enough that grouping them together is the main reason people waste money. The first is acanthosis nigricans: symmetrical, velvety, slightly thickened brown-to-black skin, usually at the back and sides of the neck, often with exaggerated skin creases and skin tags. The 2020 review by Das and colleagues describes it as arising from multifactorial stimulation of keratinocyte and fibroblast proliferation, which is why the skin feels different, not merely looks different. If you run a finger over it and the texture has changed, you are almost certainly not dealing with surface pigment.

The second pattern is post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, or PIH: flat brown marks that follow the shape of something that was inflamed first, such as folliculitis from shaving, a fragrance or hair-dye reaction, prickly heat, or eczema. The third is sun-related and constitutional darkening, which sits on the exposed front and sides and spares the shaded skin under the jaw. The fourth is melasma extending onto the neck, which behaves like the facial condition rather than a neck problem. You can read the fuller account of that on our pigmentation and melasma page. Texture is the most useful check: velvety and thickened points to acanthosis nigricans, flat and smooth to pigment alone.

Acanthosis nigricans: the velvety, thickened dark neck

Acanthosis nigricans is the condition most often behind a persistently dark neck in India, and the 2020 Journal of Cosmetic Dermatology review by Das and colleagues is the most complete published account of it. The review classifies it into benign, obesity-associated, syndromic, malignant, acral, unilateral, medication-induced and mixed types, occurring at the axilla, neck, inframammary folds and groin. It names HOMA-IR as the recommended assessment tool for insulin resistance in these patients, which places a blood test rather than a skin treatment at the front of the pathway.

Two things about that review need saying clearly, because clinic marketing tends to skip both. First, it is a literature review, not a trial, so its treatment list is drawn from small series and case reports rather than randomised comparisons. It records topical retinoids, vitamin D analogues, keratolytics, oral retinoids, insulin sensitisers, trichloroacetic acid peels and several lasers as reported treatments. Reported is not proven, and presenting laser for acanthosis nigricans as evidence-based would misrepresent what exists. Second, the authors' own conclusion is unusually blunt for a cosmetic-dermatology journal: complete cure and disappearance of lesions are difficult to achieve, weight reduction is the most scientific and practical management strategy, and long-term studies are still warranted. That is a description of the published evidence, and it is the reason any responsible plan for this condition starts with a physician assessment rather than a course of sessions.

The metabolic question: two Indian studies that disagree

Whether a dark neck signals insulin resistance is a genuinely contested question, and the two most relevant Indian studies reach different conclusions about how far the signal goes. Nithun and colleagues published a controlled cross-sectional study in the Indian Dermatology Online Journal in 2019: 60 patients with acanthosis nigricans aged 2 to 24, of any body weight, compared against 30 age- and sex-matched normal-weight controls, using the HOMA2-IR calculator with insulin resistance defined as HOMA2-IR above 1.8. Mean HOMA2-IR was 2.422 in cases against 1.322 in controls, a statistically significant gap. Overweight cases carried 2.5 times and obese cases 11.25 times the odds of insulin resistance on logistic regression. The finding that matters most for a lean reader is that the association held in normal-weight cases against controls at p = 0.045. A dark, velvety neck in someone who is not overweight is still worth investigating.

Banti and colleagues, publishing in 2022 from Bangalore, studied 87 untreated patients aged 18 to 60 without diabetes and found the opposite on severity. Insulin resistance was present in 55 of 87 (63.2%), and acanthosis nigricans grade tracked body mass index (p = 0.002) and waist-hip ratio (p = 0.016). But insulin resistance itself, the QUICKI index and triglycerides showed no significant association with the severity of the lesions or the number of affected sites (all p > 0.05). Their stated conclusion is that the association between insulin resistance and severity is not strong enough to qualify acanthosis nigricans as a screening tool for insulin resistance. That study had no control group, which limits it. Nithun's did, and found severity associations at the neck (grade 4 severity p = 0.007, grade 3 texture p = 0.001). The two studies conflict, and anyone telling you otherwise is quoting only one of them.

When a dark neck needs a doctor before a clinic

A dark neck is usually benign, but the Das 2020 review lists malignant acanthosis nigricans as a recognised type, and that single fact changes how a first appointment should be structured. Acanthosis nigricans that appears suddenly, spreads quickly, becomes extensive, involves the mucosa, or arrives alongside unexplained weight loss is a reason for medical evaluation, not for a lightening peel. Rapid onset in an adult who has not gained weight is the pattern that most warrants attention.

There are three other situations where assessment comes first. A single, solitary, new or changing dark patch on the neck is not the same problem as symmetrical flexural darkening, and it needs to be looked at properly rather than treated cosmetically. Darkening that started after a new medication began is worth reviewing with the prescriber, since the Das review names medication-induced acanthosis nigricans as a distinct type. And darkening accompanied by irregular menstrual cycles, a strong family history of type 2 diabetes, or a rising waist measurement is a reason to ask for fasting glucose and insulin testing, because in the Banti series roughly 84% of patients were overweight or obese and grade tracked waist-hip ratio. Treating neck acanthosis nigricans as a purely cosmetic problem, without any metabolic assessment, is difficult to defend clinically. If you would like that assessment before anything else, you can book a dermatologist consultation and start there.

What dark neck treatment can realistically do

Dark neck treatment has a much smaller evidence base than the marketing around it suggests, and the honest place to start is with how few studies exist. The closest well-conducted mapping of the field is the 2025 systematic review by Vahabi and colleagues in the Journal of Cosmetic Dermatology, which searched PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science and Embase for treatments of axillary hyperpigmentation, the flexural site most comparable to the neck. Only ten studies met inclusion criteria in total. The review opens by stating that there is no standard treatment. That is the size of the field: ten studies, no pooled sample, no meta-analysis possible, and no Indian-population data highlighted.

Within those ten, some things did show pigment reduction. Niacinamide, sweet orange extract, Perilla frutescens leaf extract, Cyperus rotundus oil and glycolic acid produced measurable improvement, with C. rotundus oil noted for effectiveness with minimal side effects. Q-switched Nd:YAG laser was effective across three studies with no severe adverse effects reported, though more painful than intense pulsed light. The review also lists desonide, a topical corticosteroid, among the effective topicals, and that finding should not be turned into a recommendation for Indian readers, for reasons covered below.

Chemical peels have better Indian data, though it comes from facial melasma rather than the neck. Sarkar and colleagues randomised 90 Indian patients across three peel regimens in Dermatologic Surgery in 2016 and recorded 62.36% MASI reduction with 35% glycolic acid and 60.98% with salicylic-mandelic at 12 weeks, with the salicylic-mandelic combination better tolerated and judged more suitable for Indian skin. Note the confound the authors themselves record: every arm was primed for four weeks with hydroquinone and tretinoin, so these are not peel-alone results. If a chemical peel is proposed for your neck, that distinction is worth asking about.

What the evidence does not support

Several claims commonly attached to dark neck treatment have been tested and have not held up, and this section exists because stating that is more useful than repeating them.

  • That the darkness of your neck tells you how insulin resistant you are. Banti 2022, in 87 Indian patients, found no significant association between insulin resistance, QUICKI or triglycerides and either lesion severity or the number of sites involved (p > 0.05). A darker neck is not a worse metabolic result, and a lighter one after treatment is not a metabolic improvement.
  • That laser for acanthosis nigricans is evidence-based. The Das 2020 review lists long-pulsed alexandrite, fractional 1550 nm erbium fibre and CO2 lasers as reported treatments, but these come from small series and case reports, not controlled trials. No randomised trial establishing laser efficacy for neck acanthosis nigricans appears in this literature.
  • That intravenous glutathione lightens skin. The 2025 systematic review by Sarkar and colleagues in the International Journal of Dermatology found only one placebo-controlled study of intravenous glutathione, with 6 of 16 responding (37.5%) versus 3 (18.7%) on placebo at p = 0.054, which is not statistically significant. The authors conclude that intravenous glutathione is contraindicated for lack of efficacy and side effects. Oral and topical forms produced lightening the authors described as moderate and unsustainable.
  • That an over-the-counter lightening cream is a safe shortcut. Saraswat and colleagues screened 2,926 patients with facial dermatoses across 12 Indian centres and found 433 (14.8%) using topical corticosteroids, most commonly for cosmetic skin lightening (126 patients, 29%). Adverse effects occurred in 392 of 433, or 90.5%. Hydroquinone has been prescription-only in India under Schedule H since 1 April 2019 and is not something to source independently.
  • That turning the energy up produces a better result. Kar and colleagues randomised 75 Indian patients across low-fluence Q-switched Nd:YAG, high-fluence Q-switched Nd:YAG and glycolic acid peel for pigmentation, and the high-fluence arm performed significantly worse than both the low-fluence laser and the peel. More aggressive settings bought side effects, not results.
  • That scrubbing removes it. No study supports mechanical abrasion for flexural pigmentation, and the mechanism argues against it: friction and irritation are among the recognised triggers of post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation in the first place.

Why deeper Indian skin changes the risk calculation

Most readers of this article have Fitzpatrick type IV to VI skin, and the risk numbers for pigmentation procedures are different in that range, which is why a treatment plan borrowed from a European clinic is not transferable. The clearest figure comes from Vemula and colleagues, who retrospectively analysed 473 superficial chemical peel treatments in Fitzpatrick III to VI skin and published the results in the Journal of the American Academy of Dermatology in 2018. The overall complication rate was low at 3.8%, with crusting at 2.3% and post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation and erythema tied at 1.9%. All side effects resolved within eight months. But Fitzpatrick type VI carried an odds ratio of 5.14 (95% CI 1.21 to 21.8, p = 0.0118) for any adverse event, and side effects were significantly less common in winter.

Two practical consequences follow. Deeper skin tones are not excluded from peels; the study's own conclusion is that superficial peels in types III to VI had a relatively low complication rate. But the margin for aggressive settings is narrower, and the seasonal finding is a real argument for starting a course in the cooler months rather than in a Mumbai May. Neck skin compounds this further: it is thinner than facial skin and subject to daily friction from collars, chains and hair. Where shaving or threading of the neck is contributing to recurrent folliculitis and the pigment that follows it, addressing the hair itself with laser hair reduction may reduce the inflammatory trigger, though no trial has measured that specific chain of cause and effect.

Post-inflammatory darkening on the neck, and how well it responds

Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation is the second common reason for a dark neck, and its treatment literature is worth quoting accurately because the honest summary is not encouraging. Mar and colleagues published a PRISMA systematic review in the Journal of Cutaneous Medicine and Surgery in 2024 covering 48 studies and 1,356 individuals with skin of colour. Fitzpatrick IV made up 40% of the population and type V a further 34%, so this is a directly relevant group. In 89% of cases the pigmentation was precipitated by an inflammatory condition.

The most-used interventions were topical retinoids, used in 22% of studies with partial improvement in 85% of patients, and laser therapy, used in 17% with partial improvement in 66%. Laser was the only intervention that produced complete resolution in a subgroup, at 26%, but the review also records cases of post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation being made worse by laser. Chemical peels (9%) and hydroquinone (7%) had less effective outcomes. The authors' own conclusion is the sentence to hold on to: their results show a lack of robust efficacy across all treatment modalities. Roughly one in four achieving complete clearance with the best-performing option, and a real possibility of aggravation, is the realistic frame for treating post-inflammatory neck pigment. It also explains why controlling whatever caused the inflammation matters more than the pigment treatment that follows.

What a sensible plan looks like

A defensible approach to a dark neck runs in a specific order, and the order is what distinguishes a clinical plan from a package. It starts with examination and history: is the skin velvety and thickened or flat and smooth; is it symmetrical; how quickly did it appear; what products, medications and hair-removal habits are involved. Where the pattern fits acanthosis nigricans, metabolic assessment follows, because HOMA-IR is what the Das 2020 review names as the assessment tool and because 63.2% of Banti's Indian patients had measurable insulin resistance. Anything done to the skin sits after that step, not instead of it.

Where a topical or procedural course is appropriate, the evidence supports being conservative rather than aggressive: gentler settings outperformed stronger ones in Kar's 75 Indian patients, superficial peels carried a 3.8% complication rate in Vemula's 473 treatments, and winter treatment carried fewer side effects. Daily broad-spectrum photoprotection applied to the neck, not just the face, is a low-cost part of any plan. Expect a course rather than a session, expect partial rather than complete change, and expect maintenance, since the Das review states that complete disappearance of lesions is difficult to achieve and that the underlying driver has to be addressed for anything to hold. Session counts and current ranges are on our pricing page, and those move over time. None of this is a substitute for an in-person assessment by a qualified dermatologist, and if your neck darkening appeared suddenly or is spreading, that assessment should happen before any cosmetic treatment is booked.
