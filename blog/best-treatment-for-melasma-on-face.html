Best Treatment for Melasma on Face: The Ladder, Ranked by Evidence

By Dr. Punit Sakhare, MD Dermatologist · 2026-09-03 · 11 min read

Quick Facts

Ask what the best treatment for melasma on face is and the answer usually comes back as a device name. The published evidence says something else, and it has said it consistently for a decade: the treatments with the strongest trial support for facial melasma are prescription creams, not machines. A 2020 systematic review in the American Journal of Clinical Dermatology by McKesey, Tovar-Garza and Pandya covered 113 randomised and controlled trials with 6,897 participants and concluded that triple combination cream and hydroquinone monotherapy are both the most effective and the best studied options, while chemical peels and laser or light devices are equal or inferior to topicals and carry a higher risk of adverse effects.

That is an awkward finding for an aesthetic clinic to publish. It is also the finding. What follows is the ladder in order, with the sample size, the effect size and the funding source attached to each rung, and with the places where two well-conducted trials disagree left visible. It ends with what melasma treatment demonstrably does not do, because the relapse figures measured over one and two years are the ones most often missing. None of this is medical advice: melasma is a medical diagnosis, the drugs that treat it best are prescription drugs, and you should consult a qualified dermatologist before acting on anything here.

What Melasma Is, and Why That Determines the Order of Treatment

Melasma is a chronic, relapsing pigmentary disorder producing symmetrical brown to grey-brown patches across the cheeks, forehead, upper lip and jawline. It affects women far more often than men and usually appears from the late twenties onwards. Melasma is not a stain sitting on the skin surface, and that single fact explains most of the treatment hierarchy.

Histology of affected skin shows more than extra epidermal pigment: solar elastosis in the dermal matrix, damage to the basement membrane separating epidermis from dermis, increased dermal blood vessels and increased mast cells. Three consequences follow. Treatment quietens melanocytes rather than removing them. A damaged basement membrane lets melanin and melanocytes drop into the dermis, where creams cannot reach them. And the drivers, which include sunlight, visible light, heat and hormones, carry on regardless.

The Indian relapse data is blunt about what that means. A retrospective cohort of 957 patients followed over five years at PGIMER Chandigarh, published by Kumaran and colleagues in 2021, found patients on oral tranexamic acid mostly relapsed by six weeks after stopping, patients on triple combination cream at around two months, and non-hydroquinone regimens at around four months. Response rates at 16 weeks in that cohort were 33.6% for triple combination cream and 40.9% for the non-hydroquinone regimens. That study was uncontrolled and retrospective, which weakens it as a measure of efficacy, but it is the largest Indian long-term follow-up and its relapse timings are the honest numbers to plan around.

The Best Treatment for Melasma on Face: What Two Systematic Reviews Put First

Two independent evidence syntheses have ranked melasma treatments, and they agree on the top of the ladder even where they disagree elsewhere. McKesey and colleagues pooled 113 randomised and controlled trials with 6,897 participants and put triple combination cream, which contains hydroquinone, tretinoin and a corticosteroid, and hydroquinone alone at the top, with peels and light-based devices equal to or worse than topicals and carrying more adverse effects. Its stated limitations are real: heterogeneous designs, small samples, almost no long-term follow-up anywhere in the field.

The second is a network meta-analysis of 59 randomised controlled trials by Liu and colleagues, published in Frontiers in Medicine in 2021. Ranked against placebo for short-term efficacy, its top five ran 1064 nm Q-switched Nd:YAG, intense pulsed light, ablative fractional laser, triple combination cream and topical vitamin C, with picosecond laser and hydroquinone near the bottom of fourteen modalities. Read alone that looks like a case for lasers. The authors did not draw it: their own summary named triple combination cream the most favourable topical and oral tranexamic acid the most favourable systemic option.

The reason is that this network meta-analysis measured MASI, the standard melasma severity score, at the end of treatment. It did not measure relapse. A device that clears pigment fastest at twelve weeks and returns it by month twenty-four ranks first on that outcome and last on the one that matters. The same paper reported side-effect rates in trial arms with more than 80 participants, and that order is close to reversed: tretinoin 10.1%, oral tranexamic acid 17.6%, hydroquinone 18.2%, ablative fractional laser 20.0%, Q-switched Nd:YAG 21.5%, triple combination cream 25.7%, topical tranexamic acid 36.75%, peels 38.0% and microneedling 52.3%.

Tier 1: Prescription Topicals, and the Numbers Behind Them

Prescription topicals are the first rung of the melasma ladder in both systematic reviews, and triple combination cream is the best-evidenced item on it. The most useful trial for the question patients actually ask, which is how long results last, is a randomised, investigator-blinded, controlled study by Arellano and colleagues across 16 centres in Brazil and Mexico. After eight weeks of daily triple combination cream, 78.8% of participants had no or mild melasma. The 242 who cleared entered a six-month maintenance phase on either twice-weekly or tapering application. At the end of it 53% were still relapse-free, and median time to relapse was about 190 days. The trial was funded by the cream's manufacturer, and it was investigator-blinded rather than double-blind.

Read that figure carefully, because it is the most honest single number in melasma: roughly half of the people who cleared completely and kept applying maintenance cream relapsed within six months anyway. Baseline severity at study entry, not severity when maintenance began, predicted who relapsed.

Two Indian points matter here. Hydroquinone has been a Schedule H prescription-only drug in India since 1 April 2019, so over-the-counter sale of hydroquinone-containing topicals is prohibited. And the corticosteroid inside triple combination cream is there to limit irritation from the other two ingredients over a defined course, not as a pigment treatment in its own right; steroid creams bought informally and used on the face for months produce atrophy, telangiectasia and steroid-induced acne rather than durable results. One further finding from the Liu network meta-analysis belongs in any plan: of its 31 trials comparing a combination against a single agent, 87% found the combination superior.

Tier 2: Oral Tranexamic Acid, and the Trial That Disagrees

Oral tranexamic acid is the best-evidenced systemic treatment for moderate to severe or recurrent facial melasma, and the strongest single trial for an Indian audience was run in this city. Minni and Poojary, in the Journal of the European Academy of Dermatology and Venereology in 2020, ran a prospective, triple-blind, placebo-controlled trial at a Mumbai medical college: 130 randomised, 120 completed, 12 weeks of treatment and 24 weeks of follow-up. At week 12, 65.6% of the tranexamic acid plus triple combination cream group had more than 75% improvement in modified MASI, against 27.1% on cream plus placebo. At week 24, recurrence was 18.0% versus 64.4%.

Those are strong numbers from a properly blinded, placebo-controlled trial in Fitzpatrick IV to V skin, and they are not the whole picture. A parallel-group randomised trial by Lajevardi and colleagues, blinded at both assessor and analyst level, added oral tranexamic acid to 4% hydroquinone in 100 patients and found mean MASI 1.8 points lower in the tranexamic acid arm (95% CI 0.36 to 3.24, p = 0.015) but relapse of 30% with the drug versus 26% without, which was not significant. Two well-designed trials, two answers: oral tranexamic acid clearly improves severity, and whether it reduces relapse is genuinely contested.

On safety, the largest cohort is a retrospective review of 561 patients at Singapore's National Skin Centre by Lee, Thng and Goh: improvement in 89.7%, relapse in 27.2%, adverse events in 7.1%, mostly transient. One patient developed a deep vein thrombosis and was later diagnosed with familial protein S deficiency. That single case is why oral tranexamic acid is a prescribed drug requiring a clotting-risk history, and why pregnancy, a personal or family history of thrombosis and combined hormonal contraception belong in the conversation before it is started.

Tier 3: Chemical Peels and Low-Fluence Laser Toning in Indian Skin

Chemical peels sit below topicals for facial melasma in both systematic reviews, and the Indian trial data supports that placement without dismissing peels. The cleanest Indian comparison is a randomised three-arm study by Kar, Gupta and Chauhan in 75 patients, published in the Indian Journal of Dermatology, Venereology and Leprology in 2012, setting low-fluence Q-switched Nd:YAG against a glycolic acid peel against high-fluence Q-switched Nd:YAG over 12 weeks. All three arms improved significantly. The order was low-fluence laser first, glycolic acid peel second, high-fluence laser last, and the gap between the gentlest and most aggressive arm was significant (p < 0.005). There was no untreated control arm, so the ranking between arms, not the absolute effect size, is the usable result.

That is the most important finding in this article for Fitzpatrick IV to VI skin. Turning the energy up did not clear melasma faster. It produced the worst outcome of the three. Melasma melanocytes are hyper-reactive, and a procedure generating significant inflammation can generate more pigment than it removes.

Peels also carry a real adverse-event burden: in the Liu network meta-analysis, peel arms with more than 80 participants reported a 38.0% side-effect rate, second only to microneedling. That argues for superficial peels at conservative concentrations, widely spaced, in someone already on topicals and photoprotection, rather than as a standalone course. If you are weighing up a chemical peel for melasma, ask which acid, at what strength, at what interval, and what is suppressing melanocyte activity between sessions.

Tier 4: Lasers and Devices, Strong at Twelve Weeks and Poor at Two Years

Lasers for melasma show the widest gap in this field between short-term and long-term data. On short-term MASI reduction, 1064 nm Q-switched Nd:YAG ranked first of fourteen modalities in the Liu network meta-analysis. On anything measured beyond six months, the picture inverts.

The best long-term laser evidence available is a randomised, split-face controlled study with two-year follow-up by Zhou, Li, Hamblin and Wen, published in Lasers in Surgery and Medicine in 2024. It set a 755 nm picosecond alexandrite laser against 1064 nm Q-switched Nd:YAG on opposite halves of the same face, three treatments a month apart, in 20 patients of whom 17 were analysed. At six months, modified MASI was significantly below baseline on the Q-switched Nd:YAG side only (p = 0.022); the picosecond side showed no statistically significant difference from baseline at all. Q-switched Nd:YAG was also less painful (p = 0.014). At two years, 10 of the 17 patients, or 58.8%, had recurrence or worsening, with no difference between the two lasers.

Seventeen patients is a small study and should be read as one. It is also the only methodologically sound two-year follow-up of laser treatment for melasma in the literature, and its result matches the Indian relapse data. Low-fluence laser toning still has a legitimate role as an adjunct, and Kar's Indian trial ranked it first of three arms, but it is not a substitute for the topical rung and carries complications of its own, including mottled hypopigmentation and rebound darkening.

Photoprotection Is a Rung on the Ladder, Not an Afterthought

Photoprotection in melasma is a treatment, not general advice, and the trial that demonstrates it changed only the sunscreen. Castanedo-Cazares and colleagues randomised 68 patients, 61 of whom completed, into a double-blind eight-week study in which every participant received 4% hydroquinone and a sunscreen of SPF 50 or above. The only difference was that one sunscreen contained iron oxides and blocked visible light as well as ultraviolet, while the other blocked ultraviolet alone. The visible-light-blocking arm improved 15% more on MASI, 28% more on colorimetric measurement of skin lightness, and 4% more on histological melanin.

The 4% histological difference is modest and the study was single-centre with fewer than 70 participants, so this is a real but not enormous effect. It matters disproportionately in Indian skin for a physiological reason: visible light induces erythema and a tanning response in deeply pigmented skin but not in fair skin. A clear sunscreen that stops only ultraviolet leaves that trigger unaddressed in Fitzpatrick IV to VI skin, and the tint in an iron-oxide formulation is the active part, not packaging.

Heat is a second driver worth naming in Mumbai, alongside cooking over a flame and side-window sun on long commutes. Our guide to sunscreen for Indian skin covers formulation and quantity.

What the Evidence Does Not Support

Several claims made routinely about melasma treatment are contradicted by the studies that went looking.

That any treatment removes facial melasma permanently. Zhou's two-year split-face study found recurrence or worsening in 58.8% of patients regardless of laser; the Indian cohort of 957 found relapse within six weeks to four months of stopping any modality; and Arellano's maintenance trial found 53% relapse-free at six months among people who had cleared and were still applying cream. Our separate article on whether melasma can be cured permanently works through the recurrence literature in detail.

That picosecond lasers are the breakthrough. In the only two-year randomised comparison, the picosecond side showed no statistically significant improvement over baseline at six months while the older Q-switched Nd:YAG side did, and it was the more painful of the two.

That stronger settings clear pigment faster. In 75 Indian patients, high-fluence Q-switched Nd:YAG performed significantly worse than both low-fluence Q-switched Nd:YAG and a glycolic acid peel. More energy bought more inflammation, not more clearance.

That oral tranexamic acid reliably stops melasma returning. The Mumbai trial recorded recurrence of 18.0% versus 64.4%, but the blinded trial that added the drug to hydroquinone recorded relapse of 30% versus 26%, which is no difference. The evidence is divided and should be presented that way.

That microneedling is the gentle option for pigmented skin. In the network meta-analysis of 59 trials, microneedling recorded the highest adverse-event rate of any modality studied, 52.3%, against 10.1% for tretinoin.

That devices outperform creams. The 113-study systematic review found peels and light-based devices equal or inferior to topicals, with a higher adverse-effect burden. On the published evidence, the machine is the adjunct and the prescription regimen is the treatment.

How the Melasma Ladder Is Usually Sequenced, and What to Expect

A sequence matching the evidence starts with confirming the diagnosis, because symmetrical facial pigment is not always melasma. Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation follows the shape of whatever inflamed the skin first and is asymmetric; solar lentigines are well-demarcated and scattered across sun-exposed sites. A single new, solitary or changing dark patch is a reason for medical assessment rather than a cosmetic appointment, because the differential for a pigmented facial macule includes lesions that must not be lasered.

From there the order the trial data supports runs: daily visible-light-blocking photoprotection and trigger management first and permanently; a prescription topical regimen next, usually a combination, for a defined course under supervision; oral tranexamic acid added for moderate to severe or recurrent disease after a clotting-risk history; superficial peels or low-fluence device sessions layered on only once that base is in place; then maintenance indefinitely, because that is what the relapse data demands.

Expectations belong against the numbers rather than before-and-after photographs. Response at 16 weeks in the large Indian cohort was 33.6% for triple combination cream. Clearance at eight weeks in the maintenance trial was 78.8%, but only 53% of those who cleared were still relapse-free six months later. Improvement is realistic and well documented. Elimination is not, and any plan promising it is not describing the published evidence.
