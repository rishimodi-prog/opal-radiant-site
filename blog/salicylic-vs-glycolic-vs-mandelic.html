Mandelic Acid for Indian Skin vs Salicylic and Glycolic: What the Trials Show

By Dr. Punit Sakhare, MD Dermatologist · 2026-10-16 · 12 min read

Three acids account for most superficial chemical peels performed in Indian clinics: salicylic acid, glycolic acid and mandelic acid. They are sold as if the choice between them were settled — salicylic for oily, acne-prone skin, glycolic for pigmentation, and mandelic acid for Indian skin because its larger molecule supposedly penetrates more slowly and irritates less. Two of those three positions have trial data behind them. The third does not, at least not the part about penetration.

What follows is the evidence, agent by agent, from the Indian and Asian studies that tested these acids head to head in Fitzpatrick IV to VI skin. The summary up front is deflationary: across roughly a dozen comparative studies totalling around 700 patients, no agent is clearly more effective than another. What separates them reproducibly is tolerability, and that gradient runs from lactic acid and salicylic–mandelic at the gentle end, through mandelic and salicylic, to glycolic and then trichloroacetic acid at the harsh end. Choosing well means choosing for the diagnosis and for how reactive the skin has already shown itself to be, not for the acid's reputation.

The three acids are different molecules doing different jobs

Salicylic, glycolic and mandelic acid differ chemically in ways that shape how each is used, though the clinical consequences are less well proven than the chemistry. Glycolic acid is an alpha hydroxy acid with a molecular weight of 76 daltons, the smallest of the commonly used acids; it is water-soluble and acts across the whole treated surface. Salicylic acid is a beta hydroxy acid and is lipid-soluble, which is why every review describes it as concentrating in the pilosebaceous unit — the oil-gland-and-follicle structure where acne begins — while staying largely epidermal. Mandelic acid is an aromatic alpha hydroxy acid with a molecular weight of 152 daltons, roughly double glycolic acid's.

Those molecular weights are chemical fact. The inference drawn from them — bigger molecule, slower and more even penetration, less irritation — is not. Targeted searches of the indexed literature for comparative human skin-penetration data on mandelic versus glycolic versus lactic acid return zero studies. The larger-molecule story is the accepted explanation and clinical trials do consistently find mandelic acid better tolerated than glycolic, but nobody has measured the penetration difference the explanation rests on.

The difference visible in the room is how each peel is ended. Under the IADVL national guideline, glycolic acid is a timed peel, neutralised after a predetermined duration, usually three minutes, and neutralised immediately with 10–15% sodium bicarbonate the moment erythema or epidermolysis appears, regardless of the timer. Salicylic acid is taken to pseudofrost, the acid crystallising white on the surface, in one to three coats, washed off after three to five minutes. A superficial chemical peel is a controlled injury with a defined endpoint, and that endpoint — not the label on the bottle — is what the operator watches.

Mandelic acid for Indian skin: what the randomised trials actually measured

Mandelic acid for Indian skin has two directly relevant randomised trials, and they point in opposite directions.

The favourable one is Dayal and colleagues in 2020, who randomised 50 Indian patients with mild-to-moderate acne to 45% mandelic acid or 30% salicylic acid, six sessions at two-week intervals across 12 weeks, scored on the Michaelsson acne score. The result was almost equal efficacy, with no significant difference in overall score: salicylic did better on non-inflammatory lesions, mandelic on inflammatory ones, and adverse effects were fewer with mandelic. That is an on-point randomised finding in Fitzpatrick IV to V skin, and it is also 50 patients, single-centre and unblinded.

The unfavourable one is Malviya and colleagues in 2025, who randomly assigned 70 Indian patients with periorbital melanosis — dark circles arising from pigmentation — to 30% mandelic acid or 30% lactic acid, three sessions at weeks 0, 2 and 4 with assessment at week 6. Lactic acid won on both axes. Half the lactic group reached more than 30% improvement on a visual analogue scale (p = .001), patient satisfaction was 100% against 83.9% (p = .011), and irritation was more frequent with mandelic acid. The authors named their own limitations: small sample, short duration, no blinding.

Read together, the defensible position is that mandelic acid is a reasonable, generally well-tolerated option, particularly where inflammatory acne dominates, and that it is not the automatically gentlest acid its marketing implies. In the only randomised Indian trial that put it against lactic acid, it was the more irritating of the two.

Salicylic acid: a 25-patient pilot carrying 25 years of practice

Salicylic acid is the agent on which the idea of safe peeling in Fitzpatrick V and VI skin was built, and it is worth knowing how narrow that foundation is.

The founding study is Grimes in 1999: 25 patients of skin types V and VI — nine with acne, five with post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, six with melasma and five with rough, oily skin — primed for two weeks with 4% hydroquinone, then given five 20% or 30% salicylic acid peels at two-week intervals. Moderate-to-significant improvement was recorded in 88%, and “minimal to mild side effects occurred in 16%”. That is an open-label, uncontrolled pilot with no comparison group, published over 25 years ago. Every subsequent review repeats its conclusion; almost none repeats its design. The 16% figure is real; so is the design caveat.

The second load-bearing salicylic study is Lee and Kim in 2003: 35 Korean patients given 30% salicylic acid peels every two weeks for 12 weeks, with a blinded evaluator counting lesions. Both inflammatory and non-inflammatory lesion counts fell in proportion to how long treatment continued, and the Cunliffe grade dropped significantly. There was no control arm, so part of that improvement may be the natural course of acne. The same study produced the most deflationary measurement in this field, dealt with below.

For active, oily, comedonal acne in deeper skin, salicylic acid remains the conventional first choice, and it sits in the middle of the tolerability gradient rather than at the gentle end. Where post-acne marks and scarring dominate, the calculation changes.

Glycolic acid: the most effective and the most irritating

Glycolic acid is the most studied superficial peel agent in Indian dermatology and, consistently, the most irritating of the common choices.

In the strongest Indian comparison, Sarkar and colleagues randomised 90 melasma patients in 2016 into three groups of 30 — 35% glycolic acid, 20% salicylic with 10% mandelic acid, or a phytic acid combination — all primed for four weeks, with peels every 14 days to 12 weeks. The melasma area and severity index fell 62.36% with glycolic, 60.98% with salicylic–mandelic and 44.71% with phytic. Both of the first two beat phytic (p = .00); glycolic against salicylic–mandelic came out at p = .876, meaning no detectable difference. The authors concluded the two were equally efficacious but that “salicylic-mandelic peels are better tolerated and more suitable for Indian skin”.

Where glycolic acid does pull ahead on efficacy, it pays for it. In a 2016 comparative study of 90 Indian patients with constitutional periorbital melanosis, more than 50% improvement was reached by 73.34% on 20% glycolic acid, 56.67% on 15% lactic acid and 26.67% on topical 20% vitamin C — glycolic clearly best and, in the authors' own words, “associated with increased rate of side effects”. Two further 90-patient studies from the same group repeat the pattern: glycolic gave the best global assessment while causing the most erythema and itching of the agents tested.

One caveat covers all of that: four of these studies come from a single academic department — consistency, not independent replication — and none ran beyond six months. Glycolic acid is a defensible choice for melasma and pigmentation in skin that has already shown it tolerates acids, and a poor first choice for skin that flares easily.

Salicylic&ndash;mandelic: the combination the Indian head-to-heads favour

The salicylic–mandelic combination, usually 20% salicylic with 10% mandelic acid, is the formulation the Indian literature most often nominates as the best tolerated in deeper skin.

Its origin is Garg, Sinha and Sarkar in 2009: 44 Indian patients with active acne plus post-acne scarring and hyperpigmentation, treated fortnightly for six sessions with either 35% glycolic acid or the salicylic–mandelic combination. Both worked. The combination was significantly better for most active acne lesion types (p < .001) and for hyperpigmentation (p < .001), with fewer side effects. The design matters: two arms with an independent observer, but not randomised, not blinded, and 44 patients is a thin basis for a hierarchy.

When the same group re-ran the comparison properly randomised, in melasma, in 90 patients, the efficacy advantage vanished — that is the p = .876 result above — and only the tolerability advantage survived. That pattern is exactly what you would expect if the real difference between these two acids is how they feel rather than how hard they work.

What the combination feels like is documented in one small series. A retrospective pilot in 17 Indian patients of Fitzpatrick IV to V, treated on the axillae for acanthosis nigricans, reported post-peel erythema in 100% and burning in 90%, both lasting one to two days. Those are expected effects rather than complications, and better known before a first session than after it.

What the evidence does not support

Several claims made routinely about these three acids are not merely unproven; they are contradicted by the studies that went looking.

No acid is proven superior for acne. The 2020 Cochrane review of topical acids in acne covered 49 trials and 3,880 participants. On the exact comparison at issue here it reports: “There may be little or no difference in PGA when comparing glycolic acid peel to salicylic-mandelic acid peel (RR 1.06, 95% CI 0.88 to 1.26; 1 study, 40 participants; low-quality evidence)”. The entire global meta-analytic base for that head-to-head is one study of 40 people, rated low quality, showing no difference.

Peels do not hydrate skin or repair the barrier. Lee and Kim instrumented their 35 patients through 12 weeks of fortnightly 30% salicylic acid peels and reported, verbatim, that “stratum corneum hydration, skin surface lipid, skin pH, and transepidermal water loss were unchanged from baseline levels”. Lesion counts improved; every barrier measurement stayed flat.

Peels do not control oil or shrink pores. No study in this literature measured pore diameter at all, and the one that measured sebum found the opposite of the marketing line: in 28 Polish women aged 49 to 71, 40% mandelic and 20% azelaic acid peels produced a significant increase in sebum secretion in the U-zone. Small, uncontrolled, elderly and non-Indian — and still the only sebum measurement available.

The mandelic acid mechanism has never been measured. Comparative human penetration data for mandelic versus glycolic versus lactic acid does not exist in the indexed literature; the rationale is physicochemical reasoning validated only by downstream tolerability data.

Peels do not change your complexion, and no outcome can be promised. These acids act on dyschromia — melasma, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, tanning — not on constitutive pigmentation, which is genetic. No study cited here measured a change in baseline skin colour, and none reports a guaranteed result.

Depth and priming matter more than which acid you pick

In Fitzpatrick IV to VI skin, the choice between salicylic, glycolic and mandelic acid matters less than how deep the peel goes and what was done to the skin beforehand.

The IADVL Task Force standard guidelines of care for chemical peels are explicit on depth: “Superficial peels are considered safe in Indian patients”; “Medium depth peels should be performed with great caution, especially in dark skinned patients”; and “Deep peels are not recommended for Indian skin.” That is consensus rather than trial evidence — no randomised trial has assigned Indian patients to superficial, medium or deep peels and counted complications — but it is the national position. The same guideline mandates priming rather than suggesting it: “It is essential to do prepeel priming of the patient's skin with sunscreens, hydroquinone and tretinoin for 2–4 weeks.”

Priming has randomised support of its own. Nanda, Grover and Reddy ran a double-blind randomised trial in 50 Indian melasma patients, 56% of them Fitzpatrick IV, comparing 2% hydroquinone against 0.025% tretinoin around a course of TCA peels. At 12 weeks the groups were comparable; by six months they had diverged, with 24% of the hydroquinone group continuing to improve against 16% on tretinoin, and 28% worsening against 40%. Read the winning arm carefully: over a quarter were worse at six months despite priming and sunscreen throughout.

The best-powered safety dataset in deeper skin is a 2018 retrospective analysis of 473 superficial peel treatments in Fitzpatrick III to VI patients. Complications occurred in 3.8%: crusting 2.3%, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation 1.9% and erythema 1.9%, all resolving within eight months. Fitzpatrick type VI carried an odds ratio of 5.14 for any adverse event (95% CI 1.21–21.8), and side effects were significantly less common in winter, which in Mumbai argues for scheduling a course in the cooler months. An assessment by a qualified dermatologist before a first peel is what the guideline assumes has happened.

Matching the acid to the diagnosis

Choosing between salicylic, glycolic and mandelic acid means matching the agent to the diagnosis and to how reactive the skin has proved to be. The table summarises what the studies above support and nothing beyond.

The tolerability ranking is the most reproducible finding in this literature: lactic acid and salicylic–mandelic at the gentle end, then mandelic, then salicylic, then glycolic, with trichloroacetic acid harshest. Lactic acid is also under-marketed relative to its evidence, winning on tolerance in two separate Indian comparisons and on efficacy in one.

How many sessions, and what happens when you stop

A course of superficial peels follows a schedule that is strikingly consistent across the Indian trials. Six sessions at two-week intervals across 12 weeks is what essentially every study cited here used: Garg 2009, Sarkar 2016, Dayal 2020 and the melasma comparisons all ran that schedule. Meaningful pigmentation-score reduction was measured at 12 weeks, not at three sessions and not at six weeks. A single peel is a maintenance step, not a treatment course.

After a session, erythema and a burning sensation are usual and, in the small Indian series that recorded them, settled within one to two days, though that varies by individual and agent. What is not usual is prolonged redness, crusting or new pigmentation, and any of those is a reason to stop and be reviewed rather than keep to the schedule.

The part least often mentioned is what happens when a course ends. In a retrospective review of 40 Fitzpatrick IV to VI patients treated with sequential glycolic and TCA peels, melasma recurrence reached 70% within 12 weeks of stopping; that series also reported irritation in 77.5% and transient hyperpigmentation in 12.5%, with no scarring. It is uncontrolled, single-centre and South African, but it runs with everything else here. These acids manage dyschromia; they do not cure it, and results are held by maintenance topicals and continued photoprotection rather than by the peels. It also helps to know the difference between purging and a genuine breakout beforehand.

This article is educational and is not medical advice. Chemical peels are medical procedures with real risks in deeper skin, and the right agent, strength and interval depend on an individual assessment. Consult a qualified dermatologist before starting any peel.
