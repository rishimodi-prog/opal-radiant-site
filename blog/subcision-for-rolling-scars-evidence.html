Acne Scars

Subcision Treatment for Rolling Acne Scars: What the Evidence Shows

By Dr. Punit Sakhare, MD Dermatologist · 2026-10-06 · 11 min read

Quick Facts

What subcision is
A needle or blunt cannula is passed under a depressed scar in the plane of the dermis and moved side to side to cut the fibrous bands tethering the skin down. No tissue is removed and the skin surface is not resurfaced.
Best-controlled trial
A split-face randomised trial with an untreated control side and blinded raters, using five subcision sessions four weeks apart. It enrolled five patients (Chen et al., 2024).
Published improvement range
10% to 100% across the ten subcision studies pooled in a 2017 systematic review — a range wide enough to be close to uninformative (Kravvas and Al-Niaimi, 2017).
The most transferable Indian number
In a blinded-observer split-face trial of 30 patients at a Mumbai hospital, subcision with a cryoroller gave 57% improvement and subcision with a dermaroller 40%, after three sittings a month apart (Gadkari and Nayak, 2014).
Sessions in published protocols
Two to five sessions at three to four week intervals. Individual requirements vary and no trial has established an optimal number.
Documented complications
Transient erythema, oedema and bruising in most trials; one persistent haematoma after conventional subcision and two entry-point burns after the radiofrequency-assisted version, in a 17-patient Indian split-face trial (Kaur et al., 2020).

Rolling scars are the wide, shallow depressions with sloping edges that appear when fibrous strands tether the skin down to the layers beneath. Subcision treatment addresses that tethering directly rather than working on the surface: a needle or blunt cannula is passed under the scar within the dermis and moved back and forth to cut the bands, releasing the depressed skin. Of the four acne scar morphologies, rolling is where the mechanism of the scar and the mechanism of the procedure line up most cleanly, and it has the most direct evidence behind it.

That evidence is thinner than clinic marketing implies. The only subcision trial published with a genuine untreated control side enrolled five patients. The improvement range across the pooled subcision literature runs from 10% to 100%. Several adjuncts routinely sold alongside subcision have been tested against it and failed to beat it on any objective measure. What follows is what the trials measured and where the numbers run out. This article is educational information, not medical advice; deciding whether any procedure suits your skin requires in-person assessment by a qualified dermatologist.

What Subcision Treatment Actually Does to a Rolling Scar

Subcision treatment — from "subcuticular incisionless surgery" — releases a depressed scar from below. After local anaesthesia, usually a regional nerve block for full-face work, the operator inserts a needle or blunt-tipped cannula through a single entry point, advances it under the scar, and sweeps it laterally to divide the fibrous bands anchoring the scar base to deeper tissue. Nothing is excised and the surface is not resurfaced. The released skin lifts, and the small pocket of bleeding underneath is part of the intended process: the organising haematoma and the healing that follows are what hold the correction.

That mechanism is why the procedure is subtype-specific. Rolling scars exist because of tethering, so cutting the tether addresses the cause. Ice pick scars are narrow tracts of collagen loss with no tether to release, and they do not respond to techniques that work by generalised dermal remodelling. A randomised Indian pilot study of 30 patients with grade 4 atrophic scars recorded substantial improvement in rolling and boxcar scars but "only a mild change in icepick scars" (Bhargava et al., 2019) — a pilot with 15 per arm, but the ice pick signal is consistent wherever it has been looked for.

That rolling is the best-responding subtype has been shown directly. In a single-blinded split-face randomised trial measuring scar depth by ultrasound alongside two blinded observers, the morphology that improved most in both arms was rolling, at p = 0.001 in the subcision-only arm (Dastgheib et al., 2024). Twelve patients is a very small trial, but it is a measured result rather than an inference. If you are unsure which types of acne scars you have, that question comes first.

The Best-Controlled Subcision Trial Enrolled Five Patients

The cleanest design in the subcision literature is a split-face randomised trial in which one side of the face received subcision and the other received nothing at all. An untreated contralateral control is rare and valuable, because acne scars change slowly on their own and photographic comparison alone cannot separate treatment effect from natural history. Assessment was blinded twice over: a blinded live rater plus two double-blinded dermatologist raters. The protocol was five subcision treatments four weeks apart, with follow-up at weeks 20 and 36 (Chen et al., 2024).

The result was a greater decrease in global scarring score on the subcision side than on the untreated side, and half the participants reported greater satisfaction with the treated side. The trial ran at an academic centre with no manufacturer involvement.

It enrolled five patients. A five-patient trial cannot establish an effect size, cannot generate a percentage figure worth quoting, and cannot detect anything but the largest safety signals. What it can do — and what nothing else in this literature does — is show that the treated side changed more than an untreated side of the same face, judged by raters who did not know which was which. That is the honest status of subcision: a coherent mechanism, a repeatedly observed benefit, and one controlled trial containing five people.

How Much Improvement the Studies Actually Report

The most useful single figure for subcision expectations is a range, not a number. A systematic review of non-energy-based acne scar treatments examined 36 articles, of which ten studied subcision, and reported an improvement range of 10% to 100%; the same review reported 31% to 62% for microneedling (Kravvas and Al-Niaimi, 2017). A range spanning 10 to 100 is close to uninformative as a prediction, but it accurately describes what the published studies contain, and it is the answer to any clinic advertising a fixed percentage.

The most transferable numbers for a patient in Mumbai come from a split-face controlled trial at Nair Hospital in 30 patients with Goodman and Baron grade 2 to 4 scars, with a blinded observer and six-month photographic assessment. Subcision was performed under nerve block across the whole face at three sittings a month apart, then one half treated with a 2.5 mm dermaroller and the other with a cryoroller. On the quantitative grading scale the cryoroller side improved 57% and the dermaroller side 40%; patient-perceived improvement was 61% and 45%, significant on Wilcoxon signed-rank testing (Gadkari and Nayak, 2014). Both sides received subcision, so this does not isolate its own contribution — but it is Indian skin, a Mumbai population, a blinded assessor and a real number.

Across the field, controlled studies of atrophic acne scarring cluster around 40% to 60% improvement on validated grading scales after a full course, and none reports complete resolution.

Needle, Cannula or Radiofrequency: The Comparative Trials

Technique has been compared directly, and this is where the largest subcision trial sits. A two-arm randomised trial of 100 patients, fifty per group, assessed by three blinded dermatologists, compared subcision with an 18-gauge blunt cannula against the conventional 27-gauge needle method. The cannula arm achieved "good" or "very good" improvement in about 83% of patients, significantly more than the needle arm at p < 0.05, with the authors reporting almost no adverse events (Nilforoushzadeh et al., 2020). The caveats are real: no untreated control, and "good or very good" is a subjective ordinal outcome, not a validated scar score. Even so, at n = 100 it is several times larger than anything else in the field.

Radiofrequency-assisted subcision, in which energy is delivered through the subcising instrument, was tested against conventional subcision in a split-face randomised trial at AIIMS New Delhi — an Indian population, directly relevant to Fitzpatrick IV and V skin. Twenty-one were enrolled and seventeen completed two sessions four weeks apart. Both arms improved significantly from baseline on Goodman and Baron score and scar count, both at p = 0.0001. Improvement was comparable between arms on the Goodman and Baron score and on blinded investigator global assessment; only the patients' own global assessment favoured the radiofrequency version (Kaur et al., 2020).

That trial also produced the clearest adverse event data in the subcision literature: two small entry-point burns with radiofrequency subcision, healing within a month, and one persistent haematoma after conventional subcision. Seventeen completers cannot establish complication rates, but these events are documented, not theoretical.

Subcision Compared With Fillers, Microneedling and Resurfacing

Subcision has been compared with dermal filler in a randomised, single-blind split-face trial of 20 patients, using a naturally sourced porcine collagen filler on the opposite side. Patients rated subcision superior at three months, p = 0.03. By six months that advantage had shrunk to non-significance at p = 0.12, and blinded evaluators showed no preference between the two sides at six months, p = 0.69 (Sage et al., 2011). That pattern — an early patient preference that does not survive blinded assessment later — recurs throughout this literature, and is worth remembering whenever a clinic reports satisfaction scores rather than graded outcomes.

Against microneedling the comparison also fails to separate. A split-face study of 50 patients in Bikaner treated one side with microneedling plus platelet-rich plasma and the other with subcision plus platelet-rich plasma over three sessions. On Goodman and Baron qualitative grading the microneedling side reached two-grade improvement in 70% and three-grade in 10%, the subcision side two-grade in 50% and three-grade in 30%, and the authors reported no significant difference (Yadav et al., 2024). Side allocation was fixed left and right rather than randomised and no blinding was described — genuine weaknesses — but it is a reasonable sample in an Indian population.

Subcision is not competing with radiofrequency microneedling or fractional resurfacing for the same job. It releases tethering under a rolling scar; energy devices remodel the dermis and resurface the epidermis, which is what boxcar walls and generalised texture need. Most faces carry mixed morphologies, and the most reproducible finding here is that combinations outperform any single modality repeated.

Sessions, Recovery and Documented Complications

Published subcision protocols cluster tightly: five sessions four weeks apart (Chen et al., 2024), two sessions four weeks apart (Kaur et al., 2020), three sittings a month apart (Gadkari and Nayak, 2014) and two sessions a month apart (Dastgheib et al., 2024). That gives a working range of two to five sessions at three to four week intervals. These are the numbers investigators chose, not a validated dose: no trial has compared session counts against each other, so the optimal number is unknown and requirements vary between people.

Recovery is dominated by bruising, which here is expected rather than a complication: blood collecting under the released scar is part of the mechanism. Trials record transient erythema, oedema and haematoma as routine (Gadkari and Nayak, 2014), with the persistent haematoma and radiofrequency entry-point burns above as the documented problems beyond that (Kaur et al., 2020). No controlled trial of an acne scar procedure reports an absence of visible after-effects, so describing subcision as a no-downtime procedure is not supported by the published data.

Duration is where the evidence is weakest. Trials call the erythema, swelling and bruising transient but do not standardise how long that takes, so it varies with depth of undermining, area treated, instrument and bruising tendency. Follow-up runs to two months in the AIIMS trial, six months in the Mumbai trial and week 36 in the five-patient trial; nothing here follows patients beyond about nine months.

Subcision in Indian Skin: Known and Unknown

Two of the most relevant subcision trials were run in Indian populations: the split-face trial at Nair Hospital in Mumbai with 30 patients (Gadkari and Nayak, 2014) and the split-face randomised trial at AIIMS New Delhi with 21 enrolled (Kaur et al., 2020). Subcision has therefore at least been studied in the skin types most patients in Mumbai have.

The Mumbai trial recorded transient post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation on the cryoroller side rather than as a consequence of the subcision itself — a useful distinction, because pigment risk in a combined protocol may come from the adjunct rather than the undermining. Mechanistically that is what you would expect: subcision works below the epidermis and delivers no thermal energy to the surface, which is the physical reason its pigmentary risk profile differs from ablative fractional resurfacing. That is an inference, and should be labelled as one. No trial has quantified the incidence of post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation after subcision by Fitzpatrick skin type. The number does not exist.

What deeper skin does change is the visibility and consequence of the bruising: bruising resolving in Fitzpatrick IV to VI skin can be followed by pigmentary change at the site, which is why sun protection through the healing weeks is standard advice. For anyone whose main complaint is discolouration rather than contour, subcision is the wrong tool — pigment sits in a different layer, as covered under acne scars.

What the Evidence Does Not Support

The most commonly sold addition to subcision is platelet-rich plasma, and the best test of it went the other way. A randomised split-face study of 30 patients at Cairo University, with two blinded investigators evaluating serial photographs across three monthly sessions, compared platelet-rich plasma alone against combined subcision plus platelet-rich plasma. The authors reported that platelet-rich plasma alone showed a better response, fewer side effects and shorter downtime than the combination (Hassan et al., 2020). One 30-patient trial does not settle a question, but it means the claim that subcision plus platelet-rich plasma beats either alone is not established.

Injectable skin boosters and hyaluronic acid mesogels added to subcision have been tested with objective instrumentation and failed. In a single-blinded split-face randomised trial of 12 patients using two blinded observers and ultrasound-measured scar depth, every objective endpoint was null: total scar reduction 29.74 with the mesogel versus 22.27 with subcision alone (p = 0.56), sonographic depth reduction 29.21 versus 28.53 (p = 0.4), global improvement 4 in both arms (p = 0.89). Only patient satisfaction on a visual analogue scale differed, at p = 0.02. The authors nonetheless concluded in favour of the added product — an over-claim against their own three null objective results (Dastgheib et al., 2024).

Three further claims do not hold. Radiofrequency-assisted subcision is not shown to outperform conventional subcision: improvement was comparable on the Goodman and Baron score and on blinded investigator assessment, and only patient perception favoured it (Kaur et al., 2020). Dermal filler is not shown to outperform subcision for rolling scars, with blinded evaluators showing no preference at six months, p = 0.69 (Sage et al., 2011). And subcision is not a treatment for ice pick scars, which showed "only a mild change" in the trial that looked (Bhargava et al., 2019).

Two things nobody can promise. No fixed percentage improvement can be quoted, because the published range is 10% to 100% across ten studies (Kravvas and Al-Niaimi, 2017). And no study here demonstrates permanence or complete clearance: the longest follow-up in any trial cited is 36 weeks, so claims about results lasting years rest on no published measurement.

Sequencing, and What to Ask Before Booking

Scar revision sits at the end of a sequence, not the start. Active inflammatory acne should be controlled first, because new lesions produce new scars faster than any procedure closes old ones, and because treating over active inflammation raises the risk of pigmentary complications in deeper skin. Timing relative to oral isotretinoin is an individualised decision for your treating doctor: the manufacturer's product information and specialist consensus statements do not agree on the interval required.

Four questions are worth asking before agreeing to a course. What proportion of my scars are actually rolling, rather than boxcar or ice pick. Needle or blunt cannula, given that the largest randomised trial favoured an 18-gauge cannula in about 83% of patients (Nilforoushzadeh et al., 2020). How many sessions, and on what basis, given that published protocols range from two to five. And if an adjunct is added, what controlled evidence shows it improves the objective result — for platelet-rich plasma, skin boosters and radiofrequency, the current answer is none.

Subcision may improve the appearance of rolling acne scars, and it has the most direct mechanistic and trial support for that morphology. It does not clear scarring, and its evidence base is small enough that anyone quoting precise outcome figures is going beyond what has been published.
