Dark Underarms Treatment: Why It Happens and What the Evidence Actually Clears

By Dr. Punit Sakhare, MD Dermatologist · 2026-10-04 · 12 min read

Dark underarms are among the most common cosmetic complaints in Indian dermatology practice and among the least studied. The entire published evidence base for dark underarms treatment is ten studies. Not ten trials per option, ten studies in total: that is what a 2025 systematic review found when it searched PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science and Embase for treatments of axillary hyperpigmentation, and that review opens by stating plainly that there is no standard treatment for the condition.

That number should shape what you expect before booking anything. Underarm darkening is a genuine dermatological finding with identifiable causes, and some of those causes matter medically rather than cosmetically. This article covers what the pigment actually is, which triggers have evidence behind them, when underarm darkening is a metabolic sign rather than a skin problem, what topicals and lasers achieved in the ten studies that exist, why Fitzpatrick IV to VI skin changes the risk calculation, and which claims have no study behind them at all. This is not medical advice. Consult a qualified dermatologist before starting any treatment.

What Dark Underarms Actually Are

Underarm darkening is, in most cases, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation occupying the axilla. Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, or PIH, is the pigment that skin deposits after it has been inflamed. A 2022 focused review of PIH pathophysiology describes the hallmark as increased melanin production and distribution within the epidermis and, in some cases, the dermis, with triggers spanning inflammatory and autoimmune conditions, iatrogenic causes and mechanical injury. The same review states directly that the exact mechanisms underlying PIH are not completely understood. Any clinic that explains underarm pigment with complete mechanistic confidence is going further than the literature does.

Depth matters more than almost anything else for what treatment can achieve. A 2011 review in the American Journal of Clinical Dermatology sets out a colour rule still used at the bedside: epidermal PIH appears tan, brown or dark brown, while dermal hypermelanosis appears blue-grey. Depth predicts response, and dermal pigment responds poorly to topical agents. A blue-grey axilla and a mid-brown axilla are not the same problem, even though they arrive as the same complaint.

It is also worth stating what underarm pigment is not. It is not dirt, and it is not a hygiene failure. Scrubbing harder is mechanical injury, and mechanical injury is a named PIH trigger, so the most common self-treatment can feed the process it is meant to fix. The clinical goal is treating a diagnosed pigmentary condition and reducing patchiness, not changing anyone's complexion. For how post-inflammatory pigment behaves generally, our evidence-based PIH treatment ladder covers the same biology on the face.

The Causes of Underarm Darkening, Ranked by What the Evidence Supports

The best-supported cause of underarm darkening is contact dermatitis. A 2024 review in Cutis reports that irritant and allergic contact dermatitis are common causes of axillary skin disease, with deodorants, antiperspirants, detergents, soaps and clothing the frequently implicated sources. Fragrance is named as the ubiquitous culprit ingredient, alongside metals and dyes, and diagnosis rests on clinical assessment, histopathology and patch testing. It is a narrative review, so it carries no incidence figures, but it establishes the causal chain cleanly: an allergen or irritant inflames the axilla, the inflammation resolves, and the pigment it leaves behind is what the patient eventually notices.

Shaving and friction are the second commonly cited cause, and here the honest position is different. Mechanical injury is a named PIH trigger in the 2022 pathophysiology review, so the mechanism is real and plausible. But no controlled study in any population has quantified how much axillary hyperpigmentation is attributable to shaving, waxing, tight clothing or friction. That is a named gap in the literature, not a finding waiting to be looked up. Treat the shaving link as clinical consensus with a sound mechanism rather than a measured effect, and be sceptical of any percentage attached to it.

The third cause is acanthosis nigricans, and it changes what should happen next.

When Underarm Darkening Is a Metabolic Sign, Not a Cosmetic One

Acanthosis nigricans presents in the axilla, and mistaking it for ordinary friction pigmentation is the most consequential error in this area. A 2020 review in the Journal of Cosmetic Dermatology defines it as symmetric velvety hyperpigmented plaques on intertriginous areas including the axilla, neck and groin, driven by multifactorial stimulation of keratinocyte and fibroblast proliferation. The review lists benign, syndromic, malignant, acral, medication-induced and mixed types, and names HOMA-IR as the recommended assessment tool for insulin resistance. The texture is the tell: velvety and thickened rather than flat and stained.

Two Indian studies have examined how well it tracks insulin resistance, and they disagree. A 2019 cross-sectional study in the Indian Dermatology Online Journal compared 60 cases aged 2 to 24 against 30 age- and sex-matched normal-weight controls, and found mean HOMA2-IR of 2.422 in cases versus 1.322 in controls. The association held even in normal-weight cases (p = 0.045), and Grade 4 axillary severity was significantly associated with insulin resistance (p = 0.001). A 2022 cross-sectional study of 87 untreated adult acanthosis nigricans patients in Bangalore found high insulin resistance in 55 of 87 (63.2%), but no significant association between insulin resistance and either severity or number of sites (p > 0.05). Its authors conclude the association is not strong enough to qualify acanthosis nigricans as a screening tool. That study has no control group, which limits it, and the two findings have not been reconciled.

What both support is the referral, not the prediction. Velvety axillary darkening warrants a metabolic assessment rather than a bleaching peel, and the 2020 review is blunt about the ceiling of skin-directed treatment: complete cure and disappearance of lesions are difficult to achieve, and the authors describe addressing the underlying driver as the most practical management strategy. That review also lists malignant acanthosis nigricans as a recognised type. Sudden-onset, rapidly progressive or extensive darkening, particularly with mucosal involvement or unexplained weight loss, needs medical evaluation before any cosmetic procedure is considered.

Dark Underarms Treatment: The Whole Evidence Base Is Ten Studies

The 2025 systematic review in the Journal of Cosmetic Dermatology is the only synthesis of axillary hyperpigmentation therapy that exists, and it found exactly ten studies meeting its inclusion criteria across four databases, with one further study excluded for focusing on hair reduction rather than pigment. No pooled sample size is reported, no meta-analysis was possible, and no Indian-population data is highlighted. The review's own opening position is that there is no standard treatment for axillary hyperpigmentation.

Within that base, three categories showed something. Topical agents associated with some pigment reduction were niacinamide, desonide, sweet orange extract, Perilla frutescens leaf extract, Cyperus rotundus oil, glycolic acid and several proprietary serums, with desonide and Cyperus rotundus oil singled out for significant effectiveness and minimal side effects. Q-switched Nd:YAG laser was effective across three of the ten studies, making it the most-studied device for this indication, with no severe adverse effects reported, though it was more painful than intense pulsed light. Intense pulsed light showed significant improvement with minimal adverse events, and one comparative study within the review found it superior to an alpha-hydroxy acid for both lightening and texture.

The desonide finding needs a warning attached rather than a recommendation. Desonide is a topical corticosteroid, and applying a steroid to the axilla for cosmetic pigment reduction is a route to skin atrophy, striae and steroid dependence. A finding in a systematic review is not a prescription, and the axilla is thin, occluded, frictional skin where steroid adverse effects concentrate.

Why Fitzpatrick IV to VI Skin Changes the Risk Arithmetic

Most patients seeking treatment for dark underarms in Mumbai are Fitzpatrick IV to VI, and in that skin the same procedures that reduce pigment can also produce it. The clearest Indian-relevant safety data comes from a 2018 retrospective study in the Journal of the American Academy of Dermatology of 473 superficial chemical peels in Fitzpatrick III to VI skin. The overall complication rate was 3.8%, which is reassuring, but Fitzpatrick VI carried an odds ratio of 5.14 (95% CI 1.21 to 21.8) for any adverse event. Note how wide that confidence interval is: the direction is clear, the magnitude much less so. Crusting was the commonest complication at 2.3%, with post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation and erythema tied at 1.9%. All complications resolved within eight months, and side effects were significantly less common in winter, an argument for scheduling a course outside Mumbai's hottest months.

For PIH specifically, the most useful honesty check is a 2024 PRISMA systematic review in the Journal of Cutaneous Medicine and Surgery covering 48 studies and 1,356 individuals with skin of colour (Fitzpatrick IV 40%, V 34%, VI 6%). Topical retinoids were the most-used intervention at 22% of cases, producing partial improvement in 85%; laser therapy accounted for 17%, with partial improvement in 66% and complete resolution in a 26% subgroup, the only intervention to produce complete clearance in anyone. Chemical peels at 9% and hydroquinone at 7% had less effective outcomes. The reviewers also recorded cases of PIH exacerbation after laser. Their verbatim conclusion is that the results show a lack of robust efficacy across all treatment modalities. That sentence should be read before committing to a course, and it is why our pigmentation and melasma approach starts with diagnosis rather than device selection.

What the Evidence Does Not Support

Several claims commonly attached to underarm pigmentation do not survive contact with the published literature. Each of these is worth knowing before a consultation.

  • That underarm darkening is dirt or a hygiene failure. It is post-inflammatory pigment, acanthosis nigricans, or contact dermatitis sequelae. Abrasive scrubbing is mechanical injury, a named PIH trigger.
  • That shaving is a proven cause with a known contribution. No controlled study in any population has quantified shaving- or deodorant-attributable axillary hyperpigmentation. The mechanism is plausible; the number does not exist.
  • That any treatment clears underarm pigment. The 2025 systematic review describes pigment reduction, not clearance, and reports no standard treatment. None of the ten studies reported quantified long-term relapse rates, so nothing published supports a permanence claim in either direction.
  • That steroid-containing creams are a safe way to reduce pigment. A 2011 prospective multicentre study across 12 Indian dermatology centres screened 2,926 patients with facial dermatoses and found 433 (14.8%) using topical corticosteroids. Use as a fairness cream was the single most common indication, in 126 patients (29%), and adverse effects occurred in 392 of 433, a rate of 90.5%. Non-physician recommendation accounted for 59.3% of use. That study is on facial skin, but the axilla is thinner and occluded, which makes the risk profile worse rather than better.
  • That how dark acanthosis nigricans looks tells you how insulin resistant someone is. The 2022 Bangalore study of 87 patients found no significant association between insulin resistance and either severity or number of sites (p > 0.05), and its authors explicitly reject acanthosis nigricans as a screening tool. The 2019 study found severity associations. The two disagree, and neither supports reading a metabolic number off the skin.
  • That injectable skin-lightening agents belong anywhere in this discussion. They do not, and the evidence against the most heavily marketed of them is covered in our review of what the safety data on skin-whitening injections actually shows.

One more absence is worth naming. The ten-study base contains no Indian-population data highlighted by the reviewers, so every efficacy figure quoted for this indication is extrapolated across skin types and populations. That is a reason for measured expectations, and it should be said out loud.

What Has the Best Support: Remove the Trigger, Then Be Patient

The strongest evidence in underarm hyperpigmentation is not about a device. It is about identifying and removing what is inflaming the skin. The 2024 Cutis review names patch testing as the diagnostic step that identifies the responsible allergen, with fragrance the most common culprit and metals and dyes close behind. The 2024 PIH systematic review found that 89% of PIH cases were precipitated by inflammatory conditions, which is the statistical expression of the same point: control the inflammation and the pigment engine stops being fed.

In practice that means a fragrance-free antiperspirant, a review of detergent and fabric, avoiding abrasive scrubs and depilatory creams while the skin is irritated, and treating any active dermatitis before a pigment-directed procedure begins. It is unglamorous and it is the part with the best support. A pigment laser applied over an unrecognised contact dermatitis treats the smoke while the fire continues.

Expect this to be slow. There is no controlled study establishing a resolution timeline for spontaneous PIH in Fitzpatrick V to VI skin, which is a named gap in the literature. The nearest benchmark is the 473-peel study, where all complications in Fitzpatrick III to VI skin resolved within eight months. Months, not weeks, is the realistic frame, and dermal blue-grey pigment may take considerably longer or may not fully resolve.

How to Approach a Course Sensibly, and When to See a Dermatologist

A sensible sequence starts with a diagnosis rather than a device. The first question is which of the three patterns is present: flat brown or blue-grey staining consistent with post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, velvety thickened plaques consistent with acanthosis nigricans, or an active dermatitis with itching, scaling or redness still in progress. Those three have different investigations and different treatment paths, and the difference is not always obvious in the mirror.

If contact dermatitis is suspected, patch testing is the investigation that names the allergen. If acanthosis nigricans is suspected, metabolic assessment belongs in the plan, and the 2019 Indian study is a reminder that the association held even in normal-weight patients (p = 0.045). Only once the driver is identified does the question of a topical, a peel or a pigment laser become answerable, and even then the honest framing is a reduction in pigmentation that may or may not be achieved, over months, alongside continued trigger avoidance.

Some findings should stop a cosmetic pathway entirely. Sudden-onset or rapidly progressive darkening, extensive involvement beyond the axillae, mucosal pigmentation, or unexplained weight loss all warrant medical evaluation first, because malignant acanthosis nigricans is a recognised type in the 2020 review. A single new or changing dark patch anywhere is a diagnostic question rather than a cosmetic one.

None of this is medical advice, and no article substitutes for examination of your own skin. If you want the pattern assessed and the options explained against the evidence rather than against a package, book a consultation with a qualified dermatologist and ask what the published evidence for the proposed treatment actually is.
