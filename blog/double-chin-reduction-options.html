Double Chin Treatment: What the Evidence Actually Shows Works

By Dr. Punit Sakhare, MD Dermatologist · 2026-09-13 · 12 min read

Quick Facts

Strongest evidence
Injectable deoxycholic acid. In REFINE-1 (n = 506), 70.0% reached at least a one-grade improvement versus 18.6% on placebo; only 13.4% reached a two-grade change versus 0%.
Submental fat freezing, measured
Pooled reduction of 2.78 mm in fat thickness and 19.57 cm3 in volume across 8 studies and 206 patients (Do 2025) - and every one of those 8 studies was uncontrolled.
The India position
Approved deoxycholic acid (ATX-101) is not marketed in India (Shome 2019). Any deoxycholic acid used here is generic or compounded, and off-label.
Patients who see nothing
5.13% of 39 patients rated their submental cryolipolysis result as 'no result' at three months (Rodopoulou 2020). Kilmer 2016: 23% did not report visible fat reduction.
The complication that does not settle
Paradoxical adipose hyperplasia: pooled incidence 0.22%, about 1 in 455 patients, on low-certainty evidence (Mah 2025). It does not resolve on its own.
What none of this is
None of these are weight-reduction methods or treatments for obesity. They change fat-layer thickness in one small treated patch; body weight is not the endpoint any of these trials moved.

A double chin is not one problem, and that is the main reason double chin treatment disappoints. Under the skin of the submental triangle sit at least three separate things: subcutaneous fat above the platysma muscle, deeper fat below it, and skin and muscle laxity that no fat-reduction procedure touches. A jaw that sits back relative to the neck produces the same silhouette with very little excess fat at all. Reducing fat in someone whose contour is driven by laxity can make the result worse, not better.

What follows is the published evidence for the four options an Indian clinic can realistically offer, graded by study design rather than by marketing volume. The short version: injectable deoxycholic acid has four placebo-controlled Phase 3 trials behind it and is not marketed in India in its approved form; submental cryolipolysis has a pooled effect of 2.78 mm of fat-layer thinning and not a single controlled trial anywhere in the world literature; high-intensity focused ultrasound rests on one open-label study of 40 people; and no head-to-head trial of any of these against another has ever been published.

What a double chin actually is, and why three different things get called one

Submental fullness - the double chin - is produced by at least three anatomically distinct contributors, and the treatment that helps one can make another look worse. The first is subcutaneous submental fat, the pad sitting between the skin and the platysma muscle. This is the only layer any non-surgical fat-reduction treatment reaches. The second is subplatysmal fat, which sits beneath that muscle; cooling applicators and superficial injections do not reach it, and neither will change a contour driven by it. The third is skin and platysmal laxity, which becomes the dominant factor with age and after significant weight loss.

A fourth contributor is skeletal: a retruded chin, or a low and obtuse cervicomental angle, produces the same profile in someone with almost no submental fat. A downward-tilted phone camera exaggerates all four at once.

This defines who the trials actually studied. The placebo-controlled deoxycholic acid trials enrolled patients graded on a clinician-rated submental fat severity scale; the cryolipolysis studies selected for discrete, pinchable fat pads. Their results say nothing about fullness that is skeletal or laxity-driven. A consultation that does not palpate the area with the platysma relaxed and then contracted, and assess the jawline in profile, cannot say whether fat reduction is the right category at all.

The four double chin treatment options, ranked by how well they have been tested

Ranking double chin treatment by evidence quality rather than popularity produces an order most clinic pages reverse. The ranking below is by study design: controlled versus uncontrolled, blinded versus open-label, and sample size.

  • Injectable deoxycholic acid (ATX-101). Four randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled Phase 3 trials, plus a controlled three-year follow-up. The strongest evidence in this field by a wide margin - and the approved product is not marketed in India.
  • Submental cryolipolysis (fat freezing under the chin). One manufacturer-run pivotal study of 60 patients with blinded photographic assessment, and a 2025 meta-analysis pooling 8 studies and 206 patients. All 8 were before-and-after series with no control group.
  • High-intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU). One prospective, single-arm, open-label study of 40 Korean patients assessed at 8 weeks. No control arm, no placebo.
  • Phosphatidylcholine with deoxycholate, sold as 'fat-dissolving injections'. One randomised contralateral-control trial that enrolled 13 people and finished with 7 completers.

Two things are true across the list. There is no head-to-head trial comparing any of these against another. And a response rate from an open-label study cannot be set beside one from a placebo-controlled trial, where the placebo arm alone scored 18.6%. A comparison table that puts those percentages in the same column is comparing incompatible measurements.

Injectable deoxycholic acid: the only submental option with placebo-controlled trials

Injectable deoxycholic acid (ATX-101) is a synthetic bile acid that disrupts fat-cell membranes when injected into subcutaneous fat. It is the only submental treatment with placebo-controlled evidence.

REFINE-1 (Jones and colleagues, Dermatologic Surgery, 2016) randomised 256 patients to ATX-101 at 2 mg/cm2 and 250 to placebo, with endpoints 12 weeks after the last treatment and MRI for objective volume. At least a one-grade composite improvement was reached by 70.0% on the drug versus 18.6% on placebo (p < .001). The more demanding two-grade composite response - the change a patient would call obvious - was reached by 13.4% versus 0%. MRI-confirmed responders were 46.3% versus 5.3%. Read together: seven in ten improved by a measurable grade, but fewer than one in seven improved by two.

Durability is the one place this field has controlled long-term data. Humphrey and colleagues followed REFINE responders for three years with no further treatment: maintenance of the one-grade response was 86.4% at year one, 90.6% at year two and 82.4% at year three. The caveat sits in the same table - the placebo group also 'maintained' at 56.8%, 73.8% and 65.0%, which shows how much of a maintained response is regression and measurement rather than drug.

The main procedural risk is specific to this anatomy: REFINE-1 recorded marginal mandibular nerve paresis in 4.3% of patients, in about 1.0% of all sessions, mostly mild and transient. Every one of these trials was manufacturer-sponsored with industry authorship throughout - a reason to read the effect sizes carefully, not to dismiss the design.

In India this matters less than it should, because the approved product is not sold here. Shome and colleagues, in the only published Indian submental-fat injectable dataset (2019, 50 patients of confirmed Indian ethnicity), put it in writing: 'As none of the aforementioned [ATX-101, Kybella (United States), Belkyra (Canada)] are available in India, we undertook this study to study the efficacy of generic DCA for SMF reduction in Indian patients.' That open-label, uncontrolled study used generic deoxycholic acid at 3 mg/cm2 - 50% higher than the dose tested in REFINE-1 - and reported at least a one-point clinician-rated improvement in 90% of patients. With no control group, no blinding and no placebo arm, that 90% is not comparable to REFINE-1's 70%. It is the only Indian data that exists, and it records that 32 of the 50 patients needed three sessions.

Submental cryolipolysis: 206 patients, eight studies, and no control group anywhere

Submental cryolipolysis cools the fat pad under the chin through a small vacuum applicator, aiming to trigger fat-cell apoptosis and clearance over the following weeks. The pivotal study (Kilmer and colleagues, 2016) treated 60 patients with one 60-minute cycle at -10 C, with an optional second at six weeks. Three blinded physicians correctly identified the baseline photograph 91% of the time; mean fat-layer reduction on ultrasound was 2.0 mm; 83% were satisfied and 77% reported visible fat reduction - meaning roughly one in four did not. The study was manufacturer-run and had no control group.

The 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis by Do and colleagues screened 951 articles and pooled the eight that qualified, covering 206 patients. Pooled effect: 2.78 mm reduction in submental fat thickness and 19.57 cm3 reduction in volume - roughly four teaspoons. The authors' methodological finding matters more than the effect size: all eight studies were pre-post designs. There is not one controlled trial of submental cryolipolysis in the world literature. Every reported result therefore carries whatever regression, expectation and photographic technique contribute, with nothing to subtract.

Non-response is documented, not hypothetical. Rodopoulou and colleagues (2020) treated 39 patients with a single session, with blinded plastic surgeons assessing photographs at three months. Patients rated themselves: 82.05% exceptional or very improved, 12.82% improved, 5.13% no result, nobody worse. If you are considering cryolipolysis for the submental area, that one-in-twenty figure belongs in the decision, not the small print.

HIFU under the chin: one open-label study of 40 people

High-intensity focused ultrasound delivers focused thermal energy at fixed depths - typically 3.0, 4.5 and 6.0 mm - heating tissue without breaking the skin. For submental fat the entire evidence base is one study. Kwon and colleagues (2021) treated 40 Korean patients who had moderate or severe submental fat with a single triple-layer HIFU session and assessed them at eight weeks: 62.5% were at least one-point responders on the clinician-rated submental fat scale, and 67.5% were satisfied. Side effects were mild and transient.

That study is prospective, single-arm and open-label, with no control and no placebo, and eight weeks is short in a field whose standard endpoint is 12 weeks after the final session. Its value for Indian readers is the population rather than the response rate: Fitzpatrick III-IV Korean skin is the closest published proxy to Indian skin in this literature.

One comparison you will see made, and should not accept: HIFU's 62.5% looks similar to deoxycholic acid's 70%. The designs are incompatible. In the placebo-controlled injectable trial, placebo alone scored 18.6% and was subtracted; the HIFU study had nothing to subtract. A HIFU treatment for the lower face and jawline is more often chosen for tightening than fat reduction, and those are different endpoints measured with different instruments.

'Fat-dissolving injections': a different compound, and seven completers

Phosphatidylcholine combined with sodium deoxycholate - sold as 'fat-dissolving injections' or 'injection lipolysis' - is a different compound from ATX-101 with a much thinner evidence base. The controlled human evidence is one randomised, open-label, contralateral-control trial (Reeds and colleagues, 2013) that enrolled 13 people and finished with seven completers, using each participant's untreated side as the control. It found a significant reduction in anterior subcutaneous abdominal fat thickness on the injected side (p = .004).

Two details matter clinically. Biopsies showed rapid increases in crown-like structures and macrophage infiltration, and the authors concluded the compound works by inducing adipocyte necrosis - uncontrolled cell death - rather than the apoptosis attributed to controlled cooling. Necrosis is a cruder form of tissue injury. And subcutaneous use is off-label: as Hoffmann set out in Der Hautarzt in 2010, the licensed route for this combination is intravenous, for fat embolism, not contouring.

A 2022 letter in Dermatologic Therapy by Fratczak and colleagues put the question in its title: 'Is it really worth the risk?' Seven completers is not an evidence base for a treatment offered as routine.

Side effects, and the one complication that does not resolve on its own

Submental fat reduction is not without consequences, and temporary effects are common rather than rare. The largest prospective adverse-event dataset in cryolipolysis (Nishikawa and colleagues, 2026, Japanese clinics, three-month follow-up, no control group) recorded an adverse-event rate of 71.6% immediately after treatment, falling to 1.3% at three months. Submental sites resolved fastest of all areas studied, at a mean of 13.6 days across 54 treatments. Pain resolved within a month; numbness persisted around three months. Swelling, redness, tenderness and altered sensation are expected in that window rather than signs of failure, and they vary between people.

The complication that changes the arithmetic is paradoxical adipose hyperplasia (PAH): a delayed, painless, firm enlargement of the treated area, typically appearing three to six months later, in which fat volume exceeds baseline. The best estimate is Mah and colleagues (2025), a meta-analysis of 28 studies and 13,078 patients: pooled incidence 0.22%, about 1 in 455 patients, on low-certainty evidence. Two secondary findings matter as much: sex-based differences in risk were not statistically significant, qualifying the repeated claim that PAH is a male phenomenon; and only 4 of the 28 studies followed patients for 16 weeks or longer - most stopped looking before PAH appears, so 1 in 455 is likely an underestimate. The figure often quoted in clinics, 0.025% or 1 in 4,000, is the manufacturer's own number and sits at the bottom of the published range.

PAH does not settle by itself. The first published description (Jalian and colleagues, JAMA Dermatology, 2014) stated that 'at this time, there is no evidence of spontaneous resolution', and that has not been overturned; in a 2022 case series, all four affected patients underwent liposuction, abdominoplasty, or both. For injectables the risk is different in kind: a cadaveric study of 62 half-heads found the marginal mandibular nerve was a single branch in only 22.6% of specimens, with four or more branches forming a plexus in 35.5%. That variability is why REFINE-1 recorded 4.3% nerve paresis, and why this area demands an injector with specific anatomical training.

What the evidence does not support

Six claims are common in aesthetic marketing for the double chin and are not supported by the published record.

  • 'Clinically proven to remove your double chin.' Do 2025 found all eight submental cryolipolysis studies were uncontrolled pre-post designs. A pooled 2.78 mm of fat-layer thinning may improve the appearance of the contour. It is not removal.
  • 'Kybella / Belkyra for the double chin.' Shome 2019, verbatim: none of ATX-101, Kybella or Belkyra 'are available in India'. Any deoxycholic acid injected here is generic or compounded and off-label, and belongs in consent as such rather than in advertising under a brand licensed elsewhere.
  • 'HIFU works as well as injections.' No head-to-head trial exists. HIFU's 62.5% comes from an open-label study of 40 patients at 8 weeks; the injectable figures come from placebo-controlled trials in which placebo alone scored 18.6%.
  • 'The result is permanent.' There is no controlled trial of submental cryolipolysis, let alone a long-term one. The only multi-year controlled data here is the three-year injectable follow-up, in which the placebo group also maintained 65.0% of its response at year three. Treated fat cells are cleared and not rapidly replaced, but surviving fat cells enlarge if weight is gained - so a maintained contour depends on stable weight, not on the device.
  • 'Everyone gets a result.' Rodopoulou 2020: 5.13% recorded no result. Kilmer 2016: 77% reported visible fat reduction, so 23% did not. Jones 2016: only 13.4% reached a two-grade change on the drug.
  • 'It slims the face and helps with weight loss.' These are localised fat-layer procedures confined to the submental triangle. They are not weight-reduction methods and not treatments for obesity; no trial cited here demonstrates a change in body weight.

What this means for a patient in Mumbai with Fitzpatrick IV-VI skin

Almost every trial quoted above ran in Fitzpatrick I-III populations, and for Indian skin the gaps should be stated rather than glossed over. No cryolipolysis clinical trial has been conducted in India. As of the most recent review of cryolipolysis in Asian populations (Putra and colleagues, 2019), only four clinical trials worldwide had been run exclusively in Asian populations - two in Korea, one in China, one in Thailand. Those four reported no pigmentary changes, but four small trials is absence of evidence rather than evidence of absence - and the same review notes that cold-based treatment in darker skin has been associated with higher rates of pigmentary change. No PAH incidence data exist for South Asian patients at all.

The consequence is not that these treatments should be avoided, but that the uncertainty around any promise is wider than a brochure implies. Which device is used, and for which cleared indication - a clearance for the abdomen does not license claims for the chin. How many applicator cycles or injection sessions a quoted price includes, rather than an undefined 'area'. What happens if the result at 12 weeks is the 5% outcome rather than the 82% one. Prices move, so confirm any figure in writing; ours are on our pricing page.

Timing is the last thing to get right. Change here is assessed 12 weeks after the final session, not at four, and Shome 2019 recorded that 32 of 50 Indian patients needed three injectable sessions roughly two months apart - a course running somewhere between six and nine months from first appointment to final assessment, and it varies. Anyone promising a jawline for a wedding six weeks away is describing the resolution of swelling, not fat reduction.

This article is not medical advice. Submental fullness has several causes, only one of which responds to fat reduction, and telling them apart requires examination rather than a photograph. Consult a qualified dermatologist before committing to any treatment.
