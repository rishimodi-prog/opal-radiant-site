Dark Circles: Why Under-Eye Treatments Fail and What Actually Works

By Dr. Punit Sakhare, MD Dermatologist · 2026-12-01 · 8 min read

Quick Facts

Dark circles are one of the most common and most frustrating cosmetic concerns, and also one of the most mistreated — because people (and products) treat them as a single problem with a single fix, when in reality 'dark circles' is an umbrella term for several completely different causes. This is why so many under-eye treatments disappoint: a brightening cream can't fix a shadow cast by volume loss, and a filler can't lighten brown pigment. The treatment only works if it matches the actual cause of your dark circles.

This guide explains the different causes of dark circles, how to work out which type (or combination) you have, the treatments that genuinely help each, why targeting the wrong cause is why most under-eye treatments fail, and the lifestyle factors that contribute. The single most useful thing you can learn here is that the first step isn't choosing a treatment — it's correctly identifying the cause, because that's what determines whether anything will work.

Dark Circles Are Not One Thing

According to PubMed, a systematic review of treatments for periorbital hyperpigmentation (the medical term for dark circles) sorts the causes into pigmentary, vascular, structural and mixed — and crucially finds that the most effective treatment differs for each. That classification is the key to the whole topic. The main causes are:

  • Pigmentary: excess melanin in the under-eye skin, appearing as a brown discolouration. Common on Indian skin, often genetic, worsened by sun and rubbing.
  • Vascular: visible blood vessels and blood pooling under thin under-eye skin, giving a bluish or purplish look — more apparent when tired or when the skin is thin.
  • Structural (shadowing): not a colour change at all, but a shadow cast by the anatomy — a tear-trough hollow, under-eye bags, or loss of volume/fat with age that creates a depression light falls into.
  • Skin laxity: crepey, lax under-eye skin that worsens shadows and texture with age.
  • Mixed: most people, in reality, have a combination of these.

Because the under-eye darkness can come from pigment, blood vessels, anatomy, laxity or a mix, there is no universal 'dark circle treatment' — the right one depends entirely on which of these is driving yours. Identifying that is step one, and it's where most people (and most product marketing) go wrong.

How to Work Out Which Type You Have

You can get useful clues yourself, though a clinician's assessment (sometimes with a light or dermatoscope) is definitive. A few simple tests:

  • The colour: genuinely brown suggests pigmentary; bluish/purple suggests vascular; a darkness that's really a shadow (the skin colour looks normal when lit from the right angle) suggests structural.
  • The stretch test: gently stretch the under-eye skin. If the darkness lightens or disappears, it's more likely vascular or structural (you've reduced the shadow/compressed the vessels); if the brown colour stays, it's more likely pigmentary.
  • The light test: look in a mirror with light from above vs straight on. If the darkness is mainly a shadow that changes with the angle of light, it's structural (hollowing/tear trough).
  • Look up / tilt the head: structural hollowing shadows change; pigment doesn't.

Most people find they have a dominant cause plus elements of others — for instance, genuine brown pigment and a tear-trough shadow, which is why a single treatment only partly helps. Getting this assessed properly matters because the treatments are so different: there's no point spending months on a pigment cream for a problem that's 80% shadow from volume loss. A good clinic will examine the under-eye area specifically to determine the mix before recommending anything — and if a clinic offers you a one-size treatment without assessing the cause, that's a reason for caution.

Treating Pigmentary Dark Circles

If your dark circles are genuinely pigmentary (brown, stays on stretching), the approach is the same family of tools used for pigmentation elsewhere, adapted for the delicate under-eye skin. The systematic review notes that topical creams/serums, chemical peels and lasers are used for pigment-based dark circles, with lasers mildly to moderately beneficial. In practice that means: dermatologist-prescribed topical agents (brightening actives suited to the thin under-eye skin), gentle chemical peels designed for the area, and laser/pigment treatments where appropriate — all building over a course, and all critically dependent on strict sun protection, since UV darkens under-eye pigment just as it does elsewhere (and the under-eye is easy to forget with sunscreen).

Two caveats specific to Indian skin: the under-eye is prone to pigmentation, and over-aggressive treatment (strong peels or lasers) can worsen pigmentation here, so the approach must be cautious and gentle — this is delicate skin, and the margin for error is smaller than on the rest of the face. Realistic expectation: pigmentary dark circles can be lightened and improved meaningfully over a course, but genetic/constitutional pigment often can't be completely erased, and it needs maintenance and sun protection to hold. For the pigment component specifically, this is the right toolkit — but it does nothing for shadow-based circles, which is the next, different problem.

Treating Structural and Vascular Dark Circles

When the darkness is structural — a shadow from a tear-trough hollow or volume loss rather than a colour — the effective treatment is to restore volume and smooth the contour, not to lighten anything. The systematic review found that soft-tissue fillers and autologous fat grafting are most effective for dark circles due to volume loss, because they fill the hollow so light no longer casts a shadow — and in the right hands a tear-trough filler can transform a shadow-based dark circle that no cream would ever have touched. (Tear-trough filler is a delicate, technique-sensitive treatment best done by an experienced injector, as the area is unforgiving.) Where skin laxity or excess skin/bags is the issue, the review notes blepharoplasty (eyelid surgery) is best, with energy-based skin tightening a non-surgical option for milder laxity.

For vascular dark circles (bluish/purple from visible vessels and thin skin), options are more limited and modest: measures that improve the skin and reduce the visibility of vessels, some laser treatments targeting the vascular component (mild-to-moderate benefit, per the review), and addressing contributing factors (sleep, allergies, thin skin). The review is honest that the overall evidence quality for dark-circle treatments is limited, so recommendations should be interpreted selectively — which is all the more reason to match treatment to cause rather than chase the latest product. The headline is simple: shadows need volume (fillers/fat) or surgery, not creams; vessels need vascular-targeted approaches, not brightening; and getting this right is the difference between a treatment that works and one that doesn't.

Why So Many Treatments Fail

Now the core lesson falls into place: most under-eye treatments fail because they target the wrong cause. A brightening eye cream aimed at a dark circle that's actually a volume-loss shadow will do essentially nothing, because there's no excess pigment to lighten — the darkness is a shadow, and no cream removes a shadow. A filler injected for a dark circle that's actually brown pigment won't lighten the colour. A laser aimed at pigment won't fill a hollow. People cycle through expensive creams, home remedies, and the occasional mismatched procedure, conclude 'nothing works for my dark circles', and give up — when the real problem was simply that each treatment was addressing a cause they didn't have.

This is why the assessment-first approach matters so much more for dark circles than for almost any other concern. Once you (and ideally a clinician) correctly identify the cause — or, more usually, the mix of causes — the treatment choice becomes rational: pigment → topicals/peels/laser; shadow from volume → filler/fat; laxity/bags → tightening or surgery; vascular → vascular-targeted and supportive measures; and for a mix, a combination addressing each component. Combination is in fact the norm, because most people have more than one cause, and treating only the dominant one leaves a partial result. The failure isn't that dark circles are untreatable — it's that they're so often treated blind.

Lifestyle, Realistic Expectations and the Bottom Line

Lifestyle factors genuinely contribute and are worth addressing alongside treatment, because they can darken circles of any type. Poor sleep makes the under-eye look worse (puffiness and paler skin making vessels more visible); rubbing the eyes (often from allergies or eczema) drives pigmentation, so managing allergies and not rubbing helps; sun exposure darkens pigment; dehydration and smoking don't help; and nutritional factors — notably iron deficiency, common in Indian women — can contribute to a tired, dark under-eye look, which is why checking for and correcting deficiencies is sometimes part of the picture (our guide on what deficiency causes dark circles covers this). These won't fix a structural or strongly genetic dark circle on their own, but they remove aggravating factors and are the sensible, free first layer.

On expectations: dark circles can be significantly improved when the treatment matches the cause, but 'completely gone forever' is often unrealistic, especially for genetic pigment or constitutional thin skin — the honest goal is a marked, maintained improvement, usually via a combination, with ongoing sun protection and good habits. The bottom line, and the single most valuable takeaway: don't choose a dark-circle treatment until you know which type you have. That one step — proper identification of the cause, or mix of causes — is what separates the people who finally improve their dark circles from the people who conclude nothing works. It's also worth saying, gently, that some under-eye darkness is simply part of your natural anatomy and genetics — thin skin, a deep-set eye, a familial tendency to pigment — and in those cases the honest goal is softening and improvement rather than erasure, often helped as much by good concealer technique, sleep and sun protection as by any procedure. There's no need to pathologise a feature that many people have and that treatment can only partly change. The useful dividing line is between darkness that genuinely bothers you and has a treatable cause worth addressing, and darkness that's a normal, minor part of your face best left alone or lightly managed. A candid assessment tells you which is which — so you neither chase an impossible result nor miss a very treatable one — and either way, you stop wasting money on treatments aimed at a cause you don't actually have.
