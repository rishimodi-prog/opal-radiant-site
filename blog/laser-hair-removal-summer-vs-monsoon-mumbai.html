Laser Hair Removal

The Best Time for Laser Hair Removal in Mumbai: Summer, Monsoon or Neither?

By Dr. Punit Sakhare, MD Dermatologist · 2026-10-20 · 11 min read

The best time for laser hair removal, in the way most people mean the question, does not exist. A course is not one appointment you slot into a cool month. It is typically four to eight sessions spaced four to eight weeks apart, which puts the whole course somewhere between four and fourteen months long. In Mumbai that span will contain a hot summer, a monsoon and a short dry winter whichever month you begin. The calendar decides very little. The hair growth cycle, your skin type, the settings used and how consistently you wear sunscreen decide almost everything.

There is exactly one published dataset that measures a seasonal difference in complications from a resurfacing-type procedure in darker skin, and it studied 473 superficial chemical peels in Boston, not lasers in Mumbai. Everything else circulating about the correct season is inference from mechanism, and some of it is wrong. This article separates the three things: what the evidence shows about timing, what it does not show, and what a Mumbai summer or monsoon genuinely changes about how a course should be run.

The best time for laser hair removal is set by the hair growth cycle, not the weather

Laser hair reduction works only on follicles that are in the active growth phase, because that is the only phase in which the melanin-rich hair shaft is physically coupled to the structures the laser needs to damage. Follicles in a given patch of skin cycle independently of each other, so any single session catches only the fraction that happen to be growing that week. That, and nothing about the season, is why courses are spread over months.

Cycle length varies by body site, and this is the most practically useful finding in the whole field. A 2022 systematic review by Krasniqi and colleagues restricted itself to randomised trials that followed patients for at least one full hair growth cycle at the treated site. Of the entire world literature, only five trials qualified, totalling 223 patients. Long-term reduction was greatest on the legs, which run roughly a one-year cycle, and lowest on the face, which runs roughly six months, with the axilla intermediate at about seven months. Reported ranges were 30-73.6% for Nd:YAG, 35-84.3% for alexandrite, 32.5-69.2% for diode and 27-52.7% for IPL.

Read that sample size again. Five randomised trials and 223 patients is the complete long-term evidence base for a treatment sold on every high street in Mumbai. It is enough to establish that laser produces durable partial reduction. It is not enough to support fine-grained scheduling advice, and no trial has ever compared starting a course in one season against starting it in another.

What a Mumbai summer actually changes: the melanin in your skin, not the laser

A Mumbai summer changes one clinically relevant variable: how much melanin is sitting in your epidermis on the day of treatment. The laser is aimed at melanin in the hair, but epidermal melanin absorbs the same light and competes for it. In Fitzpatrick IV-VI skin there is already a great deal of that competing pigment, and recent sun exposure adds more. The consequence is a narrower margin between a dose that damages the follicle and a dose that injures the surface.

How narrow is real rather than theoretical. In the most methodologically careful Indian study located, Pai and colleagues (2021) treated 40 Indian women across 108 sites with an 810 nm diode laser at 16-29 J/cm², measuring outcomes with a blinded investigator plus an objective hair-counting algorithm. Epidermolysis occurred in 5 of 40 patients, which is 12.5% of that cohort. The same study found median improvement of 60% on side locks and 53.9% on the axilla, but only 24.1% on the upper lip and 14.9% on the chin.

The honest position on tanned skin is more nuanced than clinic websites suggest. The FDA 510(k) clearance K180353 for an 808 nm diode device states the indication as hair removal and permanent hair reduction “on all skin types (Fitzpatrick skin type I-VI), including tanned skin.” Clearance, however, means substantial equivalence to an earlier device, not independent verification of efficacy or of that claim. No randomised trial has compared treating tanned versus untanned skin. Caution around a fresh tan is sound physics and standard practice; it is not a trial result, and it is a reason to adjust settings rather than to refuse the whole season.

The only seasonal number in the literature studied chemical peels, not lasers

One study is repeatedly invoked, usually without attribution, to argue that winter is the correct season for energy and resurfacing procedures in darker skin. It deserves to be quoted properly, because it is genuinely useful and it is genuinely limited.

Vemula and colleagues (2018) retrospectively reviewed 473 superficial chemical peel treatments in Fitzpatrick III-VI skin at a single Boston centre. Complications occurred in 18 treatments, an overall rate of 3.8%. The commonest were crusting at 2.3%, with post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation and erythema tied at 1.9%. All side effects were on the face and all resolved within 8 months. In the adjusted model, Fitzpatrick VI carried an odds ratio of 5.14 for any adverse event (95% CI 1.21-21.8, P = .0118). And, stratified by season, side effects were less common in winter.

Three caveats decide how much weight that last sentence can carry. It is a retrospective single-centre study, so it establishes association rather than cause. The seasonal comparison is a descriptive stratification, not the adjusted model that produced the skin-type odds ratio. And it studied superficial chemical peels, which injure the epidermis deliberately and across the whole treated field; a hair laser is targeted at pigmented follicles and spares the surface when correctly dosed. Boston winters also bear no resemblance to Mumbai's. The finding is a reasonable prompt to be more conservative when patients are getting more sun. It is not evidence that laser hair reduction is unsafe in a Mumbai May.

Sunscreen is the seasonal habit with the strongest supporting evidence

If any single behaviour deserves to be tied to the seasons in a laser hair reduction plan, it is daily sun protection, and the outcome it appears to protect against is not pigmentation. It is paradoxical hypertrichosis, the poorly understood phenomenon in which treated skin grows more or coarser hair rather than less.

Two independent studies on different continents found the same protective signal. Inoue and colleagues (2024) reviewed 7,381 patients retrospectively at a single centre; 25 developed paradoxical hypertrichosis, a rate of 0.34%, and 24 of those 25 had been treated with an alexandrite laser (P < .01) despite alexandrite being only about 70% of the caseload. Daily sun protection independently reduced incidence with an odds ratio of 0.41 (P < .05), and that effect held independent of Fitzpatrick type. Qeyam and colleagues (2025), studying facial treatment prospectively, found a far higher prevalence of 16.2%, with regular sunblock users at 12.1% against 36.1% in non-users (P = 0.001). In the same study, PCOS carried 33.3% against 14.1% (P < 0.001) and 6-10 sessions carried 30.9% against 6.8% (P = 0.001).

For scale, the largest pooled estimate comes from Snast and colleagues (2021), a meta-analysis of 9,733 patients across 2 RCTs and 20 cohort studies, which put prevalence at 3% (95% CI 1-6) but with extreme heterogeneity, I² = 97%. It was strongly concentrated on the face and neck, occurring in only 0.08% of non-facial sites. Published rates therefore span 0.34% to 16.2%, and the prospective studies find the higher figures, which is the expected direction. The mechanism remains unknown. What replicates is the sunscreen association, and in Mumbai the season that quietly ends the sunscreen habit is the monsoon, not the summer.

Monsoon, sweat and humidity: the plausible risks with no published numbers

The monsoon objection to laser hair reduction is usually stated as sweat, humidity and occlusion raising the risk of folliculitis in freshly treated skin. Folliculitis is a documented complication. Mallat and colleagues (2023) screened 358 references and included 104 publications in the most complete adverse-event catalogue available, listing pain, burns, folliculitis, leukotrichia, paradoxical hypertrichosis, pigmentary change, changes in naevi, herpes reactivation and even frostbite from the contact cooling system, alongside ocular injuries including anterior uveitis, iris atrophy and cataract.

That review has one decisive limitation the clinics quoting it tend to skip: it reports no denominators. It can tell you what has happened; it cannot tell you how often. The authors' own conclusion is that cutaneous complications are usually related to professional errors and patient characteristics, and their pain ranking places Nd:YAG highest, then alexandrite, then IPL, with diode least painful.

No study in either research brief measured humidity, ambient temperature, sweating or swimming as a predictor of any laser hair reduction outcome. Not one. The monsoon folliculitis argument is mechanistically plausible and is standard aftercare reasoning, and it is entirely unquantified. The practical version is modest: for a day or two after a session treated skin is inflamed and more reactive, so heavy sweating, occlusive gym clothing, pool water and hot waxing over the area are worth postponing briefly. How briefly varies between people and between body sites, and any clinic quoting you a precise guideline-backed number for it is inventing precision.

The genuine summer conflict is waxing season, not sunshine

The scheduling problem that actually derails Indian laser courses is not the weather. It is the wedding, festival and holiday calendar, because the grooming habit those events trigger is the one habit that makes a laser session useless.

The laser's target is melanin in the hair shaft while it sits inside the follicle. Waxing, threading, plucking and epilating all pull the shaft out, which deletes the target; the follicle becomes effectively invisible to the laser and survives the session you paid for. Shaving cuts the hair at the surface and leaves the intra-follicular shaft intact, which is why shaving is the required preparation rather than a matter of taste. Shaving also removes surface hair that would otherwise absorb energy above the skin and singe rather than heat the follicle. The conventional instruction is to shave the area in the day before a session and to avoid waxing, threading or plucking for around four to six weeks beforehand.

State the evidence grade honestly: no randomised trial has ever compared shaving against not shaving, or waxing against shaving, before a laser session. The rule is universal clinical practice with a sound mechanism behind it, endorsed in passing by clinical series but never tested. If your summer involves regular threading appointments for a run of functions, that is a real reason to shift a course, and it is a stronger reason than the temperature outside.

Planning around a wedding or a holiday: start earlier than you think

Work the arithmetic backwards rather than booking a course two months before an event. Four to eight sessions at four to eight week intervals is roughly four to fourteen months of treatment, and the interval is often lengthened deliberately as a course progresses to match the slowing return of hair. Facial areas sit at the difficult end of this: a six-month cycle means more sessions for less durable benefit, which is exactly what the Indian data showed with 14.9% median improvement on the chin.

The second piece of arithmetic is the one the industry under-communicates. Reduction is not static after a course ends. Ibrahimi and Kilmer (2012), a prospective self-controlled study in 35 patients given three treatments, measured 54% reduction at 6 months falling to 42% at 15 months. Independent groups have replicated that direction of decline. The Cochrane review by Haedersdal and Gøtzsche (2006), covering 11 randomised trials and 444 patients, found roughly 50% hair reduction with alexandrite and diode lasers up to six months, little evidence for IPL, Nd:YAG or ruby lasers, and stated plainly that long-term hair removal was not documented with any treatment. It also judged that none of the 11 trials were of high methodological quality.

So the sensible planning target is not a hair-free date. It is being deep enough into a course before an event that the visible result is stable, with maintenance sessions expected afterwards rather than treated as a failure. If you want to look a particular way in May, the conversation belongs in the previous monsoon, not in March. Our laser hair removal service page sets out how we sequence a course.

What the evidence does not support

Several confident seasonal claims have nothing behind them, and a blog that quotes numbers has an obligation to name them.

  • That winter is the only safe season for laser. The single seasonal finding available is from 473 superficial chemical peels in Boston, where the seasonal comparison was descriptive rather than part of the adjusted model. No laser hair reduction trial has compared seasons at all.
  • That the monsoon is unsafe for laser. No published study has measured humidity, sweating or rainfall against any laser outcome. The folliculitis concern is mechanism and aftercare reasoning, unquantified in the literature.
  • That laser gives permanent hair removal. The regulatory term is permanent hair reduction, defined in FDA clearance K180353 as the long-term, stable reduction in the number of hairs regrowing when measured at 6, 9 and 12 months after a treatment regime. That is a reduction in hair count measured to twelve months, not elimination and not forever.
  • That a course delivers 90-95%. Trials with follow-up of at least one hair cycle report 27-84% depending on device and body site, and objective algorithmic measurement in Indian skin produced 14.9% on the chin.
  • That there is a fixed number of days you must avoid sun, gym or swimming. No guideline sets one from trial data. Ranges given by clinics are consensus, and they vary by site, skin type and how the skin actually reacted.
  • That laser brightens skin as a seasonal bonus. There is no controlled evidence for it. Where measurable pigment change follows treatment it is usually darkening, which is post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation and a complication to avoid rather than a benefit. If pigmentation is your main concern, that is a separate conversation about pigmentation and melasma.

How season should actually change a Mumbai course

Season should change three things in a Mumbai laser hair reduction plan, and none of them is whether you start. First, sun protection becomes a treatment variable rather than a cosmetic one, because it is the only seasonal behaviour with replicated supporting data behind it, with an odds ratio of 0.41 for paradoxical hypertrichosis in 7,381 patients and 12.1% against 36.1% in a prospective facial cohort. That matters most in the monsoon, when the habit lapses because the sun is not visible.

Second, recent sun exposure is a reason to review settings, not to cancel. Conservative fluence with effective contact cooling is how the margin is kept in Fitzpatrick IV-VI skin, and epidermolysis in 12.5% of a 40-patient Indian cohort is the reminder that the margin is real. Third, if a course is running through wedding season, the waxing and threading question is the one to settle in advance, because a session on a recently waxed area cannot work.

Two things worth raising at consultation are unrelated to weather and matter far more. Paradoxical hypertrichosis was reported in 33.3% of PCOS patients against 14.1% without it in one prospective study, so hormonal history changes the counselling. And facial areas respond less durably than legs and axillae in every dataset that measured them objectively.
